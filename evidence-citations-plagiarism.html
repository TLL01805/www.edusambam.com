Evidence, Citations & Avoiding Plagiarism

A strong claim means little without support — and using someone else's support without credit can undo an otherwise excellent essay. Here is how to do both properly.

A thesis is only as strong as the evidence behind it. This guide covers how to select and use that evidence properly — and how to make sure every source is credited honestly.

This guide, the fourth in our Academic & Essay Writing series, covers what counts as strong evidence, the difference between quoting, paraphrasing, and summarizing, how citations work and why they matter, and exactly what plagiarism is — including the accidental kind that catches many honest students off guard.

1.What Counts as Evidence?

Strong essays support their claims (Topic II) with credible, specific evidence rather than vague generalizations.

  • Facts and statistics: specific, verifiable data points from a credible source.
  • Expert opinion: statements or findings from recognized authorities in a relevant field.
  • Direct quotations: exact wording taken from a primary or secondary source.
  • Examples and case studies: specific, real instances that illustrate a broader point.
  • Personal observation: appropriate in narrative or descriptive essays, though generally not sufficient alone in argumentative or expository writing.

2.Quoting, Paraphrasing, and Summarizing

These three methods of using a source are often confused, but each serves a different purpose.

Quoting

Using a source's exact words, enclosed in quotation marks. Best reserved for cases where the original wording is especially precise, powerful, or authoritative — quoting too frequently makes an essay feel like a collection of other people's sentences rather than the writer's own argument.

Paraphrasing

Restating a specific passage in your own words and sentence structure, while keeping roughly the same level of detail. Still requires a citation, since the idea originated elsewhere even though the wording has changed.

Summarizing

Condensing a longer passage or source into a much shorter overview of its main point, in your own words. Also requires a citation.

3.How to Introduce a Quotation Properly

A quotation should never be dropped into a paragraph with no introduction — this is sometimes called a "hanging quote," and it leaves the reader unsure why the quote is there or whose voice it represents.

Weak (Hanging Quote)

"Remote work increased average employee output by 13% in controlled studies." Companies should reconsider office mandates.

Strong (Properly Introduced)

According to a 2023 Stanford workplace study, "remote work increased average employee output by 13% in controlled studies." This finding suggests companies enforcing strict office mandates may be undermining their own productivity goals.

4.Why Citations Matter

A citation credits the original source of an idea, fact, or quotation. Citations serve several purposes at once: they give proper credit to the original author, allow readers to verify claims independently, and demonstrate that an argument is grounded in credible research rather than personal opinion alone.

5.In-Text Citations vs. a Reference List

Most citation systems use two connected parts working together.

In-Text Citation

A brief note within the essay itself, immediately after the borrowed material, pointing to the full source. Formats vary by style guide — for example, (Smith, 2023) in APA style, or a footnote number in Chicago style.

Reference List / Bibliography

A full list of every source cited, placed at the end of the essay, giving complete publication details for each one so a reader could locate the original source themselves.

Different academic fields and institutions favor different citation styles — APA, MLA, and Chicago are among the most common — but the underlying purpose is always the same: honest, verifiable attribution.

6.What Is Plagiarism?

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's words, ideas, or work as your own, without proper credit — whether or not it was done intentionally.

7.Types of Plagiarism

Direct Plagiarism

Copying someone else's exact words without quotation marks or citation — the most obvious and serious form.

Mosaic (Patchwork) Plagiarism

Piecing together phrases from a source with minor word changes scattered throughout, without proper citation — often mistaken for legitimate paraphrasing, but still considered plagiarism because the underlying sentence structure and wording remain too close to the original.

Self-Plagiarism

Reusing significant portions of one's own previously submitted work for a new assignment without permission or disclosure — often overlooked, but still considered a form of academic dishonesty in most institutions.

Accidental Plagiarism

Failing to cite a source properly due to carelessness or misunderstanding of citation rules, rather than intentional dishonesty — still treated as plagiarism by most academic institutions, regardless of intent.

8.How to Avoid Plagiarism

  • Keep careful notes while researching, clearly separating your own thoughts from source material as you take them.
  • When in doubt, cite it. Over-citing a common, general fact is a far smaller problem than failing to cite a genuinely borrowed idea.
  • Paraphrase properly by fully understanding the source material, then writing the idea from memory in your own words and sentence structure — rather than lightly editing the original sentence.
  • Use citation management tools to track sources accurately throughout the research and drafting process.
  • Always credit your own past work if reusing any of it, and check institutional policy on self-plagiarism beforehand.
Real-World Example

Consider a student researching climate policy who finds this original sentence in a source: "Carbon pricing mechanisms have proven more cost-effective than direct regulation in reducing industrial emissions across most studied economies." A plagiarized version might read: "Carbon pricing systems have shown to be more cost-effective than direct regulation for reducing industrial emissions in most economies studied" — nearly identical wording and structure, with no citation, making it mosaic plagiarism despite minor word swaps. A properly paraphrased and cited version would read: "Research suggests that market-based approaches like carbon pricing tend to reduce industrial emissions more efficiently than direct government regulation (Chen, 2022)." Same idea, fully credited, and expressed in genuinely different language and structure.

Why This Matters

Evidence is what transforms an opinion into an argument, and honest citation is what makes that argument trustworthy. Understanding how to select strong evidence, use it properly through quoting, paraphrasing, or summarizing, and credit it accurately protects both the integrity of the writer's work and the ideas of everyone whose research made that argument possible in the first place.
