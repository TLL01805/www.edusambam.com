Evidence, Citations & Avoiding Plagiarism

A strong claim means little without support — and using someone else's support without credit can undo an otherwise excellent essay. Here is how to do both properly.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 19 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Academic Writing

A claim is only an opinion until you show what it rests on. Evidence turns an opinion into an argument, and honest citation tells the reader exactly where that evidence came from.

This guide explains what counts as strong evidence, how to judge a source, the three ways of using a source (quoting, paraphrasing and summarising), how to introduce and explain evidence, how citations work in the main styles, what plagiarism really is (including the accidental kind), and how to use AI tools honestly. It has two worked examples, a checklist, common mistakes, myths and ten practice questions.

It builds on Types of Essays and Thesis Statements, and it connects with Essay Structure, Transitions & Cohesion and Editing Your Own Work. Spelling follows British English.

1.What Counts as Evidence?

Strong essays support each claim with credible, specific evidence instead of vague generalisations. "Many people think so" is not evidence. "A named study of named people found this result" is.

TypeExampleWatch out for
Facts and statistics"The experiment lasted nine months."Say who measured it, and when.
Expert opinionA doctor on health, a historian on historyIs the expert qualified in this topic?
QuotationThe exact words of a writer or speakerDo not use quotations as filler.
Example or case studyOne school, one town, one companyOne case may not prove a general rule.
Personal observation"In my class, most students…"Fine in a story; weak alone in an argument.

Notice the third column. Every kind of evidence has a limit, and a good writer says so. That honesty makes an essay more convincing, not less.

2.Judging a Source: Four Quick Questions

Not every page on the internet deserves a place in your essay. Before you use a source, ask four questions:

  • Who wrote it? Is there a named author, university, newspaper or organisation? Do they know the subject?
  • What is it based on? Does it show its own evidence, such as data, documents or a study? Or does it only repeat opinions?
  • When was it written? Some topics, such as technology or medicine, change fast. An old source may be out of date.
  • Why was it written? To inform, to sell something, or to persuade you to vote or buy? A seller's website is not a neutral judge of its own product.

It also helps to know the difference between two kinds of source. A primary source is the original: a diary, a speech, a set of results, a law. A secondary source explains or discusses the original: a textbook, a news report, a review. Use primary sources when you can, and always say which one you are using. If you read about a study in a news article, it is best to find the study itself and check that the article described it correctly.

Real-world connection

Journalists and scientists use the same habit. A news report that names the study, the people studied and the journal lets you check it. A report that says only "scientists say" gives you nothing to check.

3.Quoting, Paraphrasing and Summarising

There are three ways to use a source. They are often confused, but each has a different job. All three need a citation, because the idea came from someone else even when the words are yours.

Three ways to use a source
The bars show how much of the original is kept.

Diagram 1. Only a quotation keeps the author's exact words. Paraphrase and summary change the words, but the idea still belongs to the source.

Quoting

Use the exact words, inside quotation marks, when the wording itself matters: a precise definition, a powerful sentence, or an expert's own claim. Quote sparingly. An essay made mostly of quotations sounds like a collection of other people's sentences, not your argument. Keep quotations short and cut them to the part you need.

Paraphrasing

Restate one passage in your own words and your own sentence structure, with about the same level of detail. Changing a few words is not enough (see section 9). A good paraphrase shows you understood the passage.

Summarising

Condense a longer passage, or a whole source, into its main point in your own words. A summary is much shorter than the original and leaves out the detail.

Which should you choose? Quote when the exact wording is the point. Paraphrase when you need the detail but not the wording. Summarise when you only need the big idea. Most of an essay's evidence should be paraphrase and summary, with a few well-chosen quotations.

4.Introducing and Explaining Evidence

Evidence should never be dropped into a paragraph without a word of introduction. A quotation with no introduction is called a hanging quote. The reader cannot tell why it is there or who is speaking. Strong paragraphs follow a simple pattern:

  1. Claim: say your point in your own words.
  2. Evidence: introduce the source and give the fact, quotation or example, with a citation.
  3. Explanation: tell the reader what the evidence shows and why it supports your claim.

Here is the pattern with a real, checked study. It was run by the economist Nicholas Bloom and three co-authors at Ctrip, a Chinese travel company with about 16,000 employees. Call-centre staff who volunteered to work from home were randomly assigned to work from home or in the office for nine months. The authors report that home working led to a 13% performance increase. About 9 points of that came from working more minutes per shift (fewer breaks and sick days), and about 4 points from more calls per minute.

Weak: no introduction, no explanation

Working from home increases output by 13%. Companies should allow it.

Problems: no source, no setting, and the number sounds like a rule for every job.

Strong: claim, evidence, explanation

Working from home can raise productivity in some jobs. In a nine-month experiment at the Chinese travel company Ctrip, call-centre staff who worked from home performed 13% better than office colleagues (Bloom et al., 2015). The study involved volunteers doing one kind of work, so it does not prove the same gain for every job, but it shows that remote work need not reduce output.

The strong version names the study, says who and what was measured, gives a cautious conclusion and includes a citation. The same study also found that the home workers' promotion rate fell relative to their performance. Mentioning a result like that, when it is relevant, shows the reader you have not chosen only the facts that suit you.

Useful verbs for introducing evidence: reports, argues, found, suggests, claims, notes, shows. Choose the verb that matches how strong the evidence is. A single study "suggests"; a large body of research may "show".

5.Why Citations Matter

A citation credits the original source of an idea, fact or quotation. It does three jobs at once:

  • It gives credit to the person who did the work.
  • It lets the reader check your evidence for themselves.
  • It shows your argument rests on research, not on opinion alone.

Citations also protect you. A writer who cites carefully can show exactly where each idea came from. A writer who does not may be suspected of copying even when they did not mean to.

6.In-Text Citations and the Reference List

Most citation systems have two connected parts. The in-text citation is a short note inside your essay, placed right after the borrowed material. The reference list (or bibliography) is a full list of every source at the end, so a reader could find each one. Each in-text citation must lead to an entry in the list.

Here is how the same article would appear in an APA reference list:

APA reference list entry

Bloom, N., Liang, J., Roberts, J., & Ying, Z. J. (2015). Does working from home work? Evidence from a Chinese experiment. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 130(1), 165–218. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju032

Points to remember about styles:

  • APA puts the date next to the author. MLA uses author and page number. Chicago has two systems: footnotes with a bibliography, or author and date like APA. Harvard, widely used in the UK, is also author and date, but its details change from one university to another.
  • Page numbers are normally required for quotations and are good practice for close paraphrases. Web pages without page numbers are cited by author and date, or by title.
  • Use one style all the way through a piece of work, and follow the style your teacher or exam board asks for. A style guide or a library website will show the exact format for books, web pages and videos.
  • Citation tools can format entries for you, but they make mistakes. Check every entry yourself.

7.What Is Plagiarism?

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's words, ideas or work as your own, without proper credit. It does not depend on intention. Copying a paragraph on purpose and forgetting a citation by mistake can both be treated as plagiarism, although the penalty may differ.

Plagiarism is about credit, not only about copying words. If you take a clever argument from a website and rewrite it in different words without citing it, you have still used someone else's idea.

What you do not need to cite:

  • Your own ideas and conclusions, reached by your own thinking.
  • Common knowledge: facts that are widely known and found in many sources, such as "Paris is the capital of France".
  • Your own experiment results or survey answers, which you collected yourself (say how you collected them).

What counts as common knowledge depends on the readers and the subject. A fact that every student in your class knows may be new to someone else. When in doubt, cite. Citing a fact that did not need it is a small fault. Failing to cite a borrowed idea is a serious one.

8.Types of Plagiarism

TypeWhat it looks like
DirectCopying exact words with no quotation marks and no citation.
Mosaic (patchwork)Joining phrases from a source, with a few words swapped, and no citation.
Self-plagiarismHanding in your own earlier work again without permission or a note.
AccidentalMissing a citation through carelessness or not knowing the rules.
MisattributionGiving the wrong source, or citing a source you never opened.
Buying or borrowing workSubmitting an essay written by another person, including a paid service.

Mosaic plagiarism is the one that surprises honest students. It happens when you keep the original sentence structure and swap in synonyms. The writer thinks "I changed the words", but the sentence is still the source's sentence. You will see an example in section 10.

Self-plagiarism sounds odd, because the work is yours. But a teacher expects new work for a new task. If you want to build on an earlier piece, ask first and say so.

9.How to Paraphrase Honestly

A true paraphrase is not a copy with new words. Use this method:

  1. Read the passage until you understand it fully.
  2. Cover the source, so you cannot see the words.
  3. Write the idea from memory, in your own words and your own sentence shape.
  4. Compare with the original. Check the meaning is correct. If a phrase is identical and distinctive, change it or put it in quotation marks.
  5. Cite the source straight away, so you do not forget.

Keep careful notes while you research. Mark every copied phrase with quotation marks and the page number in your notes, and write your own thoughts in a different colour or a different section. Most accidental plagiarism begins when notes from a source and notes of your own look the same three days later.

10.Worked Examples

Example A: Spotting and fixing mosaic plagiarism

The original sentence below is invented for teaching. It is not from a real source.

Original (Surname, year, p. 12): "Tidal power stations produce electricity predictably, because the moon's pull makes the tides arrive on a timetable that can be worked out years ahead."

1Mosaic version: "Tidal power plants generate electricity in a predictable way, since the moon's pull causes tides to come on a schedule calculated years in advance." The structure is the same, and only a few words were swapped. This is plagiarism, with or without a citation. With a citation it should be a quotation.
2Understand the idea: tides follow the moon, so their times can be forecast, and that makes the power supply easy to plan.
3Write from memory, in a new shape: "One advantage of tidal energy is that engineers can forecast the output of a station well in advance, because the tides follow the moon (Surname, year)."
4Check: the meaning matches, the sentence is built differently, and the source is credited.

Try it yourself. Paraphrase this invented sentence and add a citation: "Regular sleep helps students concentrate in class, because the brain sorts and stores new information during the night."

Sample answer: "Students who sleep regularly find it easier to focus, since the brain organises what it has learned while they rest (Surname, year)." The idea is the same, the sentence is built differently, and the source is credited.

Example B: Turning a hanging quote into strong evidence

Draft: "Home working led to a 13% performance increase." Schools should copy this.

1Name the source and setting: the Ctrip experiment, with call-centre staff, by Bloom and co-authors, 2015.
2Say what was measured: performance of staff working from home compared with office colleagues, over nine months.
3Cite in a chosen style (APA here) and add the entry to the reference list.
4Explain, with a limit: say what it shows and what it does not. A study of call-centre staff cannot, by itself, tell us about school pupils.

Improved: A nine-month experiment at the travel company Ctrip found that call-centre staff working from home performed 13% better than office colleagues (Bloom et al., 2015). This shows that home working can succeed in some workplaces. It does not show the same would happen in schools, where pupils need supervision and teachers.

Try it yourself. Write a two-sentence evidence paragraph about a topic you know, using a source you can name. Include a claim, one piece of evidence with a citation and one limit.

11.Using AI Tools Honestly

AI chatbots can write fluent text in seconds, which makes them tempting and risky for schoolwork. The rules depend on your school, your teacher and your exam board, so always check what is allowed for each task. Some guidance is already clear.

  • Your own work. In the United Kingdom, the Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ), which speaks for the main exam boards, states that all work submitted for qualification assessments must be the student's own. Using AI so that the submitted work is not your own can be treated as malpractice.
  • Acknowledge it. The JCQ guidance says that where students use AI, they must acknowledge it and show clearly how they used it, so the teacher can judge whether the use was appropriate.
  • AI can be wrong. The JCQ guidance warns that chatbots may produce incorrect answers, biased information or fake references. Never copy a reference you have not opened and checked. If an AI tool lists sources, find each one and verify it before you cite it.
  • Do not cite what you have not read. A reference to a book or paper that does not exist is a serious problem, even if a chatbot invented it for you.

A sensible approach: use AI, if your teacher allows it, to understand a topic or to suggest questions, then find real sources and write the essay yourself. Keep a note of what you asked and what you used. If you are not sure whether a use is allowed, ask before you submit, not after.

Other exam systems have their own rules. Check the regulations for your own board or school and follow them.

13.Common Mistakes

MistakeBetter habit
Dropping in a quotation with no introductionIntroduce it and explain it.
Swapping a few words and calling it a paraphraseCover the source and rewrite from memory.
Citing in the text but leaving the entry out of the listCheck both ends of every citation.
Using a statistic with no source or dateName who measured it and when.
Treating one study as proof for everythingSay what the study covers and what it does not.
Citing a source you have not openedRead it, or do not cite it.

14.Common Myths

MythIf I change the words, I do not need a citation.
FactIdeas need credit as well as words. A paraphrase still needs a citation.
MythPlagiarism only counts if it was on purpose.
FactAccidental plagiarism is still treated as plagiarism by most schools. Intention may affect the penalty, not the label.
MythAnything on the internet is free to use as my own.
FactOnline writing, images and data have authors. You must credit them, and sometimes you need permission.
MythIf an AI tool wrote it, nobody owns it, so I can use it freely.
FactWhere your own work is required, submitting AI text as yours breaks the rules. Where AI use is allowed, it must be acknowledged.

15.Key Terms

Evidence: facts, examples or expert views that support a claim.
Primary source: an original document, speech or set of results.
Secondary source: a work that explains or discusses primary sources.
Quotation: a source's exact words, in quotation marks.
Paraphrase: a passage restated in your own words and structure.
Summary: a short version of the main point, in your own words.
Citation: a credit to the source of an idea, fact or quotation.
Reference list / bibliography: the full list of sources at the end.
Plagiarism: presenting another person's words or ideas as your own.
Common knowledge: facts widely known and found in many sources.

16.Why This Matters

Evidence is what makes an argument worth listening to, and honest citation is what makes it trustworthy. The same habits help you outside school: checking where a claim comes from before you share it, saying where your ideas came from, and being clear about what a study does and does not show. Continue with Transitions & Cohesion to link your evidence smoothly, and Essay Structure to place it in the right paragraph.

Key points to remember
  • Use specific, checkable evidence, and say what it cannot show.
  • Ask who, what, when and why about every source.
  • Quote the exact words only when the wording matters; otherwise paraphrase or summarise.
  • Introduce evidence, cite it, then explain it.
  • Every in-text citation needs an entry in the reference list.
  • Plagiarism is about credit; changing a few words does not fix it.
  • When in doubt, cite. Use AI only as your school allows, and acknowledge it.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give pupils one short invented passage and ask them to write a quotation, a paraphrase and a summary of it, each with a citation. Swap papers and let partners use the five-step method to test whether each paraphrase is honest. Then discuss what your school allows for AI tools in each kind of task.

Please note: style details (APA, MLA, Chicago, Harvard) vary by edition and institution, and AI rules differ by school, subject and exam board. Follow your own syllabus and teacher. The UK examples describe JCQ guidance and are not a statement about other systems. The study is used as a real example of how to cite; the passages marked "invented" are not from real sources.
