Organic Chemistry Basics: The Chemistry of Carbon

Fuels, plastics, medicines, food and every living cell are built on carbon. Learn the few simple rules that organise millions of carbon compounds.

Carbon is one atom with a remarkable talent: it can join to other carbon atoms and to a handful of other elements in almost endless combinations. Organic chemistry is the study of those carbon compounds. It explains petrol and plastic, vinegar and soap, sugar and DNA. The good news is that you do not need to memorise millions of compounds. A few families and a few rules organise nearly all of them.

1.What Is Organic Chemistry?

Organic chemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies compounds of carbon, most of which also contain hydrogen and often oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur or halogens such as chlorine. The word “organic” has an old origin. Two centuries ago many scientists believed these compounds could only be made by living things. That belief ended in 1828, when Friedrich Wöhler made urea, a compound found in urine, in the laboratory from non-living starting materials. After that, chemists realised that carbon compounds follow the same rules as all other chemistry.

A few carbon-containing substances are still usually classed as inorganic in school chemistry: carbon dioxide (CO₂), carbon monoxide (CO), carbonates such as calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), and the pure element in its forms such as diamond and graphite. So “contains carbon” is not the full definition. Organic compounds are, in practice, the compounds built on chains and rings of carbon atoms, usually bonded to hydrogen.

Do not confuse this chemical meaning with the word “organic” on food labels, which is about how crops are farmed and has nothing to do with the chemistry of the molecules.

2.Why Carbon Is Special

A carbon atom has four outer-shell electrons, so it forms four covalent bonds. This is called tetravalency (valency 4). In methane, CH₄, the four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron with angles of about 109.5°, so molecules are three-dimensional, not flat.

Carbon also shows strong catenation: its atoms bond to each other to form long chains, branched chains and rings. The carbon–carbon single bond is strong and stable, so these chains do not fall apart easily. Other elements such as silicon and sulfur can form short chains, but none matches carbon in the length, variety and stability of its structures. Carbon can also form double bonds (C=C) and triple bonds (C≡C), which adds even more variety.

Think about it

With four bonds, one carbon atom can be at the centre of a molecule like methane, or it can be one link in a chain of thousands of carbon atoms. This one flexible building block is why carbon compounds outnumber the compounds of all other elements combined in everyday life.

3.Hydrocarbons: Carbon and Hydrogen Only

A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen. The main families are shown below. A saturated hydrocarbon has only single bonds between carbon atoms, so each carbon holds as many hydrogen atoms as it possibly can. An unsaturated hydrocarbon has at least one double or triple carbon–carbon bond, so it holds fewer hydrogen atoms.

FamilyBondingGeneral formulaFirst members
AlkanesOnly C–C single bonds (saturated)CₙH₂ₙ₊₂CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈
AlkenesOne C=C double bond (unsaturated)CₙH₂ₙC₂H₄, C₃H₆, C₄H₈
AlkynesOne C≡C triple bond (unsaturated)CₙH₂ₙ₋₂C₂H₂, C₃H₄
Figure 1. Structural formulae of four small hydrocarbons. Every carbon atom has exactly four bonds; a double line counts as two bonds and a triple line as three.

Notice that “unsaturated” does not mean “has no hydrogen”. It means the molecule has room to take on extra atoms by opening up its double or triple bond. This is the key to alkene chemistry later in this article.

4.Homologous Series and Naming

A homologous series is a family of compounds with the same functional group, the same general formula, and similar chemical properties. Each member differs from the next by one extra –CH₂– unit. Their physical properties change in a regular way, for example boiling point rises as the chain gets longer, because longer molecules have stronger forces between them.

The name of an organic compound tells you its structure. The first part (the stem) shows the number of carbon atoms in the longest chain, and the ending shows the family.

CarbonsStemAlkane (–ane)Alkene (–ene)Boiling point of alkane (approx.)
1meth-methane CH₄none−162 °C
2eth-ethane C₂H₆ethene C₂H₄−89 °C
3prop-propane C₃H₈propene C₃H₆−42 °C
4but-butane C₄H₁₀butene C₄H₈about −1 °C
5pent-pentane C₅H₁₂pentene C₅H₁₀36 °C
6hex-hexane C₆H₁₄hexene C₆H₁₂69 °C

This is why methane, ethane, propane and butane are gases at room temperature, while pentane and hexane are liquids. Remember the first four stems (meth, eth, prop, but) in order; they are used in almost every organic question.

Worked example 1: finding formulae from the general formula

Question: Write the molecular formulae of the alkane and the alkene that each contain 7 carbon atoms.

  1. Alkane: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ with n = 7 gives C₇H₁₆ (2 × 7 + 2 = 16).
  2. Alkene: CₙH₂ₙ with n = 7 gives C₇H₁₄ (2 × 7 = 14).
  3. Check: the alkene has two fewer hydrogen atoms than the alkane because of the C=C bond.

Answer: heptane C₇H₁₆ and heptene C₇H₁₄.

5.Isomers: Same Formula, Different Structure

Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae. The smallest alkane that has isomers is butane, C₄H₁₀. One isomer is a straight chain of four carbons (butane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃). The other has a branch: a chain of three carbons with a CH₃ group on the middle one (2-methylpropane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃). They have different boiling points and slightly different properties, even though both are C₄H₁₀.

The number of possible isomers grows quickly as chains get longer. This is one reason there are so many organic compounds: C₅H₁₂ has three isomers and C₈H₁₈ has eighteen.

6.Crude Oil, Fractional Distillation and Cracking

Crude oil is a mixture of many hydrocarbons, formed over millions of years from the remains of tiny sea organisms. It is separated by fractional distillation. The oil is heated until most of it vaporises and is fed into a tall column that is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top. Each hydrocarbon rises until it reaches a height where it is cool enough to condense. Small molecules with low boiling points reach the top; large molecules with high boiling points condense near the bottom.

Figure 2. Fractional distillation of crude oil (simplified, not to scale). Watch the gold dots: hot vapour rises and condenses at the level matching its boiling point. Each fraction is itself a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar chain lengths.

As chain length increases, hydrocarbons become less flammable, more viscous (thicker and more like treacle) and darker, and their boiling points rise. This pattern explains why bitumen is a thick black solid-like material while petrol flows easily.

The refinery produces more large molecules than society needs, and more small ones than it gets. Cracking fixes this by breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful molecules. It uses high temperature and a catalyst (or steam at very high temperature). The products include a shorter alkane and an alkene. For example, decane can crack to octane and ethene:

C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄

Check the balance: carbon 10 = 8 + 2 and hydrogen 22 = 18 + 4. Cracking is valuable because the alkenes it produces are the starting materials for plastics and many other chemicals.

7.Reactions of Alkanes

Alkanes are fairly unreactive because their C–C and C–H bonds are strong and non-polar. Their two important reactions are burning and substitution.

Combustion

With plenty of air, alkanes burn completely to give carbon dioxide and water, and release a lot of energy. This is why they are used as fuels.

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

If the air supply is limited, incomplete combustion occurs and the products can include carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon (soot), as well as water. The flame turns yellow and sooty.

Safety note

Carbon monoxide is a colourless, odourless, poisonous gas. Never use a gas heater, charcoal burner or generator in a closed room. Make sure gas appliances are well ventilated and serviced.

Worked example 2: balancing the combustion of propane

Question: Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of propane, C₃H₈.

  1. Write the skeleton: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
  2. Balance carbon first: 3 carbons on the left, so put 3 in front of CO₂.
  3. Balance hydrogen: 8 hydrogens on the left, so put 4 in front of H₂O (4 × 2 = 8).
  4. Count oxygen on the right: 3 × 2 + 4 = 10 oxygen atoms, so put 5 in front of O₂ (5 × 2 = 10).
  5. Check every atom again: C 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 10 = 10.
C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Substitution

In ultraviolet light, an alkane reacts with chlorine in a substitution reaction: a hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom.

CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl  (UV light)

Further substitution can continue to produce CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃ and CCl₄, so a mixture of products is usually formed.

8.Reactions of Alkenes

Alkenes are much more reactive than alkanes because the C=C double bond can open up. In an addition reaction two reactants combine to form a single product, and no atoms are lost. This is possible because the double bond becomes a single bond as new atoms attach to the two carbons.

Reactant addedConditionsProduct from ethene
Hydrogen (H₂)nickel catalyst, heatethane, C₂H₆ (hydrogenation)
Bromine (Br₂)room temperature1,2-dibromoethane, C₂H₄Br₂
Steam (H₂O)phosphoric acid catalyst, high temperature and pressureethanol, C₂H₅OH (hydration)

The bromine water test

This is the standard test to tell alkenes from alkanes. Shake the substance with orange-brown bromine water. An alkene decolourises it (orange-brown to colourless) because the bromine adds across the double bond. An alkane causes no change, because it is saturated.

Safety note

Bromine is toxic and corrosive. Bromine water tests are done by a teacher or in a properly equipped laboratory, never at home.

Worked example 3: which gas is it?

Question: Gas X and gas Y both burn. When each is bubbled through bromine water, X leaves the water orange-brown and Y turns it colourless. Which is the alkene?

Reasoning: A colour change to colourless means bromine has added across a C=C bond. So Y contains a double bond and is the alkene (unsaturated). X does not react, so it is consistent with a saturated alkane.

9.Alcohols and Carboxylic Acids

A functional group is the atom or group of atoms that gives a family its characteristic reactions. Two functional groups appear in almost every school syllabus.

FamilyFunctional groupGeneral formulaCommon example
Alcohols–OH (hydroxyl)CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OHethanol, C₂H₅OH
Carboxylic acids–COOH (carboxyl)CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOHethanoic acid, CH₃COOH

Ethanol can be made by fermentation, in which yeast converts glucose to ethanol and carbon dioxide at about 25–35 °C without oxygen:

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

It is also made industrially from ethene and steam. Ethanol burns well, is used as a solvent, in disinfectant products and as a fuel additive. Drinking alcoholic beverages affects the brain and liver, which is why they are age-restricted.

Ethanoic acid (the acid in vinegar) is a weak acid. It reacts with metals, carbonates and bases like other acids, but only partly ionises in water. Ethanol and ethanoic acid react together, with a little concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst, to form an ester and water. Ethyl ethanoate has a fruity smell, and many esters give fruits and perfumes their scents.

CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O

10.Polymers: Making Giant Molecules

A polymer is a very long molecule made by joining many small molecules called monomers. In addition polymerisation, monomers that contain a C=C double bond open their double bonds and link together in a chain, with no other product. Ethene becomes poly(ethene), commonly called polythene; propene becomes poly(propene); chloroethene becomes PVC.

Figure 3. Addition polymerisation of ethene. The double bond of each monomer opens and the carbons link into one long chain. The gold bonds pulse to show the new links forming.

The simplest way to write it is: n CH₂=CH₂ → –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–, where n can be thousands.

Some polymers form by a different route called condensation polymerisation. Two different monomers join and a small molecule, usually water, is lost each time. Nylon and polyester (PET, used in drinks bottles) are made this way. Natural polymers include starch and cellulose (from glucose), proteins (from amino acids) and DNA.

Real-world connection

Most common plastics are very durable and do not rot, which makes them useful but creates waste problems. Reducing use, reusing, recycling and proper disposal all help. Never burn plastics: many release harmful fumes, and PVC can produce toxic, corrosive gases.

11.Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Saying all carbon compounds are organic. CO₂, CO and carbonates are normally treated as inorganic.
  • Giving carbon five bonds. Count the bonds on every carbon in your drawings; there must be exactly four.
  • Thinking unsaturated means “no hydrogen”. It means at least one C=C or C≡C bond.
  • Writing “the bromine goes clear”. The correct observation is orange-brown to colourless (clear can still be coloured).
  • Mixing up addition and substitution. Alkenes undergo addition (one product); alkanes undergo substitution (two products).
  • Forgetting that isomers must have the same molecular formula. Different formulae mean different compounds, not isomers.

12.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. Write the formula of the alkane with 12 carbon atoms.

Answer: C₁₂H₂₆ (2 × 12 + 2 = 26).

2. What is the general formula of the alkenes, and what is the formula of the alkene with 4 carbon atoms?

Answer: CₙH₂ₙ; butene is C₄H₈.

3. Balance: C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O for complete combustion.

Answer: 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O.

4. Why does the boiling point increase along the alkane series?

Answer: Longer molecules have stronger forces between them, so more energy is needed to separate them.

5. What is the observation when ethene is shaken with bromine water, and why?

Answer: Orange-brown to colourless, because bromine adds across the C=C double bond.

6. Name the two isomers of C₄H₁₀.

Answer: Butane (straight chain) and 2-methylpropane (branched).

7. Name the monomer of poly(ethene) and the type of polymerisation.

Answer: Ethene; addition polymerisation.

13.Summary

  • Carbon forms four bonds and chains, rings, double and triple bonds, giving huge variety.
  • Hydrocarbons contain only C and H: alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, saturated), alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ) and alkynes (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂).
  • A homologous series shares a functional group, general formula and similar reactions.
  • Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation; cracking turns long chains into shorter alkanes and alkenes.
  • Alkanes burn and undergo substitution; alkenes undergo addition and decolourise bromine water.
  • Alcohols (–OH) and carboxylic acids (–COOH) are key functional-group families.
  • Polymers are long chains built from monomers by addition or condensation polymerisation.
For teachers and parents

Suggested use: have students build models of C₄H₁₀ isomers with modelling kits, then draw them. The bromine-water test should only be demonstrated by a teacher with suitable ventilation and eye protection; cyclohexane versus cyclohexene is a typical alkane-versus-alkene pair for demonstration. Parents can help by connecting the topic to fuels, packaging and cooking at home, and by reinforcing the safety messages about carbon monoxide and burning plastics. Please follow your own syllabus for naming rules and required reactions.
