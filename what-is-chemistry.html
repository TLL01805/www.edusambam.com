What Is Chemistry? The Science of Matter and Change

Chemistry is the science of what things are made of and how they change. Start here to see the big picture before you meet atoms, equations and reactions.

Why does bread rise, why does iron rust and why does soap clean? Chemistry gives the answers. It is the science that studies what everything is made of, how it behaves and how it changes. This opening article gives you the big picture: what chemistry is, how chemists work, how they measure, and how to stay safe. The other Chemistry articles on EDUSAMBAM build on it.

1.What Is Chemistry?

Chemistry is the science that studies matter (anything that has mass and takes up space), its properties, its structure, and the changes it undergoes. Because everything physical around you is matter, from the air to your phone to your body, chemistry touches every part of life.

Chemistry is sometimes called the “central science” because it connects physics, biology, earth science and engineering. Physics explains the forces and energy inside atoms; biology depends on the chemical reactions inside cells; geology studies the minerals and chemistry of rocks; medicine relies on chemicals that act on the body.

2.Matter: Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

All matter can be sorted into a few types:

  • An element contains only one kind of atom, for example oxygen, iron or gold. There are about 118 known elements, listed in the periodic table.
  • A compound contains two or more elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio, for example water (H₂O) or sodium chloride (NaCl).
  • A mixture contains two or more substances that are not chemically joined, for example air or sea water, and can be separated by physical methods.

A physical property can be observed without changing the substance, such as colour, melting point, density or whether it dissolves. A chemical property describes how a substance reacts, such as whether it burns or rusts. A physical change (like melting) does not make a new substance; a chemical change (like burning) does. You will study these ideas in the Mixtures and Solutions, States of Matter and Chemical Reactions articles.

3.The Main Branches of Chemistry

Chemistry is huge, so chemists specialise. Most areas belong to one of a few branches, though they often overlap.

4.A Short History of Chemistry

Chemistry grew from ancient crafts such as metalworking, pottery, dyeing and medicine. Some key steps are:

  • Alchemy (many centuries, in several civilisations): alchemists tried to turn cheaper metals into gold and to find medicines that would prolong life. They did not succeed, but they developed apparatus and methods, such as distillation, that later chemists used.
  • 1661, Robert Boyle: argued in his book The Sceptical Chymist that chemistry should rely on experiments and clear ideas about elements, and he studied how gases behave.
  • 1770s to 1780s, Antoine Lavoisier: showed the role of oxygen in burning and established the conservation of mass through careful weighing.
  • Early 1800s, John Dalton: developed the modern atomic theory.
  • 1869, Dmitri Mendeleev: published a periodic table that organised the elements and predicted undiscovered ones.
  • 1898 onward, Marie Curie and Pierre Curie: studied radioactivity and discovered polonium and radium. Marie Curie won Nobel Prizes in both Physics (1903) and Chemistry (1911).

Chemists in many countries have contributed to the science, including scholars of the medieval Islamic world such as Jabir ibn Hayyan, whose name is linked with early laboratory methods, though historians note that many works attributed to him were written by later authors.

5.How Chemists Work: The Scientific Method

Chemists learn about the world through experiments that test ideas. The process is a loop, not a fixed recipe.

Figure 2. The scientific method as a loop. The red arrows pulse in order, from question to conclusion, and then back again when others check and extend the work.

A good experiment is a fair test: you change only one factor and keep everything else the same.

  • The independent variable is the one thing you change on purpose.
  • The dependent variable is what you measure as the result.
  • Control variables are the things you keep the same.

Repeating an experiment improves reliability. Accuracy means how close a measurement is to the true value; precision means how close repeated measurements are to each other.

Worked example 1: a fair test

Question: A student wants to test whether temperature changes how fast sugar dissolves. Name the variables.

  1. Independent variable: the water temperature (for example 20 °C, 40 °C, 60 °C).
  2. Dependent variable: the time taken for the sugar to dissolve.
  3. Control variables: mass of sugar, volume of water, stirring, and the type of sugar.

She should repeat each temperature three times and use the mean time.

6.Measuring in Chemistry

Chemistry depends on careful measurement, using SI units (the international system).

QuantityCommon unitTypical apparatus
Massgram (g), kilogram (kg)Balance
Volumecubic centimetre (cm³), cubic decimetre (dm³); 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³Measuring cylinder, pipette, burette
Temperaturedegree Celsius (°C); kelvin (K) = °C + 273Thermometer
Timesecond (s)Stopwatch
Amount of substancemole (mol)Calculated, not measured directly
Energyjoule (J), kilojoule (kJ)Calorimeter

Read liquid volumes at eye level, from the bottom of the curved surface (the meniscus). Write measurements with the correct unit, and avoid giving more digits than your apparatus can measure.

Worked example 2: density

Question: A metal block has a mass of 27 g and a volume of 10 cm³. Calculate its density.

  1. Density = mass ÷ volume.
  2. = 27 ÷ 10 = 2.7 g/cm³.

Answer: 2.7 g/cm³. This value is close to that of aluminium, so the block could be aluminium.

Worked example 3: mean of repeated readings

Question: Three titration readings are 24.10, 24.20 and 24.15 cm³. Find the mean.

  1. Add: 24.10 + 24.20 + 24.15 = 72.45.
  2. Divide by 3: 72.45 ÷ 3 = 24.15 cm³.

7.Staying Safe in the Laboratory

Safety is part of chemistry, not an extra. Hazard symbols on containers warn you of the risks.

Figure 3. Common hazard symbols, simplified. Always check the label of every chemical and follow your teacher’s instructions.
  • Wear eye protection, and tie back long hair. Wear a lab coat or apron if provided.
  • Never taste, and do not sniff chemicals directly. If you need to smell a gas, waft it gently towards your nose, only when told to.
  • Point test tubes away from people when heating, and use tongs or holders.
  • Keep flammable liquids away from flames, and never leave a flame unattended.
  • Do not eat or drink in the laboratory, and wash your hands afterwards.
  • Report spills and breakages to your teacher at once; do not clean up chemicals yourself.
  • If a chemical gets on your skin or in your eyes, rinse with plenty of running water and tell your teacher straight away.
Safety note

Never experiment with unknown chemicals at home, and never mix household cleaning products. Some combinations release poisonous gases.

8.Chemistry and Society

Chemistry has helped to produce medicines, clean water, fertilisers that help feed the world, fuels, plastics, fabrics and electronics. It also raises problems such as pollution, plastic waste and climate change. Green chemistry aims to design products and processes that use less energy, produce less waste and use safer materials. Understanding chemistry helps citizens make informed decisions about health, food, energy and the environment.

Careers that use chemistry include chemist, pharmacist, doctor, nurse, chemical engineer, food scientist, forensic scientist, environmental scientist, materials scientist, teacher and many more.

9.How to Study Chemistry Well

  1. Learn the symbols and the first 20 elements early; they are the alphabet of chemistry.
  2. Practise writing and balancing equations regularly, and check atoms on both sides.
  3. Always write units with answers and show your working in steps.
  4. Connect each topic to something real: cooking, cleaning, batteries, rust.
  5. Draw diagrams and flow charts, and explain ideas aloud to someone else.
  6. Use past papers and quizzes, and learn from every mistake.

10.Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Thinking chemicals are only dangerous liquids in laboratories. Water, salt and sugar are chemicals, and natural substances can also be harmful.
  • Calling every mixture a compound. Compounds have a fixed formula; mixtures do not.
  • Mixing up physical and chemical changes. Check whether a new substance forms.
  • Changing more than one variable in an experiment. The test is then not fair.
  • Confusing accuracy and precision. A set of readings can be close together but all wrong.
  • Ignoring units. A number without a unit has no meaning in chemistry.

11.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. In one sentence, what is chemistry?

Answer: The science that studies matter, its properties and structure, and how it changes.

2. Is air an element, compound or mixture?

Answer: A mixture of gases.

3. Which branch of chemistry identifies and measures substances?

Answer: Analytical chemistry.

4. Name the independent, dependent and one control variable in an experiment on how salt amount affects the boiling point of water.

Answer: Independent: mass of salt; dependent: boiling temperature; control: volume of water (or pressure, or the thermometer used).

5. What is the density of a block of mass 54 g and volume 20 cm³?

Answer: 54 ÷ 20 = 2.7 g/cm³.

6. Convert 250 cm³ to dm³.

Answer: 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.25 dm³.

7. What should you do first if a chemical gets in your eyes?

Answer: Rinse with plenty of running water and tell your teacher straight away.

12.Summary

  • Chemistry studies matter, its properties, structure and changes.
  • Matter is made of elements, compounds and mixtures; changes can be physical or chemical.
  • Main branches: organic, inorganic, physical, analytical, biochemistry, and industrial and environmental chemistry.
  • Chemists use experiments, fair tests and repeats to build reliable knowledge.
  • Use SI units, read apparatus carefully and keep sensible precision.
  • Hazard symbols, eye protection and good habits keep everyone safe.
  • Chemistry shapes medicine, food, materials, energy and the environment.
For teachers and parents

Suggested use: use this article as the first lesson of a unit. Students can sort household items into elements, compounds and mixtures, design a fair test for dissolving, and match hazard symbols to cards. Make sure every practical begins with a short safety talk and a risk check. Parents can encourage curiosity by asking “what is it made of, and what changes when…?” at home. Please follow your own school safety rules and syllabus.
