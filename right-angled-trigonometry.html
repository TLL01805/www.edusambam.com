Right-Angled Trigonometry: SOH-CAH-TOA Explained

Label the sides, choose sin, cos or tan, and find any missing side or angle. Includes exact values, angles of elevation and depression, and tools to check your work.

How tall is a tower you cannot climb? How wide is a river you cannot cross? How steep is a ramp? Trigonometry answers questions like these by using the angles and sides of right-angled triangles. If you know one angle and one side, you can calculate every other side and angle without measuring them. This guide explains the idea in plain words, builds the three ratios (sine, cosine and tangent) from similar triangles, and then shows how to find missing sides, missing angles, and how to handle angles of elevation and depression.

1.What Trigonometry Is For

A right-angled triangle has one angle of exactly 90°. Its other two angles add up to 90°. In a right-angled triangle the sides and the angles are tied together: if you change an angle, the shape of the triangle changes and the sides change in a fixed way. Trigonometry records that link. The word comes from Greek words meaning “triangle” and “measure”.

You may already know Pythagoras’ theorem, which connects the three sides of a right-angled triangle (a² + b² = c²) but never uses an angle. Trigonometry is the tool you need when an angle is involved, either given in the question or asked for.

2.Labelling the Sides

Every trigonometry question starts with the same first step: label the three sides. The labels depend on which angle you are using, which we usually call θ (the Greek letter “theta”).

  • The hypotenuse is the longest side. It is always opposite the right angle, and it never changes, whichever angle you use.
  • The opposite side is directly across from the angle θ. It does not touch θ.
  • The adjacent side is next to θ. It touches θ and the right angle. (Adjacent means “next to”.)
Figure 1. The labels are relative to the angle θ. The sides glow in turn: adjacent, opposite, hypotenuse.
“The opposite side is always the vertical side”

Opposite and adjacent depend on the angle you choose, not on how the triangle is drawn.

The fix: in Figure 1 the vertical side is opposite θ. But if you work from the other acute angle (at the top), the vertical side touches that angle, so it becomes the adjacent side, and the bottom side becomes the opposite. Only the hypotenuse keeps its name. Always ask: “Which angle am I using? Which side is across from it?”

3.The Three Ratios: SOH-CAH-TOA

Why does an angle fix a ratio of sides? Look at Figure 2. The small triangle (sides 3, 4, 5) and the big triangle (sides 6, 8, 10) share the same angle θ, so they have the same shape: they are similar. In both, opposite ÷ hypotenuse is 3/5 = 6/10 = 0.6. Making the triangle bigger multiplies every side by the same number, so the ratio does not change. The ratio depends only on the angle. That is why a calculator can store these ratios for every angle.

Figure 2. A bigger triangle with the same angle θ has the same ratios. The small triangle pulses.

There are three main ratios. Each has a short name and is remembered with the word SOH-CAH-TOA:

RatioFormulaMemory
Sine (sin)sin θ = opposite ÷ hypotenuseSOH
Cosine (cos)cos θ = adjacent ÷ hypotenuseCAH
Tangent (tan)tan θ = opposite ÷ adjacentTOA

A common way to remember it is a silly sentence, such as “Some Old Horses Can Always Hear Their Owners Approach”. In the sentence, the first letters spell out S-O-H, C-A-H, T-O-A.

Do the three ratios depend on the size of the triangle? No. Use the explorer below to prove it to yourself: change the hypotenuse and watch the sides change but sin θ, cos θ and tan θ stay the same.

4.Using Your Calculator

Your calculator has sin, cos and tan keys. Before you start, make sure it is in degree mode (it shows DEG or D on the screen). Test it: type sin 30 and press equals. The answer should be exactly 0.5. If you see −0.988, the calculator is in radian mode and every answer will be wrong.

To go from a ratio back to an angle, press SHIFT (or 2ndF) and then sin, cos or tan to get sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹ or tan⁻¹. These “inverse” functions are written arcsin, arccos and arctan on some calculators.

“sin 30 = −0.988”

This happens when the calculator is in the wrong mode, and it is a silent way to lose every mark in a test.

The fix: switch the calculator to degree mode before you begin. In radian mode the calculator reads “30” as 30 radians, which is a very different angle (about 1719°), so sin 30 comes out as −0.988. Always test with sin 30 = 0.5 at the start of an exam.

5.Finding a Missing Side

Use these three steps every time:

  1. Label the sides relative to the known angle: hypotenuse, opposite, adjacent.
  2. Choose the ratio that uses the side you know and the side you want (ignore the third side). Tick which letters you have: O and H means sine, A and H means cosine, O and A means tangent.
  3. Write the equation, rearrange for the unknown, and calculate.

When the unknown is on top of the fraction, multiply. When the unknown is on the bottom, divide.

Example 1 · Unknown on top (sine)

A ladder 4.5 m long leans against a wall and makes an angle of 58° with the ground. How high up the wall does it reach?

The ladder is the hypotenuse (H = 4.5). The height is opposite the 58° angle (O is wanted). O and H, so use sine: sin 58° = O ÷ 4.5. So O = 4.5 × sin 58° = 4.5 × 0.8480 = 3.816, which is 3.82 m to 3 significant figures.

Example 2 · Unknown on top (cosine)

How far is the foot of the same ladder from the wall? The distance along the ground is adjacent to the 58° angle (A is wanted), and H = 4.5. A and H, so use cosine: cos 58° = A ÷ 4.5, so A = 4.5 × cos 58° = 4.5 × 0.5299 = 2.38 m. Check with Pythagoras: 3.82² + 2.38² = 14.6 + 5.7 ≈ 20.25 = 4.5² ✓.

Example 3 · Using tangent

A ramp has a horizontal length of 8 m and makes an angle of 12° with the ground. Find the height it rises.

The horizontal length is adjacent (A = 8) and the height is opposite (O wanted). Use tangent: tan 12° = O ÷ 8, so O = 8 × tan 12° = 8 × 0.2126 = 1.70 m.

Example 4 · Unknown on the bottom

A right-angled triangle has an adjacent side of 7 cm and an angle of 40°. Find the hypotenuse.

A and H, so use cosine: cos 40° = 7 ÷ H. The unknown is on the bottom, so rearrange: H = 7 ÷ cos 40° = 7 ÷ 0.7660 = 9.14 cm.

Example 5 · Unknown on the bottom (tangent)

The opposite side is 9 cm and the angle is 28°. Find the adjacent side. O and A, so use tangent: tan 28° = 9 ÷ A. So A = 9 ÷ tan 28° = 9 ÷ 0.5317 = 16.9 cm. A sensible check: the adjacent side must be longer than the opposite side here, because 28° is smaller than 45°.

“cos 40° = 7 ÷ H, so H = 7 × cos 40° = 5.36 cm”

The hypotenuse is the longest side, so it cannot be shorter than the 7 cm side.

The fix: the unknown H is on the bottom of the fraction, so you divide: H = 7 ÷ cos 40° = 9.14 cm. A good habit is to check that the hypotenuse is the longest side. 5.36 cm is shorter than 7 cm, so it fails the check straight away.

6.Finding a Missing Angle

When you know two sides and want the angle, work out the ratio from the two sides and then use the inverse function. For example, if tan θ = 0.625, then θ = tan⁻¹(0.625). The label-and-choose steps are the same: decide which two sides you know (O and A, O and H, or A and H) and pick tan, sin or cos.

Example 6 · Using tangent

The opposite side is 5 cm and the adjacent side is 8 cm. Find θ.

tan θ = 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625, so θ = tan⁻¹(0.625) = 32.0° (1 d.p.). The other acute angle is 90° − 32.0° = 58.0°, because the angles of a triangle add up to 180°.

Example 7 · Using sine

The opposite side is 5 cm and the hypotenuse is 13 cm. sin θ = 5 ÷ 13 = 0.3846…, so θ = sin⁻¹(5 ÷ 13) = 22.6°.

Example 8 · A ladder against a wall

A ladder 6 m long has its foot 2 m from the wall. Find the angle it makes with the ground and the height it reaches.

The 2 m along the ground is adjacent and the ladder is the hypotenuse, so use cosine: cos θ = 2 ÷ 6 = 1/3, so θ = cos⁻¹(1/3) = 70.5°. For the height, use Pythagoras: √(6² − 2²) = √32 = 5.66 m.

“tan θ = 5 ÷ 8, so θ = 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625°”

0.625 is a ratio, not an angle.

The fix: 0.625 is the value of tan θ. To get the angle you must use the inverse function: θ = tan⁻¹(0.625) = 32.0°. A quick sense check: an angle in a right-angled triangle must be less than 90°, and here the opposite side (5) is shorter than the adjacent side (8), so the angle must be less than 45°.

7.Special Angles: Exact Values

For 30°, 45° and 60° you can write the ratios exactly, without a calculator. They come from two simple triangles (Figure 3). Cut a square along its diagonal to get a right-angled isosceles triangle with sides 1, 1 and √2 (by Pythagoras: 1² + 1² = 2). Cut an equilateral triangle of side 2 in half to get a triangle with angles 30°, 60°, 90° and sides 1, √3 and 2 (the height is √(2² − 1²) = √3).

Figure 3. The two special triangles give the exact values of sine, cosine and tangent at 30°, 45° and 60°.

Two patterns help you remember the table. First, sin 30° = cos 60° and sin 60° = cos 30°, because the two acute angles of a right-angled triangle add up to 90°, so “opposite” for one angle is “adjacent” for the other. Second, tan θ = sin θ ÷ cos θ, so tan 60° = (√3/2) ÷ (1/2) = √3. (tan 90° is not defined because cos 90° = 0 and you cannot divide by zero.)

Example 9 · Exact answers

(a) A ramp 8 m long makes an angle of 30° with the ground. The rise is 8 × sin 30° = 8 × ½ = 4 m exactly.

(b) A triangle has an adjacent side of 4 cm and an angle of 60°. The opposite side is 4 × tan 60° = 4√3 cm, which is about 6.93 cm.

(c) In a right-angled isosceles triangle with equal sides 5 cm, the hypotenuse is 5√2 cm, so sin 45° = 5 ÷ 5√2 = 1/√2 = √2/2 ✓.

8.Angles of Elevation and Depression

The angle of elevation is measured upwards from the horizontal to the line of sight (for example, looking up at the top of a tower). The angle of depression is measured downwards from the horizontal (for example, looking down from a cliff at a boat). Both are measured from a horizontal line, never from the vertical.

Figure 4. The angle of depression at A equals the angle of elevation at B. The dashes flow along the line of sight.

The two angles in Figure 4 are equal because the horizontal lines are parallel, so they are alternate angles. This lets you draw a triangle with the angle at the bottom, which is usually easier to work with.

Example 10 · Elevation

A person stands 20 m from the base of a tower. The angle of elevation of the top is 35°. Find the height of the tower.

The distance along the ground is adjacent and the height is opposite: tan 35° = height ÷ 20, so height = 20 × tan 35° = 20 × 0.7002 = 14.0 m.

Example 11 · Depression

From the top of a 60 m cliff, the angle of depression of a boat is 25°. How far is the boat from the foot of the cliff?

The angle of elevation from the boat to the cliff top is also 25°. The cliff height is opposite and the distance is adjacent: tan 25° = 60 ÷ d, so d = 60 ÷ tan 25° = 60 ÷ 0.4663 = 128.7, which is about 129 m.

Example 12 · Two triangles

You stand 40 m from a building. The angle of elevation of the bottom of a flagpole on the roof is 32° and of the top of the pole is 41°. Find the height of the pole.

Height to the bottom of the pole: 40 × tan 32° = 40 × 0.6249 = 24.99 m (the building is about 25.0 m tall). Height to the top of the pole: 40 × tan 41° = 40 × 0.8693 = 34.77 m. The flagpole is the difference: 34.77 − 24.99 = 9.78 m. Keep the full calculator values until the last step, or the subtraction will lose accuracy.

9.Trigonometry and Pythagoras Together

Use Pythagoras when you know two sides and want the third. Use trigonometry when an angle is involved. Many problems need both: find a missing side by one method, then use it in the other.

Example 13 · A 5, 12, 13 triangle

A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 13 cm and one side 5 cm. By Pythagoras the third side is √(13² − 5²) = √(169 − 25) = √144 = 12 cm. The smallest angle is opposite the shortest side: sin θ = 5 ÷ 13, so θ = 22.6°.

Example 14 · A rectangle

A rectangle is 12 cm long and 9 cm wide. The diagonal is √(12² + 9²) = √225 = 15 cm. The angle between the diagonal and the 12 cm side has tan θ = 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75, so θ = tan⁻¹(0.75) = 36.9°.

For triangles without a right angle, you need the Sine Rule and Cosine Rule. For directions and 3D shapes, see Bearings & 3D Trigonometry.

10.Trigonometry in Real Life

  • Shadows. The sun is at an elevation of 35° and a pole casts a shadow 12 m long on level ground. The height of the pole is 12 × tan 35° = 8.40 m.
  • Rivers and distances. To find the width of a river, stand directly opposite a tree on the far bank, walk 40 m along the bank and measure the angle between the bank and the line to the tree as 52°. The width is 40 × tan 52° = 51.2 m.
  • Slopes and ramps. A road that rises 1 m for every 10 m along the horizontal (a gradient of 1 in 10) has an angle of tan⁻¹(0.1) = 5.71°. A ramp that rises 1.2 m over a horizontal distance of 8 m has angle tan⁻¹(1.2 ÷ 8) = 8.5°, and its sloping length is √(8² + 1.2²) = 8.09 m.
  • Building and safety. Carpenters, surveyors and engineers use these ratios to find roof lengths, ladder positions and the heights of structures they cannot measure directly.
  • Navigation and GPS. Finding positions from distances and directions is a trigonometry problem. See Bearings for a first look.

11.Common Mistakes

  • Labelling from the wrong angle. Opposite and adjacent change when you change the angle. Only the hypotenuse stays the same.
  • Calculator in the wrong mode. Check that it shows DEG and test with sin 30 = 0.5.
  • Multiplying when you should divide. If the unknown is on the bottom, divide. Check that the hypotenuse is the longest side.
  • Forgetting the inverse function when finding an angle (tan 0.625 is not 32°).
  • Rounding too early. Keep at least 4 significant figures in the middle of a calculation and round only the final answer, as in Example 12.
  • Using the wrong ratio. Say which two sides are involved (O and H, A and H, or O and A) before you pick sin, cos or tan.
  • Mixing the vertical and the horizontal for angles of elevation and depression. Both are measured from the horizontal.

12.Try It Yourself

  1. A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 10 cm and an angle of 40°. Find the side opposite the 40° angle.
  2. The opposite side is 6 cm and the adjacent side is 8 cm. Find the angle.
  3. The adjacent side is 5 cm and the angle is 60°. Find the hypotenuse (give an exact value).
Show answers

1. sin 40° = O ÷ 10, so O = 10 × sin 40° = 10 × 0.6428 = 6.43 cm.

2. tan θ = 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75, so θ = tan⁻¹(0.75) = 36.9°.

3. cos 60° = 5 ÷ H, so H = 5 ÷ ½ = 10 cm.

13.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] In triangle PQR, angle Q = 90°, PR = 12 cm and angle P = 35°. Find QR.
  2. [3 marks] A right-angled triangle has opposite side 9 cm and adjacent side 14 cm. Find the angle θ.
  3. [3 marks] A kite string 80 m long is taut and makes an angle of 52° with the horizontal ground. Find the height of the kite.
  4. [3 marks] From the top of a 30 m cliff the angle of depression of a boat is 18°. Find the distance of the boat from the foot of the cliff.
  5. [3 marks] A right-angled isosceles triangle has two equal sides of 5 cm. Show that the hypotenuse is 5√2 cm and that sin 45° = √2/2.
  6. [4 marks] A ladder 7.5 m long rests against a wall with its foot 2.1 m from the wall. Find (a) the angle the ladder makes with the ground and (b) the height it reaches.
  7. [5 marks] From a point on level ground 60 m from the foot of a tower, the angle of elevation of the top of the tower is 28° and of the top of a mast on the tower is 33°. Find the height of the mast.
Show answers

1. QR is opposite angle P and PR is the hypotenuse: QR = 12 × sin 35° = 6.88 cm.

2. tan θ = 9 ÷ 14, so θ = tan⁻¹(0.6429) = 32.7°.

3. Height = 80 × sin 52° = 63.0 m.

4. tan 18° = 30 ÷ d, so d = 30 ÷ tan 18° = 92.3 m.

5. By Pythagoras the hypotenuse is √(5² + 5²) = √50 = 5√2 cm. Then sin 45° = 5 ÷ 5√2 = 1/√2 = √2/2 (multiply top and bottom by √2).

6. (a) cos θ = 2.1 ÷ 7.5 = 0.28, so θ = cos⁻¹(0.28) = 73.7°. (b) Height = √(7.5² − 2.1²) = √51.84 = 7.2 m.

7. Top of mast: 60 × tan 33° = 38.96 m. Top of tower: 60 × tan 28° = 31.90 m. Mast = 38.96 − 31.90 = 7.06 m (using unrounded values).

14.Key Points

  • Label the sides from the chosen angle: hypotenuse (longest), opposite (across), adjacent (next to).
  • SOH-CAH-TOA: sin = O/H, cos = A/H, tan = O/A. The ratios depend only on the angle.
  • Unknown on top: multiply. Unknown on the bottom: divide.
  • To find an angle use sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹ or tan⁻¹.
  • Exact values: sin 30° = 1/2, cos 60° = 1/2, sin 45° = cos 45° = √2/2, tan 45° = 1, sin 60° = √3/2, tan 60° = √3.
  • Elevation and depression are measured from the horizontal, and they are equal for two observers looking at each other.
  • Use degree mode, keep full values in the middle, round at the end.

15.For Teachers and Parents

Begin with similar triangles and let learners measure the sides of several drawn triangles that share one angle: the ratio opposite ÷ hypotenuse should come out nearly the same every time, which makes the idea of “sine” concrete before the word is used. Make labelling a ritual: colour the hypotenuse, opposite and adjacent in three colours every time, and name the angle first. A clinometer made from a protractor, a straw and a weighted string lets learners measure the angle of elevation of a real tree or flagpole and check the height with a tape measure. Insist on a sense check (is the hypotenuse the longest side? is the angle below 90°?) after every answer.
