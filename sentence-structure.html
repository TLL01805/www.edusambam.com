Sentence Structure: From Basic to Advanced

Once you know the parts of speech, the next step is learning how they combine into sentences. Here is everything you need — from the simplest sentence to the most sophisticated.

A sentence is not just a string of words. It is a structure — and once you understand that structure, you can build sentences of any length or complexity with total control.

Every sentence in English, from the shortest command to the longest, most elaborate paragraph opener, is built from the same basic ingredients: subjects, predicates, and the way clauses combine. This guide starts with those basics and works all the way up to the sentence structures used in advanced academic and professional writing.

1.What Makes a Sentence a Sentence?

A complete sentence must do two things: name something (the subject) and say something about it (the predicate). Without both, a group of words is only a fragment, not a sentence.

Examples

Birds fly. — "Birds" is the subject, "fly" is the predicate.

The tired old dog slept peacefully on the porch all afternoon. — subject: "The tired old dog." Predicate: everything else.

2.Subjects

The subject is who or what the sentence is about. It is usually a noun, pronoun, or noun phrase.

  • Simple subject: the single noun or pronoun itself (e.g., "dog" in "The dog barked").
  • Complete subject: the simple subject plus all words describing it (e.g., "The large, muddy dog").
  • Compound subject: two or more subjects joined by a conjunction (e.g., "Sarah and Tom left early").

3.Predicates

The predicate tells us what the subject does, is, or has done to it. It always contains the main verb.

  • Simple predicate: just the verb (e.g., "ran" in "She ran").
  • Complete predicate: the verb plus everything that modifies or completes it (e.g., "ran quickly to the store").
  • Compound predicate: two or more verbs sharing the same subject (e.g., "She cooked dinner and washed the dishes").
Example

The exhausted marathon runner (complete subject) collapsed onto the grass and gasped for breath (compound predicate).

4.Phrases vs. Clauses

This distinction underpins everything that follows, so it is worth getting exactly right.

  • A phrase is a group of related words with no subject-verb pair of its own (e.g., "in the morning," "running quickly," "the old wooden chair").
  • A clause does contain a subject and a verb. Every sentence contains at least one clause.

5.Independent and Dependent Clauses

Clauses come in two kinds, and the difference is the single most important idea in this whole guide.

Independent Clause

Expresses a complete thought and can stand alone as its own sentence. Example: "The rain stopped."

Dependent (Subordinate) Clause

Has a subject and verb but cannot stand alone — it depends on an independent clause to complete its meaning. Example: "because the rain stopped" (incomplete on its own).

6.The Four Sentence Structures

Every English sentence, no matter how sophisticated, falls into one of exactly four structural categories.

StructureMade OfExample
SimpleOne independent clauseThe dog barked.
CompoundTwo+ independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction or semicolonThe dog barked, and the cat ran.
ComplexOne independent clause + one or more dependent clausesBecause it was raining, the dog barked.
Compound-ComplexTwo+ independent clauses + one or more dependent clausesBecause it was raining, the dog barked, and the cat ran inside.

Simple sentences contain a single independent clause — but "simple" does not mean short; a simple sentence can still have a compound subject or predicate, as long as there is only one clause.

Compound sentences link two independent clauses using a coordinating conjunction (remember the acronym FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or a semicolon.

Example

I wanted to go for a walk, but it started raining. / I wanted to go for a walk; it started raining.

Complex sentences combine an independent clause with at least one dependent clause, usually introduced by a subordinating conjunction (although, because, since, when, if, while, unless) or a relative pronoun (who, which, that).

Examples

Although she was tired, she finished the race. / The book that I borrowed is overdue.

Compound-complex sentences — the most advanced structure — combine two or more independent clauses with at least one dependent clause, all in a single sentence.

Example

Although the meeting ran late, the team finished the report, and everyone went home satisfied.

7.Sentence Types by Purpose

Separately from structure, sentences are also classified by what they do:

  • Declarative: makes a statement. "The library closes at nine."
  • Interrogative: asks a question. "Does the library close at nine?"
  • Imperative: gives a command or request. "Close the door, please."
  • Exclamatory: expresses strong emotion. "The library is closing right now!"

8.Word Order (SVO)

Standard English follows a Subject–Verb–Object word order, unlike many other languages. Getting this order wrong is one of the most common mistakes for learners moving from another language into English.

Example

Maria (subject) reads (verb) novels (object). Rearranging this order — "Novels reads Maria" — is grammatically incorrect in English.

9.Common Structural Errors

Even confident writers slip into these three mistakes regularly:

  • Sentence fragment: a group of words punctuated as a sentence but missing a subject, verb, or complete thought. "Because I was late." (incomplete — needs an independent clause attached.)
  • Run-on sentence: two or more independent clauses joined with no punctuation or connector at all. "I was late I missed the bus."
  • Comma splice: two independent clauses joined by only a comma, with no conjunction. "I was late, I missed the bus." (needs "and," a semicolon, or a period instead.)

10.Advanced: Parallel Structure

At a more advanced level, strong writing also depends on parallelism — keeping grammatically similar elements in the same form within a sentence, especially in lists.

Incorrect vs. Correct

Incorrect: "She likes reading, to write, and swimming."

Correct: "She likes reading, writing, and swimming."

11.Advanced: Relative Clauses

Relative clauses add detail about a noun using who, whom, whose, which, or that — and come in two forms that change punctuation and meaning.

Restrictive (no commas)

"The students who studied hard passed the exam." (Only those specific students — essential information.)

Non-Restrictive (with commas)

"My sister, who lives in Canada, is visiting next month." (Extra detail — the sentence still works without it.)

Real-World Example

Consider this sentence from a news report: "Although the storm had passed by morning, residents remained cautious, and local authorities continued monitoring the river levels closely." This is a compound-complex sentence: the dependent clause "Although the storm had passed by morning" sets up the context, while two independent clauses — "residents remained cautious" and "local authorities continued monitoring the river levels" — are joined by "and." Recognising this structure is exactly what separates a fluent reader from someone still decoding word by word.

Why This Matters

Sentence structure is the difference between writing that merely communicates and writing that persuades, flows, and holds a reader's attention. Once parts of speech (Topic I) tell you what each word is doing, sentence structure (Topic II) tells you how to arrange those words into clear, correct, and increasingly sophisticated ideas — the exact foundation the rest of this Grammar Basics series builds on.
