Transformations: Translation, Reflection, Rotation & Enlargement

Slide, flip, turn and resize shapes on a coordinate grid, and learn to describe each transformation fully and exactly. This is the skill examiners check for.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|28 min read|Last updated: November 2026|Mathematics

When you slide a book across a desk, look at yourself in a mirror, turn a steering wheel or zoom in on a photo, you are carrying out a transformation. In mathematics a transformation is a precise rule that moves or resizes a shape. There are four to master: translation (slide), reflection (flip), rotation (turn) and enlargement (resize). This guide shows you how to carry each one out on a coordinate grid, how to describe each one completely (the part that earns exam marks), and how they combine. It builds on Vectors and links to Matrices.

1.Object, Image and Prime Notation

The shape you start with is the object. The shape you end with is the image. A corner of the object is usually named with a capital letter, say A, and the matching corner of the image gets a prime mark: A′, read “A dash” or “A prime”. So the corners A, B, C of the object map to A′, B′, C′ on the image.

The first three transformations never change the size or shape of the object. Lengths and angles stay the same, so the image is congruent to the object. Only an enlargement changes lengths, and then the image is similar to the object: same angles, lengths multiplied by the scale factor. (See Congruence & Similarity.)

TranslationNoNoCongruent

2.Translation: Sliding by a Vector

A translation slides every point of a shape the same distance in the same direction. The slide is written as a column vector: the top number says how far to move right (positive) or left (negative), and the bottom number says how far to move up (positive) or down (negative). The shape does not turn or flip.

The vector 53 means “5 to the right and 3 up”. The vector −2−4 means “2 to the left and 4 down”. To find an image point, add the vector to the object point: (x, y) becomes (x + a, y + b).

Figure 1. The gold copy slides along the dashed lines from the object (blue) to the image (red). Every corner moves by the same vector 53.
Example 1 · A single point

Point A(2, 3) is translated by 5−2. Find A′.

A′ = (2 + 5, 3 + (−2)) = (7, 1). The point moved 5 right and 2 down.

Example 2 · A whole triangle

Triangle ABC has corners A(2, 1), B(5, 1) and C(2, 4). It is translated by −43. Find the image.

Add −4 to every x and 3 to every y: A′ = (−2, 4), B′ = (1, 4), C′ = (−2, 7). Check: the base A′B′ is still 3 units long and A′C′ is still 3 units tall, so the shape is unchanged.

Example 3 · Finding the vector

A translation maps A(−2, 5) onto A′(3, 1). Find the vector.

Subtract object from image: x-change = 3 − (−2) = 5, y-change = 1 − 5 = −4. The vector is 5−4. Check with a second point: B(0, 2) should map to (5, −2), and it does. If one point fails the check, the transformation is not a translation by that vector.

The distance moved is √(5² + (−4)²) = √41 ≈ 6.4 units, found with Pythagoras.

Example 4 · Two translations in a row

Point P(1, 1) is translated by 32 and then by −54. Add the vectors: 32 + −54 = −26. So P′ = (1 − 2, 1 + 6) = (−1, 7). Doing the two slides one at a time gives (4, 3) and then (−1, 7), the same answer.

“The vector 2−3 means 2 up and 3 to the left”

This is a very common mix-up of the order and the signs.

The fix: the top number is always the horizontal move (x) and the bottom number is the vertical move (y). A positive top number means right and a negative one means left. A positive bottom number means up and a negative one means down. So 2−3 means 2 right and 3 down. Say “across, then up” to remember the order.

3.Reflection: Flipping in a Mirror Line

A reflection flips a shape over a mirror line. Every point and its image are the same perpendicular distance from the mirror, on opposite sides, so the mirror line cuts the line joining a point to its image exactly in half at right angles. A reflection reverses the orientation: if the corners of the object read A, B, C clockwise, the corners of the image read A′, B′, C′ anticlockwise.

To describe a reflection fully you must give the equation of the mirror line. Vertical lines have equations of the form x = a and horizontal lines have equations of the form y = b. The x-axis is the line y = 0 and the y-axis is the line x = 0.

Figure 2. Each corner and its image are the same distance from the red mirror line. The gold copy folds over the mirror to land on the image.

Reflection rules to remember

Mirror linePoint (x, y) maps to
x-axis (y = 0)(x, −y)
y-axis (x = 0)(−x, y)
y = x(y, x)
y = −x(−y, −x)
Vertical line x = a(2a − x, y)
Horizontal line y = b(x, 2b − y)

The last two rows work for any vertical or horizontal mirror. They come from the “same distance” idea: a point at x is (x − a) from the line x = a, so its image is that same distance on the other side, at a − (x − a) = 2a − x.

Example 5 · Reflecting in the axes

Point P(5, −2) is reflected (a) in the x-axis and (b) in the y-axis.

(a) The x-axis changes the sign of y: P′ = (5, 2). (b) The y-axis changes the sign of x: P′ = (−5, −2).

Example 6 · Reflecting in y = x

Point B(4, 7) is reflected in the line y = x. The coordinates swap: B′ = (7, 4). Check: the midpoint of B and B′ is (5.5, 5.5), which lies on the line y = x ✓.

Example 7 · Mirror lines that are not the axes

(a) Reflect (7, 2) in the line x = 3. The point is 4 units to the right of the mirror (7 − 3 = 4), so the image is 4 units to the left of it: 3 − 4 = −1. Using the rule, 2 × 3 − 7 = −1. The image is (−1, 2).

(b) Reflect (3, 4) in the line y = −1. The point is 5 above the mirror (4 − (−1) = 5), so the image is 5 below it: −1 − 5 = −6. The image is (3, −6).

Example 8 · Finding the mirror line

A reflection maps P(2, 6) onto P′(6, 2). Find the mirror line.

The mirror is the perpendicular bisector of PP′ (see Constructions & Loci). The midpoint of PP′ is ((2 + 6) ÷ 2, (6 + 2) ÷ 2) = (4, 4). The gradient of PP′ is (2 − 6) ÷ (6 − 2) = −1, so the mirror has gradient +1 (perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to −1). A line of gradient 1 through (4, 4) is y − 4 = x − 4, so the mirror is y = x.

“Reflecting in y = −x gives (−x, −y)”

This is the rule for a half-turn, not for the mirror line y = −x.

The fix: reflection in y = −x swaps the coordinates and changes both signs: (x, y) → (−y, −x). Test it with a point on the mirror. The point (1, −1) lies on y = −x, so it must stay where it is. The rule (−y, −x) gives (1, −1) ✓. The wrong rule (−x, −y) would send it to (−1, 1), moving a point that is on the mirror. Points on the mirror line never move. Another check: (2, 1) goes to (−1, −2), and the midpoint (0.5, −0.5) of those two points lies on y = −x ✓.

4.Rotation: Turning About a Centre

A rotation turns a shape about a fixed point called the centre of rotation. To describe it completely you need three things: the centre, the angle and the direction (clockwise or anticlockwise). Common angles are 90° (a quarter-turn), 180° (a half-turn) and 270°. Turning 270° anticlockwise gives the same result as turning 90° clockwise.

Figure 3. The gold triangle turns 90° anticlockwise about the red centre O and lands on the image. The dashed arc shows the path of corner A.

Rules for rotations about the origin

Rotation about (0, 0)Point (x, y) maps to
90° anticlockwise(−y, x)
90° clockwise (= 270° anticlockwise)(y, −x)
180° (either direction)(−x, −y)

A quick test: the point (1, 0) sits on the positive x-axis. A quarter-turn anticlockwise takes it to the positive y-axis, (0, 1). The rule (−y, x) gives (−0, 1) = (0, 1) ✓.

Example 9 · Quarter-turn anticlockwise

Point C(3, 5) is rotated 90° anticlockwise about the origin. Use (x, y) → (−y, x): C′ = (−5, 3) = (−5, 3).

Example 10 · Clockwise and half-turn

(a) Rotate (4, −1) by 90° clockwise about O. Use (x, y) → (y, −x): the image is (−1, −4) = (−1, −4).

(b) Rotate (−3, 2) by 180° about O. Change both signs: (3, −2).

Example 11 · A centre that is not the origin

Rotate P(5, 2) by 90° anticlockwise about the centre (1, 1).

Step 1. Find the position of P relative to the centre: (5 − 1, 2 − 1) = (4, 1).

Step 2. Apply the origin rule (−y, x): (−1, 4).

Step 3. Add the centre back: (−1 + 1, 4 + 1) = (0, 5).

This three-step method (subtract the centre, rotate, add the centre) works for every rotation. A check: the distance from the centre is unchanged. Before: √(4² + 1²) = √17. After: √((0 − 1)² + (5 − 1)²) = √17 ✓.

Example 12 · Finding the centre of rotation

A rotation maps A(5, 1) onto A′(2, 4) and B(5, 3) onto B′(0, 4). Find the centre.

The centre is the same distance from a point and its image, so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of AA′ and also on the perpendicular bisector of BB′.

For AA′: midpoint (3.5, 2.5); gradient of AA′ is (4 − 1) ÷ (2 − 5) = −1, so the bisector has gradient 1: y − 2.5 = x − 3.5, which is y = x − 1.

For BB′: midpoint (2.5, 3.5); gradient of BB′ is (4 − 3) ÷ (0 − 5) = −1/5, so the bisector has gradient 5: y − 3.5 = 5(x − 2.5), which is y = 5x − 9.

Where they meet: x − 1 = 5x − 9, so 4x = 8, x = 2 and y = 1. The centre is (2, 1). The angle is 90° anticlockwise, because A is straight right of the centre, 3 units away, and A′ is straight above it, 3 units away.

“A rotation of 90° about the origin sends (x, y) to (y, x)”

This is the rule for a reflection, not a rotation.

The fix: swapping the coordinates without a sign change is a reflection in y = x. A 90° rotation also swaps the coordinates but changes exactly one sign. Anticlockwise: (x, y) → (−y, x). Clockwise: (x, y) → (y, −x). Test with (2, 1): the reflection gives (1, 2), but a quarter-turn anticlockwise gives (−1, 2), which is up and to the left, as an anticlockwise turn from the right-hand side should be.

5.Enlargement: Resizing from a Centre

An enlargement changes the size of a shape. It needs a centre of enlargement and a scale factor k. Every point of the image is on the straight line from the centre through the object point, and its distance from the centre is k times the original distance. A scale factor bigger than 1 makes the shape bigger. A scale factor between 0 and 1 (such as ½) makes it smaller, which is still called an enlargement.

Figure 4. Lines from the centre O through each corner of the small triangle meet the corners of the image. Every distance from O is multiplied by 3.

To enlarge a point from a centre that is not the origin, use the same “relative to the centre” idea as before: find the vector from the centre to the point, multiply it by k, and add it to the centre.

Example 13 · Centre at the origin

Point D(2, 3) is enlarged by scale factor 3 from the origin. Multiply each coordinate by 3: D′ = (6, 9).

Example 14 · Centre not at the origin

Point A(5, 4) is enlarged by scale factor 2 from the centre C(1, 2).

The vector from C to A is (5 − 1, 4 − 2) = (4, 2). Multiply by 2: (8, 4). Add to the centre: (1 + 8, 2 + 4) = (9, 6). Check: C(1, 2), A(5, 4) and A′(9, 6) are on one straight line, and CA′ is twice CA.

Example 15 · A fractional scale factor

(a) Enlarge (8, 6) by scale factor ½ from the origin: (4, 3). The shape shrinks towards the centre.

(b) Enlarge (8, 6) by scale factor ½ from the centre (2, 2). The vector from the centre is (6, 4), half of it is (3, 2), so the image is (2 + 3, 2 + 2) = (5, 4).

Negative scale factors

With a negative scale factor the image is on the opposite side of the centre. It is also turned upside down, as if rotated 180° about the centre, and its size is multiplied by the size of k, ignoring the sign.

Example 16 · Scale factor −2

(a) Enlarge E(4, 2) by scale factor −2 from the origin: E′ = (4 × −2, 2 × −2) = (−8, −4).

(b) Enlarge A(3, 3) by scale factor −2 from the centre (1, 2). The vector from the centre is (2, 1). Multiply by −2: (−4, −2). The image is (1 − 4, 2 − 2) = (−3, 0).

“An enlargement of scale factor −2 makes a shape twice as big on the same side of the centre”

The minus sign changes the side, not just the size.

The fix: the number part (2) tells you the image is twice as far from the centre. The minus sign tells you it is on the opposite side of the centre. So the image is twice the size, turned upside down, and on the other side. In Example 16(a), E(4, 2) is in the top-right region but E′(−8, −4) is in the bottom-left region, twice as far from the origin.

Example 17 · Finding the centre and the scale factor

A triangle has corners A(1, 1) and B(3, 1). After an enlargement A maps to A′(3, 2) and B maps to B′(7, 2). Find the centre and the scale factor.

Scale factor: compare lengths. AB = 2 and A′B′ = 4, so k = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 (positive, because the image is the same way up and the corners have moved away in the same direction).

Centre: draw the line through A and A′ and the line through B and B′. The centre is where they meet. Line AA′ has gradient (2 − 1) ÷ (3 − 1) = ½, so y − 1 = ½(x − 1). Line BB′ has gradient (2 − 1) ÷ (7 − 3) = ¼, so y − 1 = ¼(x − 3). Setting these equal: ½(x − 1) = ¼(x − 3), so 2x − 2 = x − 3, giving x = −1 and then y = 1 + ½(−2) = 0. The centre is (−1, 0). Check: from (−1, 0) the vector to A is (2, 1); doubled it is (4, 2); and (−1 + 4, 0 + 2) = (3, 2) = A′ ✓.

Lengths, perimeters and areas

If the scale factor is k, then every length (including the perimeter) is multiplied by k, and every area is multiplied by k². (A negative k behaves the same way: use its size.)

Example 18 · Area scale factor

A triangle has area 12 cm² and perimeter 20 cm. It is enlarged by scale factor 3.

New perimeter = 20 × 3 = 60 cm. New area = 12 × 3² = 12 × 9 = 108 cm². If instead the scale factor is ½, the new area is 12 × ¼ = 3 cm².

“Enlarging by scale factor 3 makes the area 3 times bigger”

A length triples, but an area has two dimensions.

The fix: picture a 2 cm by 2 cm square (area 4 cm²). With scale factor 3 it becomes 6 cm by 6 cm, which has area 36 cm². That is 9 times as large, and 9 = 3². Both the length and the width are tripled, so the area is multiplied by 3 × 3. (For volumes, the factor is k³. See Area & Volume.)

6.Describing a Single Transformation Fully

Exam questions often say “Describe fully the single transformation that maps shape A onto shape B”. “Fully” means every detail in the table below. Missing one detail loses marks, even when the type of transformation is right.

TypeDetails you must give
TranslationThe column vector
ReflectionThe equation of the mirror line
RotationThe centre, the angle and the direction
EnlargementThe centre and the scale factor

How to decide which one it is. Ask three quick questions:

  1. Is the image the same size? If not, it is an enlargement.
  2. Is it turned or flipped? If the shape faces the same way (a slide), it is a translation.
  3. Is the order of the labels reversed? If A, B, C go clockwise on the object but A′, B′, C′ go anticlockwise on the image, it is a reflection. If the order is kept but the shape has turned, it is a rotation.
Exam Tip

Write “rotation, 90° anticlockwise, centre (2, 1)”, not “turned 90°”. Mark schemes award one mark for the name of the transformation and one mark for each detail. Always name the transformation first.

7.Combining Transformations

When one transformation is followed by another, the result is the image of the image. The order matters. A few combinations always simplify:

  • Two translations make one translation: add the vectors (Example 4).
  • Two reflections in parallel mirror lines make a translation. It is perpendicular to the mirrors and twice the distance between them.
  • Two reflections in lines that cross make a rotation about the crossing point, through twice the angle between the lines.
Example 19 · Reflections in parallel lines

Reflect the point (0, 2) in the line x = 1 and then in the line x = 4.

First: 2 × 1 − 0 = 2, giving (2, 2). Second: 2 × 4 − 2 = 6, giving (6, 2). The mirrors are 3 apart, and the point moved 2 × 3 = 6 to the right. So the pair of reflections is a translation by 60.

Example 20 · Reflections in crossing lines

Reflect (3, 2) in the x-axis and then in the y-axis. First (3, −2), then (−3, −2). This is a rotation of 180° about the origin (the crossing point), and the angle between the axes is 90°, so twice the angle is 180° ✓.

Another pair: reflect (3, 1) in the x-axis, giving (3, −1), then in the line y = x, giving (−1, 3). The mirror lines cross at the origin at 45°, so the result is a rotation of 90° about the origin. Anticlockwise rotation by 90° of (3, 1) is (−1, 3) ✓.

Example 21 · The order matters

Start with the point (2, 1). Let R be “rotate 90° anticlockwise about O” and X be “reflect in the x-axis”.

R then X: R gives (−1, 2); X gives (−1, −2).

X then R: X gives (2, −1); R gives (1, 2).

The answers differ, so doing the transformations in the other order gives a different image. Also try the translation example in the exam questions below.

8.Invariant Points

A point is invariant under a transformation if it maps onto itself. Finding invariant points is a quick way to find a centre or a mirror line.

TransformationInvariant points
TranslationNone (unless the vector is zero)
ReflectionEvery point on the mirror line
RotationOnly the centre of rotation
Enlargement (k ≠ 1)Only the centre of enlargement

A neat link: an enlargement with scale factor −1 is the same as a rotation of 180° about the same centre. For the triangle in the explorer above, both give the same image.

9.Transformations and Matrices

A point (x, y) can be written as a column xy, and a 2 × 2 matrix can act on it. Several transformations about the origin have simple matrices:

Transformation about OMatrixEffect on (x, y)
Reflection in the x-axis(1 0 / 0 −1)(x, −y)
Reflection in y = x(0 1 / 1 0)(y, x)
Rotation 90° anticlockwise(0 −1 / 1 0)(−y, x)
Enlargement, scale factor k(k 0 / 0 k)(kx, ky)

Each matrix is written here as its first row, a slash, then its second row. Example: apply the rotation matrix to (2, 3). First row: 0 × 2 + (−1) × 3 = −3. Second row: 1 × 2 + 0 × 3 = 2. The image is (−3, 2), the same as the rule (−y, x). The page on Matrices shows how to multiply them and how combining two transformations corresponds to multiplying two matrices.

10.Transformations in Real Life

  • Translation: a sliding door, a lift moving up a shaft, a conveyor belt and a game piece moving across a board all move without turning. Repeating patterns on tiles, fabrics and carpets are often made by translating one motif again and again.
  • Reflection: the word AMBULANCE is written backwards on the front of an ambulance so that a driver looking in a rear-view mirror reads it the right way round. A plane mirror forms an image as far behind the mirror as you are in front of it. If you stand 2 m from a mirror, your image appears 2 m behind it, which is 4 m from you.
  • Rotation: the minute hand of a clock turns 360° in 60 minutes, which is 6° every minute. In 15 minutes it makes a 90° clockwise rotation about the centre of the dial, and in 40 minutes it turns 40 × 6 = 240° clockwise. A fan with three equal blades looks the same after a turn of 360° ÷ 3 = 120°.
  • Enlargement: maps and scale drawings. On a map with scale 1 : 50 000, a road measuring 3.2 cm on the map is 3.2 × 50 000 = 160 000 cm = 1.6 km in real life. A photocopier set to “141%” turns A4 paper (210 mm by 297 mm) into A3 (297 mm by 420 mm): 297 ÷ 210 ≈ 1.414 and 420 ÷ 297 ≈ 1.414. The area of A3 is twice the area of A4, matching 1.414² ≈ 2.
Example 22 · A school banner

A photograph 10 cm by 15 cm is enlarged to make a banner using scale factor 12. The banner is 10 × 12 = 120 cm by 15 × 12 = 180 cm. The photo has area 150 cm². The banner has area 150 × 12² = 150 × 144 = 21 600 cm², which is 2.16 m². The photograph is not simply “12 times bigger”: the banner covers 144 times the area, so it needs 144 times as much ink.

11.Common Mistakes

  • Not giving all the details. A rotation needs centre, angle and direction. A reflection needs the equation of the mirror line, not just “reflection”. An enlargement needs centre and scale factor.
  • Writing a vector as (x, y) pairs in the wrong order or with wrong signs. The top number is across (x), the bottom number is up or down (y).
  • Mixing up rotation and reflection rules. Both can swap coordinates; only the signs tell them apart.
  • Forgetting the centre. The origin rules apply only when the centre is (0, 0). For another centre, subtract the centre first and add it back at the end.
  • Measuring from the wrong place in an enlargement. Distances are measured from the centre of enlargement, not from the nearest corner of the shape.
  • Treating a negative scale factor like a positive one. The image lands on the opposite side of the centre.
  • Cubing or squaring the wrong way. Lengths scale by k, areas by k², volumes by k³.
  • Mixing up object and image. The prime marks the image. A′ is where A goes.

12.Try It Yourself

  1. Translate the point (−2, 5) by 4−6.
  2. Rotate the point (2, 5) by 90° clockwise about the origin.
  3. Enlarge the point (−4, 6) by scale factor ½ from the origin.
Show answers

1. (−2 + 4, 5 + (−6)) = (2, −1).

2. The clockwise rule is (x, y) → (y, −x), so (2, 5) → (5, −2).

3. Half of each coordinate: (−2, 3). The answer is (−2, 3).

13.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] Point P(−3, 4) is translated by 6−7. Find P′.
  2. [3 marks] Find the image of the point (1, 6) after reflection in the line y = 2.
  3. [3 marks] Triangle T has corners (1, 1), (4, 1) and (1, 3). Triangle U has corners (−1, 1), (−1, 4) and (−3, 1). Describe fully the single transformation that maps T onto U.
  4. [3 marks] Find the image of the point (3, 4) under an enlargement with scale factor 3 and centre (1, 2).
  5. [4 marks] Triangle V has corners (1, 1), (3, 1) and (1, 2). It is enlarged with scale factor −2 and centre the origin. (a) Find the corners of the image. (b) Find the ratio of the area of the image to the area of V.
  6. [5 marks] The point A(2, 1) is reflected in the y-axis and then translated by 34. (a) Find the final image. (b) Is the result the same if the translation is done first and the reflection second? Show your working.
Show answers

1. (−3 + 6, 4 − 7) = (3, −3).

2. The point is 6 − 2 = 4 above the mirror, so the image is 4 below it: 2 − 4 = −2. Image (1, −2).

3. T(1, 1) → U(−1, 1), (4, 1) → (−1, 4) and (1, 3) → (−3, 1). The rule (x, y) → (−y, x) fits all three, so it is a rotation of 90° anticlockwise about the origin (0, 0).

4. The vector from the centre is (3 − 1, 4 − 2) = (2, 2). Multiply by 3: (6, 6). Add the centre: (7, 8).

5. (a) Multiply each coordinate by −2: (−2, −2), (−6, −2) and (−2, −4). (b) The base of V is 2 and its height is 1, so its area is ½ × 2 × 1 = 1. The base of the image is 4 and its height is 2, so its area is ½ × 4 × 2 = 4. The ratio is 4 : 1 (the same as (−2)² = 4).

6. (a) Reflection in the y-axis: (−2, 1). Translation: (−2 + 3, 1 + 4) = (1, 5). (b) Translation first: (2 + 3, 1 + 4) = (5, 5). Then reflection in the y-axis: (−5, 5). This is not the same as (1, 5), so the order matters.

14.Key Points

  • Object → image. The prime mark shows the image: A → A′.
  • Translation, reflection and rotation keep size and shape (congruent). Enlargement keeps shape but changes size (similar).
  • Describe fully: translation = vector; reflection = mirror line equation; rotation = centre, angle, direction; enlargement = centre and scale factor.
  • Origin rules: reflect in x-axis (x, −y); y-axis (−x, y); y = x (y, x); rotate 90° anticlockwise (−y, x); rotate 180° (−x, −y); enlarge (kx, ky).
  • For another centre: subtract the centre, apply the rule, add the centre back.
  • Lengths scale by k, areas by k², volumes by k³. A negative k puts the image on the opposite side of the centre.
  • Invariant points: mirror line (reflection); centre (rotation, enlargement); none for translation.
  • Order matters when transformations are combined.

15.For Teachers and Parents

Transformations are best learned with the hands first. Cut a triangle out of paper (mark one corner with a dot so you can tell which way it faces), slide it, flip it and turn it on a sheet of squared paper, and then record each move with coordinates. Tracing paper is a good way to see rotations and to find a centre of rotation by pushing a pencil point through it. Ask learners to predict the image before they calculate, and to check their answer with a distance test: in a reflection each point and its image are the same distance from the mirror, in a rotation both are the same distance from the centre. When a student loses marks, it is almost always because a detail was left out of the description, so practise naming all the required details aloud.
