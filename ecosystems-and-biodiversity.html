Biology · Ecology & Biodiversity

Ecosystems & Biodiversity

How organisms interact with each other and their environment, and why biodiversity matters at genetic, species and ecosystem levels.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|Biology|In-depth learning article|Last updated: October 2026

No living thing lives alone. A frog needs insects to eat, plants for those insects, water to breed in and a temperature it can tolerate. Ecology is the study of these connections. This article explains how ecosystems work, how energy and materials move through them, why variety of life (biodiversity) matters, and what threatens and protects it.

Written for students up to O-Level (grades 8 to 10), and useful for parents and teachers. General education.

1.What Is an Ecosystem?

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms together with the non-living parts of their surroundings, interacting as a system. A pond, a forest, a desert, a coral reef and even a school garden can each be studied as an ecosystem. Ecosystems have no sealed walls: energy, water, nutrients and animals move in and out.

TermMeaning
HabitatThe place where an organism lives
PopulationAll the organisms of one species in an area
CommunityAll the populations living together in an area
EcosystemThe community plus its non-living environment
BiosphereAll the parts of Earth where life exists

2.Biotic and Abiotic Factors

Biotic factors are living influences: predators, food, competitors, disease and partners. Abiotic factors are non-living: temperature, light, water, pH, salinity, soil type and oxygen. Each species has a range of conditions it can tolerate. Outside that range it survives poorly or not at all, which is why different organisms are found in different places.

Worked example: estimating a population with quadrats

A student wants to estimate how many daisies grow on a 400 m² lawn. She places 10 random quadrats (each 1 m²) and counts 3, 5, 2, 4, 6, 3, 5, 4, 2, 6 daisies.

  1. Total count: 3+5+2+4+6+3+5+4+2+6 = 40.
  2. Mean per quadrat: 40 ÷ 10 = 4 daisies per m².
  3. Scale up: 4 × 400 = about 1600 daisies.

This is an estimate. More quadrats, placed randomly, make it more reliable. Random placement avoids choosing only the crowded or the sparse patches.

3.Energy Flow

Nearly all ecosystems are powered by sunlight. Producers (plants, algae and some bacteria) capture light energy by photosynthesis and store it in chemical form. Consumers get energy by eating: primary consumers eat producers, secondary consumers eat primary consumers, and so on. Each feeding step is a trophic level.

Energy flows in one direction: it enters as light and leaves as heat. At each step most of the energy is not passed on. Some is used by the organism in respiration (movement, keeping warm, growth), some is lost as heat, and some parts, such as bones, are not eaten or digested. Usually only about 10% passes to the next level (the real figure varies, often between 5% and 20%). This is why food chains rarely have more than four or five links, and why there are far fewer lions than zebras.

Figure 1. Original EDUSAMBAM energy pyramid. The numbers are practice values using the 10% rule, not measurements. The Sun pulses.
Worked example: energy transfer efficiency

A field of grass takes in 20 000 kJ of energy. Rabbits eat the grass and gain 2000 kJ. Foxes eat the rabbits and gain 180 kJ.

  1. Producer to rabbit: 2000 ÷ 20 000 × 100 = 10%.
  2. Rabbit to fox: 180 ÷ 2000 × 100 = 9%.
  3. Energy lost from the fox's meal: 2000 − 180 = 1820 kJ, mostly as heat and in waste.

The numbers are practice values, but the pattern (about a tenth passed on) is typical.

4.Food Chains and Food Webs

A food chain shows who eats whom in a single line, for example grass → grasshopper → frog → hawk. Arrows point in the direction of energy flow, from food to eater. Real feeding relationships are tangled, so scientists draw food webs.

Figure 2. A small food web (original diagram). The grass is the producer. Every organism, when it dies, feeds the decomposers.

In a web, losing one species can affect many others. If the frog disappears, the grasshopper population may grow and eat more grass, while the hawk depends more on rabbits. A species that has an effect much larger than its numbers suggest is called a keystone species; for example, sea otters that eat sea urchins help keep kelp forests healthy in some coastal ecosystems.

For a closer look at feeding relationships and how energy moves along them, see our article on food chains, food webs and energy flow.

5.Decomposers and Recycling

Energy flows through an ecosystem, but matter is recycled. Decomposers, mainly bacteria and fungi, break down dead organisms, waste and fallen leaves, releasing nutrients (such as nitrates and phosphates) back into the soil and carbon dioxide into the air. Without decomposers, dead material would pile up and nutrients would stay locked away.

6.The Carbon Cycle

Carbon moves between air, living things and the soil. Plants take carbon dioxide from the air by photosynthesis and build it into sugars, proteins and other molecules. Animals get carbon by eating. All living things release carbon dioxide by respiration, and decomposers do so when they break down dead matter. Burning fuels and wood also adds carbon dioxide to the air.

Figure 3. A simplified carbon cycle. The gold dot shows carbon moving from air to plants by photosynthesis.

Fossil fuels are the remains of ancient organisms; burning them returns carbon that was locked away for millions of years. The extra carbon dioxide is increasing the greenhouse effect and contributing to climate change, a consequence studied in more detail in physical-science and geography courses.

7.The Water and Nitrogen Cycles

In the water cycle, the Sun's heat evaporates water, plants release water vapour by transpiration, vapour condenses to form clouds, and rain or snow returns water to land and sea.

Nitrogen makes up about 78% of the air, but most organisms cannot use nitrogen gas. In the nitrogen cycle, some bacteria (nitrogen-fixing bacteria, for example in root nodules of peas and beans) convert nitrogen gas into forms plants can use. Decomposers release ammonium from dead matter; nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrates, which plants absorb and use to make proteins. Denitrifying bacteria return some nitrogen to the air. Farmers use crop rotation with legumes and fertilisers to supply nitrogen to soil.

InteractionWhat happensExample

9.Population Growth and Carrying Capacity

A population grows when births and immigration exceed deaths and emigration. When resources are plentiful a population can grow quickly, but eventually limits such as food, space, disease and predators slow growth. The largest population an environment can support over time is its carrying capacity.

10.What Is Biodiversity?

Biodiversity is the variety of life. It is described at three levels: genetic diversity (variation within a species), species diversity (the number and abundance of different species) and ecosystem diversity (variety of habitats). Tropical rainforests and coral reefs have especially high species diversity.

Counting species gives species richness. A more informative measure also considers how evenly individuals are spread among species. Two fields may both have 3 species, but one with 98 individuals of one species and 1 each of the others is less diverse than one with about 33 of each.

11.Why Biodiversity Matters

  • Ecosystem services: pollination, clean water, soil fertility, flood control and carbon storage.
  • Resilience: varied ecosystems and genetic diversity help populations cope with disease and change; a crop with no genetic variety can be wiped out by one disease.
  • Resources: food, timber, fibres and many medicines originated from wild organisms.
  • Cultural and ethical value: many people value nature for its own sake and for recreation and heritage.

12.Threats to Biodiversity

ThreatExplanation
Habitat loss and fragmentationClearing forest, draining wetlands and building roads destroy or split habitats; this is considered the greatest threat on land.
Over-exploitationHunting, fishing or harvesting faster than a population can recover.
PollutionFertiliser runoff, plastics, pesticides and oil harm organisms. Fertiliser runoff can cause eutrophication: algae bloom, then decomposers use up oxygen and fish die.
Invasive speciesNon-native species can out-compete or prey on native species.
Climate changeChanging temperatures and rainfall alter where species can live.

13.Conservation and Sustainable Use

  • Protected areas such as national parks and marine reserves.
  • Sustainable use: fishing quotas, selective logging and replanting.
  • Captive breeding and seed banks to protect threatened species and varieties.
  • Restoring habitats, such as replanting mangroves or wetlands.
  • Laws and agreements controlling trade in endangered species.
  • Individual action: using less energy and water, cutting waste, and choosing responsibly sourced products.
Real-world connection

Local ecosystems matter to local people. Mangroves protect coastlines from storm waves and act as nurseries for young fish, so protecting them can support both biodiversity and local fishing communities.

14.Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Drawing food-chain arrows from the eater to the food. Arrows point from the food to the eater.
  • Saying energy is recycled. Energy flows one way and is lost as heat; matter is recycled.
  • Confusing a population (one species) with a community (all species).
  • Saying the 10% energy loss means the rest is destroyed. It is mostly released as heat or left in uneaten or undigested parts.
  • Describing biodiversity only as 'number of animals'. It includes plants, microbes and genetic variety.

15.Summary

  • Ecosystems combine living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts.
  • Energy flows from the Sun through producers and consumers and is lost as heat; only about 10% passes on at each step.
  • Matter is recycled by decomposers in the carbon, nitrogen and water cycles.
  • Populations grow until limited by factors such as food, space, disease and predators (carrying capacity).
  • Biodiversity includes genetic, species and ecosystem diversity, and provides services humans rely on.
  • Habitat loss, over-exploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change threaten it; conservation and sustainable use help.

16.Check Your Understanding

1. Why are there usually fewer top predators than herbivores?
Answer: Only about 10% of energy passes to each next level, so less energy is available to support animals at higher levels.
2. Estimate the number of plants in a 600 m² area if the mean count is 3 per m².
Answer: 3 × 600 = 1800 plants (an estimate).
3. Explain the role of decomposers.
Answer: They break down dead matter and waste, releasing nutrients and carbon dioxide so materials are recycled.
4. Describe how fertiliser runoff can kill fish in a pond.
Answer: Nutrients cause algae to bloom; when algae die, decomposers use up oxygen as they break them down, so fish cannot get enough oxygen.
5. Give the difference between energy flow and nutrient cycling.
Answer: Energy flows one way (Sun to heat). Nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen are recycled.
6. Name two reasons biodiversity matters to people.
Answer: For example: pollination and clean water (ecosystem services), and resilience of crops or food sources against disease.

17.For Teachers and Parents

Notes for adults
Suggested use. Start with the schoolyard or a nearby park: list the organisms seen and sketch a food chain. Activity. A quadrat survey with a square of string (1 m by 1 m) and a count of a common plant is a safe and quick fieldwork task. Teaching note. The 10% figure is a rough average, so it is presented as a typical value. Climate change is introduced briefly as one ecological pressure. Local examples make ecology more meaningful.
