Collocations in English: Word Partners, Examples and Rules

Heavy rain, make a decision, deeply grateful. Learn which words go together in English, with hundreds of examples, tables, common mistakes and a quiz.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 24 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Vocabulary

English speakers say heavy rain, not strong rain; they make a mistake, not do a mistake; they take a photograph, not make a photograph. These natural word partnerships are called collocations. Learning them is one of the quickest ways to make your English sound fluent and natural.

This guide explains what collocations are, why they matter in speaking and writing, and the main types. Its main part gives 259 collocations with example sentences, grouped by type: verb collocations (make, do, take, have and others), academic collocations, adjective and adverb partners, unit expressions such as a flash of lightning, and word-plus-preposition pairs. It also includes common mistakes, key points, ten practice questions and a teacher activity.

It pairs naturally with Synonyms in English, Idioms in English and Phrasal Verbs, and connects with Common Errors in English. Spelling in this guide follows British English.

1.What Is a Collocation?

A collocation is a combination of words that naturally go together. The word comes from the Latin collocare, meaning "to place together". The linguist J. R. Firth made the idea famous with his remark that you shall know a word by the company it keeps.

Collocations are not exact rules of grammar. They are habits of the language, and a wrong collocation, such as strong rain, is understandable but sounds unnatural to a native speaker.

Collocations, idioms and phrasal verbs

TypeMeaningExample
CollocationWords that often go together; the meaning of each word is clearmake a decision, heavy rain
IdiomA fixed phrase whose meaning is not clear from the wordsspill the beans
Phrasal verbA verb plus a particle that forms a new meaninggive up
Free combinationWords that can be freely joinedread a book, red car

2.Why Collocations Matter

  • Natural English. Correct collocations make your speech and writing sound fluent.
  • Better marks. Examiners reward accurate and varied collocations in essays and speaking tests.
  • Faster reading and speaking. You learn chunks of language instead of single words.
  • Fewer errors. Many mistakes come from translating word by word from another language.

3.The Main Types of Collocation

TypePatternExamples
Verb + nounverb + objectmake a decision, take a break, pay attention
Adjective + nounadjective + nounheavy traffic, strong tea, high price

Noun + noun (unit)noun + of + nouna piece of advice, a flash of lightning
Word + prepositionverb, adjective or noun + prepositiondepend on, good at, reason for

4.Strong, Medium and Weak Collocations

  • Strong collocations are almost fixed: make a mistake, heavy rain, take place. A native speaker would never change them.
  • Medium collocations are very natural but allow some alternatives: heavy traffic or dense traffic.
  • Weak collocations are easily changed: a big house or a large house.

18.How to Learn Collocations

  • Learn words in chunks. Write make a decision, not just decision.
  • Use a collocations dictionary, or the example sentences in a learner's dictionary.
  • Notice partners while reading and highlight them.
  • Group by key words. Make a page for make, do, take and have.
  • Record in sentences, then reuse them in your own writing within a week.
  • Do not translate word by word. Check each new combination in a dictionary.
Key points to remember
  • A collocation is a natural combination of words that often go together.
  • Learn words in chunks, not alone.
  • Use make with mistake, decision, effort and progress; do with homework, research and damage; take with a break, a photograph and a risk.
  • Adjectives and adverbs are choosy: heavy rain, strong tea, deeply grateful.
  • Learn the preposition with the word: good at, depend on, reason for.
  • Do not translate collocations word by word from another language.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students ten verbs (make, do, take, have, give, keep, pay, break, catch, hold) and ask them to match each with five nouns from a list, then to write one sentence for each. For older students, ask them to find five academic collocations in a news article and to use them in a short paragraph.

Please note: this guide uses British English. Some collocations differ in American English, for example have a bath (British) and take a bath (American), and take a decision (British) alongside make a decision (more common).
