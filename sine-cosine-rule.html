Sine Rule, Cosine Rule & Area of a Triangle

Solve any triangle, not just right-angled ones: choose between the sine and cosine rules, handle the ambiguous case, find areas with ½ab sin C and Heron’s formula, and apply it all to bearings and heights.

SOH-CAH-TOA works only when a triangle has a right angle. Most triangles in real life do not: a roof truss, a field with three corners, the path of a ship that changes course. For these we use two tools that work in every triangle, the sine rule and the cosine rule, and a third formula for the area. This guide explains where each rule comes from, which one to choose, how to handle the one awkward case (when the sine rule gives two possible triangles), and how to use all three tools on real problems with bearings and heights.

1.Labelling a Triangle

For these rules every triangle is labelled in the same way. The corners (and angles) are A, B and C. The side opposite each angle gets the matching small letter: side a is opposite angle A, side b is opposite angle B, and side c is opposite angle C. Getting this pairing right is the most important habit in this topic.

Figure 1. Each side is named after the angle opposite it. Side a is across from A, and so on.

We also name the kinds of information a question can give:

  • SSS: all three sides.
  • SAS: two sides and the angle between them (the included angle).
  • ASA or AAS: two angles and one side.
  • SSA: two sides and an angle that is not between them.

Always remember that the three angles of a triangle add up to 180°. If you know two angles, you know the third.

A quick test: look for a matching pair of a side and its opposite angle, both known. If you have one, the sine rule is usually the easier route. If you do not have one, use the cosine rule.

3.The Sine Rule

In any triangle ABC, each side divided by the sine of its opposite angle gives the same number:

a ÷ sin A = b ÷ sin B = c ÷ sin C

To find an angle, it is easier to write the rule upside down: sin A ÷ a = sin B ÷ b = sin C ÷ c. You only ever use two of the three parts at a time.

Why it is true. Draw the height h from corner C down to side AB. In the right-angled triangle on the left, sin A = h ÷ b, so h = b sin A. In the one on the right, sin B = h ÷ a, so h = a sin B. The two expressions for h are equal: b sin A = a sin B. Divide both sides by sin A sin B to get a ÷ sin A = b ÷ sin B. The same argument works for the other pair of sides.

Example 1 · Finding a side

In triangle ABC, A = 40°, B = 65° and a = 8 cm. Find b and c.

The matching pair is a and A. For b: b ÷ sin 65° = 8 ÷ sin 40°, so b = 8 × sin 65° ÷ sin 40° = 8 × 0.9063 ÷ 0.6428 = 11.3 cm.

For c, first find C = 180° − 40° − 65° = 75°. Then c = 8 × sin 75° ÷ sin 40° = 12.0 cm.

Example 2 · Finding an angle

In triangle ABC, a = 7 cm, b = 9 cm and B = 72°. Find A.

sin A ÷ 7 = sin 72° ÷ 9, so sin A = 7 × sin 72° ÷ 9 = 7 × 0.9511 ÷ 9 = 0.7397. Then A = sin⁻¹(0.7397) = 47.7°.

The other value your calculator’s inverse sine hides is 180° − 47.7° = 132.3°. That would make A + B = 132.3° + 72° = 204.3°, more than 180°, so it is impossible. Here there is only one answer.

“My calculator says sin 30 = −0.988, but I used it anyway, because I trust the calculator.”

What should sin 30 be?

The fix: an angle given in degrees needs the calculator in degree (DEG) mode. In radian mode the calculator treats 30 as 30 radians, and sin 30 comes out as −0.988 instead of 0.5. Test your calculator every time: sin 30 must give 0.5. Also keep full calculator values until the last step, rounding only the final answer (3 significant figures for lengths, 1 decimal place for angles), or your answer can drift.

4.The Ambiguous Case

Suppose you are given two sides and an angle that is not between them (SSA). Because sin θ = sin(180° − θ), the sine rule can give two angles, one acute and one obtuse. Sometimes both make a real triangle.

Figure 3. A = 30°, AC = 8 cm and BC = 5 cm. The 5 cm side can swing to either of two positions on the base line.
Example 3 · Two triangles

A = 30°, a = 5 cm (opposite A) and b = 8 cm. Find the possible values of B.

sin B ÷ 8 = sin 30° ÷ 5, so sin B = 8 × 0.5 ÷ 5 = 0.8. Then B = 53.1° or B = 180° − 53.1° = 126.9°.

Check each. If B = 53.1°, then C = 180° − 30° − 53.1° = 96.9°. If B = 126.9°, then C = 180° − 30° − 126.9° = 23.1°. Both are positive, so both triangles exist. Their third sides are c = 5 × sin 96.9° ÷ sin 30° = 9.93 cm and c = 5 × sin 23.1° ÷ sin 30° = 3.93 cm.

How to know in advance

Take A as the known angle, a as the side opposite it, and b as the other known side. When A is acute:

ConditionNumber of triangles
a ≥ bOne
b sin A < a < bTwo
a = b sin AOne (a right angle at B)
a < b sin ANone: side a is too short to reach the base

If A is a right angle or obtuse, there is one triangle when a > b and none otherwise. In Example 3, b sin A = 8 × 0.5 = 4 cm, and 4 < 5 < 8, so there are two triangles, which matches the work above. In Example 2, the angle you are looking for (A) is opposite the shorter side, which must be acute, so there was only one answer.

“sin B = 0.8, so B = 53.1°. That’s the answer.”

Was the second value checked?

The fix: your calculator’s sin⁻¹ gives only the acute angle. Whenever you find an angle by the sine rule from SSA data, also test 180° − (calculator angle). Keep it if the three angles can still add to 180°. In Example 3, 126.9° works, so both answers are required.

5.The Cosine Rule

The cosine rule links all three sides with one angle. For the angle A opposite side a:

a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A

Rearranged to find an angle: cos A = (b² + c² − a²) ÷ (2bc). The same rule works for any corner, for example b² = a² + c² − 2ac cos B. The side on the left is always the one opposite the angle in the formula.

Link to Pythagoras. If A = 90°, then cos A = 0 and the rule becomes a² = b² + c², Pythagoras’ theorem. The term −2bc cos A is a correction that makes the rule work when the angle is not 90°. For an obtuse angle, cos A is negative, so the correction is added and the side becomes longer.

Figure 4. SAS: sides 6 cm and 9 cm with the 55° angle between them. The cosine rule finds the third side.
Example 4 · SAS, finding a side

b = 6 cm, c = 9 cm and A = 55°. Find a.

a² = 6² + 9² − 2 × 6 × 9 × cos 55° = 36 + 81 − 108 × 0.5736 = 117 − 61.95 = 55.05. So a = √55.05 = 7.42 cm.

Example 5 · SSS, finding every angle

A triangle has sides a = 8 cm, b = 10 cm and c = 13 cm. Find all the angles.

Start with the largest angle, which is opposite the longest side: cos C = (8² + 10² − 13²) ÷ (2 × 8 × 10) = (64 + 100 − 169) ÷ 160 = −0.03125. So C = cos⁻¹(−0.03125) = 91.8°. A negative cosine tells you the angle is obtuse.

Now use the sine rule for a second angle, choosing the one opposite the shorter side, which is certain to be acute: sin A = 8 × sin 91.8° ÷ 13 = 0.6151, so A = 38.0°. Then B = 180° − 91.8° − 38.0° = 50.3°.

A useful habit. After the cosine rule finds one angle, find the next angle with the sine rule only if the angle you want is opposite a shorter side. Otherwise use the cosine rule again, to avoid the ambiguous case altogether.

“a² = 6² + 9² + 2 × 6 × 9 × cos 55°, because both sides are added.”

Look at the sign in front of the last term.

The fix: the cosine rule has a minus: a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A. With a plus sign you would get 36 + 81 + 61.95 = 178.95 and a = 13.4 cm. A quick sense check shows this is wrong: the angle 55° is less than 90°, so a must be shorter than the Pythagoras length √(6² + 9²) = 10.8 cm. The correct working gives 7.42 cm. Also work out 2bc cos A first, and subtract the whole of it from b² + c².

6.The Area of a Triangle

You already know area = ½ × base × height. If the height is not given, you can find it from an angle. In the triangle in Figure 5, the height is h = b sin C, so the area is

Area = ½ab sin C

where C is the angle between sides a and b. The letters can change: ½bc sin A and ½ac sin B are equally correct, as long as the angle is included between the two sides used.

Figure 5. The height of the triangle is h = b sin C, so the area ½ah becomes ½ab sin C.
Example 6 · Area, and an angle from the area

(a) Two sides of 7 cm and 10 cm enclose an angle of 48°. Area = ½ × 7 × 10 × sin 48° = 35 × 0.7431 = 26.0 cm².

(b) A triangle has area 18 cm² and two sides of 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the angle between them. ½ × 6 × 8 × sin C = 18, so 24 sin C = 18 and sin C = 0.75. Then C = 48.6° or 131.4°. Both are possible, since the area does not say whether the angle is acute or obtuse.

When you know all three sides: Heron’s formula

Let s be half the perimeter, s = (a + b + c) ÷ 2. Then Area = √[s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)].

Example 7 · Heron’s formula

A triangle has sides 5 cm, 6 cm and 7 cm. s = 9, so area = √(9 × 4 × 3 × 2) = √216 = 14.7 cm². Check with the other formula: the angle between the 5 cm and 6 cm sides has cos C = (25 + 36 − 49) ÷ 60 = 0.2, so sin C = 0.9798 and area = ½ × 5 × 6 × 0.9798 = 14.7 cm². The two methods agree.

A parallelogram with adjacent sides a and b and the angle θ between them has area ab sin θ, because a diagonal cuts it into two identical triangles.

7.Using the Rules in Real Problems

Example 8 · A ship and bearings

A ship sails 12 km on a bearing of 040°, then 9 km on a bearing of 120°. How far is it from its start, and what is the bearing of the finish from the start?

At the turning point B, the first leg arrived on a bearing of 040°, so the direction back to the start is 220°. The new course is 120°. The angle inside the triangle at B is 220° − 120° = 100° (equivalently, 180° − (120° − 40°)).

Distance: d² = 12² + 9² − 2 × 12 × 9 × cos 100° = 144 + 81 + 37.51 = 262.51 (cos 100° is negative, so the correction adds). So d = 16.2 km.

Angle at the start: sin A ÷ 9 = sin 100° ÷ 16.2, so sin A = 9 × 0.9848 ÷ 16.2 = 0.5471 and A = 33.1°. The ship turned right, so the bearing of the finish from the start is 040° + 33.1° = 073.1°.

Example 9 · The height of a tower

From a point A on level ground, the angle of elevation of the top T of a tower is 25°. After walking 50 m straight towards the tower, to a point B, the elevation is 40°. Find the height of the tower.

In triangle ABT, the angle at A is 25°. The angle at B inside the triangle is 180° − 40° = 140°, so the angle at T is 180° − 25° − 140° = 15°.

By the sine rule, BT ÷ sin 25° = 50 ÷ sin 15°, so BT = 50 × sin 25° ÷ sin 15° = 81.65 m. In the right-angled triangle at the foot of the tower, height = BT × sin 40° = 81.65 × 0.6428 = 52.5 m.

This is a common exam pattern: use a general triangle rule to find one length, then use right-angled trigonometry to finish. The ideas extend to three dimensions, which you can practise in Bearings & 3D Trigonometry.

8.Where These Rules Are Used

  • Surveying. A surveyor measures one baseline and two angles to a faraway point, then uses the sine rule to find its distance without crossing the river or ravine. This method is called triangulation.
  • Navigation. Ship and aircraft paths with changes of course form triangles, as in Example 8.
  • Building and design. Roof trusses, bridge frames and cranes are made of triangles whose lengths and angles must be calculated.
  • Land area. A plot with three straight sides can have its area found from two measured sides and the angle between them.

9.A Short History

The cosine rule first appears in a geometric form in Euclid’s Elements (Book II, Propositions 12 and 13, around 300 BCE), described in terms of areas of squares rather than cosines. In France it is sometimes called al-Kashi’s theorem, after the Persian astronomer Jamshid al-Kashi, who worked in the early 15th century and used it in a form suited to calculation. The sine rule was developed by astronomers: Indian and later Islamic mathematicians worked with sines of angles, and the 13th-century scholar Nasir al-Din al-Tusi is often credited with a clear statement of the law of sines for plane triangles. Heron’s formula for area is named after Heron of Alexandria and appears in his book Metrica, written around the 1st century CE. Dates in this period are sometimes approximate, and historians debate who first discovered each result.

Did You Know?

The common value of a ÷ sin A, b ÷ sin B and c ÷ sin C is the diameter of the circle that passes through all three corners (the circumcircle). Check it on a 3-4-5 right-angled triangle: 5 ÷ sin 90° = 5, and the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle is a diameter of its circumcircle.

10.Common Mistakes

  • Calculator in radian mode. Check that sin 30 gives 0.5.
  • Using the sine rule when you have SAS. There is no matching pair, so use the cosine rule.
  • Mismatching a side with the wrong angle. The angle must be opposite the side.
  • Forgetting the second answer in an SSA problem. Test 180° − the calculator angle.
  • A plus sign in the cosine rule. It is a minus. With an obtuse angle, cos is negative, so the term becomes an addition.
  • Using the wrong angle in ½ab sin C. C must be the angle between a and b.
  • Rounding too early. Keep full values on the calculator, and round once at the end.
  • Not checking sense. The longest side must be opposite the largest angle.
“Area = ½ × 6 × 9 × sin 50° because 50° is in the triangle, even though it is not between the 6 and the 9.”

Check which sides surround the angle.

The fix: the formula ½ab sin C works only if C is the angle between the sides a and b. If the 50° angle is not between the 6 and the 9, you must first find the angle that is (for example by using the angle sum or the sine rule), or use another pair of sides.

11.Try It Yourself

  1. In triangle ABC, A = 35°, B = 80° and a = 7 cm. Find b.
  2. A triangle has sides 7 cm, 9 cm and 12 cm. Find its largest angle.
  3. Two sides of 10 cm and 12 cm enclose an angle of 25°. Find the area.
Show answers

1. b = 7 × sin 80° ÷ sin 35° = 7 × 0.9848 ÷ 0.5736 = 12.0 cm.

2. The largest angle is opposite 12 cm: cos θ = (49 + 81 − 144) ÷ (2 × 7 × 9) = −14 ÷ 126 = −0.1111, so θ = 96.4°.

3. Area = ½ × 10 × 12 × sin 25° = 60 × 0.4226 = 25.4 cm².

12.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [3 marks] In triangle ABC, A = 48°, B = 71° and a = 9 cm. Find the length of b.
  2. [4 marks] In triangle PQR, p = 10 cm, q = 14 cm and P = 35°. Find the two possible values of angle Q.
  3. [3 marks] A triangle has sides 6.5 cm, 8.4 cm and 10.1 cm. Find its largest angle.
  4. [3 marks] Two sides of 8.4 cm and 11.2 cm enclose an angle of 37°. Find the area of the triangle.
  5. [5 marks] A ship sails 12 km on a bearing of 040° and then 9 km on a bearing of 120°. Find its distance from the start and the bearing of the finish from the start.
  6. [4 marks] From a point A on level ground the angle of elevation of the top of a tower is 25°. From a point B, 50 m nearer to the tower, it is 40°. Find the height of the tower.
  7. [3 marks] A triangle has area 18 cm² and two sides of 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the two possible sizes of the angle between them.
Show answers

1. b = 9 × sin 71° ÷ sin 48° = 9 × 0.9455 ÷ 0.7431 = 11.5 cm (11.45).

2. sin Q = 14 × sin 35° ÷ 10 = 0.8030, so Q = 53.4° or 180° − 53.4° = 126.6°. Both work, since 35° + 126.6° = 161.6° is less than 180°.

3. The largest angle is opposite 10.1: cos θ = (6.5² + 8.4² − 10.1²) ÷ (2 × 6.5 × 8.4) = (42.25 + 70.56 − 102.01) ÷ 109.2 = 0.0989, so θ = 84.3°.

4. Area = ½ × 8.4 × 11.2 × sin 37° = 47.04 × 0.6018 = 28.3 cm².

5. The angle at the turning point is 100°. Distance² = 144 + 81 − 216 cos 100° = 262.5, so distance = 16.2 km. The angle at the start is 33.1° (sin A = 9 sin 100° ÷ 16.2), so the bearing is 040° + 33.1° = 073.1°.

6. The angle at T is 40° − 25° = 15°, and the angle ABT inside the triangle is 140°. BT = 50 × sin 25° ÷ sin 15° = 81.65 m. Height = 81.65 × sin 40° = 52.5 m.

7. ½ × 6 × 8 × sin C = 18, so sin C = 0.75 and C = 48.6° or 131.4°.

13.Key Points

  • Side a is opposite angle A. Always match each side with its opposite angle.
  • Sine rule: a ÷ sin A = b ÷ sin B = c ÷ sin C. Use it when you have a matching side-and-angle pair.
  • Cosine rule: a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A, or cos A = (b² + c² − a²) ÷ 2bc. Use it for SAS and SSS.
  • Area = ½ab sin C, with C between the sides a and b. Heron’s formula handles three sides.
  • In SSA problems, test 180° minus the calculator angle. Two triangles are possible only when b sin A < a < b (A acute).
  • The three angles add to 180°. The longest side is opposite the largest angle.
  • Use degree mode, keep full values, and round only at the end.

14.For Teachers and Parents

Learners often learn the formulas without learning to choose. Give them mixed sets of triangles and ask only “which rule, and why?” before any calculation. The decision chart in Figure 2 helps, as does asking for the matching side-and-angle pair first. For the ambiguous case, a physical model is powerful: pin a strip of card of length a at the end of a strip of length b at the given angle, and let learners swing the short strip to see where it touches the base line. The Triangle Solver on this page lets learners check their own answers, including both solutions of an SSA problem, and the Rule Chooser reinforces the decision step. Always have learners estimate before calculating: the longest side faces the largest angle, and two sides together are longer than the third.
