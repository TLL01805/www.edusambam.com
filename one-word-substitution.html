One-Word Substitution: Phrases and Their One-Word Answers

A person who loves books is a bibliophile. Learn hundreds of one-word substitutes for phrases, with the Greek and Latin word parts that make them easy to remember.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 24 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Vocabulary

Instead of writing "a person who loves books", you can write one word: bibliophile. Instead of "a place where birds are kept", you can write aviary. This skill is called one-word substitution, and it makes writing shorter, sharper and more impressive. It is also a regular question in O Level, A Level and competitive examinations.

This guide explains how one-word substitution works, the Greek and Latin word parts that help you remember the answers, and how to use these words in sentences. Its main part gives 230 phrases with their one-word answers, grouped by theme, followed by sentences, commonly confused pairs, mistakes, key points, ten practice questions and a teacher activity.

It pairs with Synonyms in English and Idioms in English, and connects with Parts of Speech and Common Errors in English. Spelling in this guide follows British English.

1.What Is One-Word Substitution?

One-word substitution means replacing a phrase or a clause with a single word that has the same meaning. The one word is usually a noun or an adjective, and it often comes from Greek or Latin.

  • Why it matters. It makes writing concise and formal, and it shows range of vocabulary.
  • Where it is tested. Examinations ask you to choose the correct single word for a long phrase.
  • How it works. Learn the word together with the meaning, and use the word parts as clues.

14.Commonly Confused One-Word Substitutes

PairDifference
emigrant / immigrantAn emigrant leaves a country; an immigrant arrives in a country.
biannual / biennialBiannual means twice a year; biennial means every two years.
sceptic / cynicA sceptic doubts claims until there is proof; a cynic believes that people act only from selfish motives.
atheist / agnosticAn atheist does not believe in God; an agnostic says it is impossible to know.
ascetic / aestheticAscetic means strict self-denial; aesthetic relates to beauty and art.
libel / slanderLibel is a written false statement; slander is a spoken one.
biography / autobiographyA biography is written by another person; an autobiography is written by the person himself or herself.
bibliophile / bibliographerA bibliophile loves books; a bibliographer lists and describes books.
amateur / noviceAn amateur does something for love, not money; a novice is a beginner.
monarchy / oligarchyMonarchy is rule by one king or queen; oligarchy is rule by a few.

15.Common Mistakes with One-Word Substitution

MistakeCorrectWhy
bibliophobe (for a book lover)bibliophile-phile means love; -phobe means fear.
immigrant (for someone leaving)emigrantEmigrate means to leave.
aesthetic (for self-denial)asceticThe two words sound alike but differ in meaning.
patricide (for killing a mother)matricidePater means father and mater means mother in Latin.
unhearableinaudibleUse the standard Latin-based word.
claustrophobia (for fear of open spaces)agoraphobiaAgora was the open marketplace of ancient Greece.
bilingual (for a person who knows many languages)polyglotBilingual means two languages; polyglot means many.
regicide (for killing a brother)fratricideFrater means brother in Latin.
Key points to remember
  • One-word substitution replaces a phrase with a single word of the same meaning.
  • Most substitutes come from Greek and Latin, so learn the word parts: phil-, -phobia, -cide, -ology, -cracy.
  • Learn each word with its meaning and a sentence of your own.
  • Watch for similar-looking pairs such as emigrant and immigrant, or biannual and biennial.
  • In an examination, write only one word as the answer, with correct spelling.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students a list of ten phrases and ask them to write the one-word substitute, then to check the answers with a partner and use five words in sentences. A matching game with phrase cards and word cards works well for younger students. For older students, ask them to build their own phrases for words made from the same word parts.

Please note: this guide uses British spelling. Some substitutes have more than one accepted answer (for example, bureaucracy and officialdom), and only the most common are shown.
