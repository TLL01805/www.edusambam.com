Wordplay in English: Palindromes, Anagrams, Tongue Twisters and Puns

Madam, I'm Adam. Listen and silent. She sells seashells. Explore palindromes, anagrams, tongue twisters, puns and more, and learn how wordplay builds spelling, vocabulary and pronunciation.

"Madam, I'm Adam." "She sells seashells by the seashore." "Listen" and "silent". These are all examples of wordplay: using the sounds, spellings and meanings of words for fun, creativity and learning. Wordplay is not only entertainment. It sharpens spelling, builds vocabulary, trains pronunciation and helps students to notice how English really works.

This guide explains twelve kinds of wordplay and gives over 170 examples. It covers palindromes (words, sentences, numbers and the longest ones), anagrams (with a list of 55), tongue twisters with audio buttons, puns, spoonerisms, malapropisms, pangrams, riddles and word ladders. It also explains how to use wordplay in the classroom, with key points, ten practice questions and a teacher activity.

It connects with Commonly Mispronounced Words, Commonly Misspelled Words, Idioms in English and Commonly Confused Words. Spelling in this guide follows British English.

2.Why Wordplay Helps Learners

  • Spelling. Palindromes and anagrams make you look at every letter.
  • Vocabulary. Puns and riddles depend on knowing more than one meaning of a word.
  • Pronunciation. Tongue twisters train difficult sound groups.
  • Listening. Spoonerisms and malapropisms sharpen attention to sounds.
  • Confidence and fun. Games make learning memorable and reduce fear of mistakes.

3.Palindromes

A palindrome reads the same forwards and backwards. The word comes from Greek roots meaning "running back again". In phrases and sentences, ignore spaces, capital letters and punctuation.

How to make a palindrome: start with a word and its reverse (live, evil), join them with a middle (Live not on evil), and then adjust the words until the letters match from both ends.

How to solve an anagram

  1. Count the letters so that you know the length of the answer.
  2. Look for common endings such as -ing, -ed, -er, -tion and common beginnings such as un-, re-, pre-.
  3. Group the vowels and consonants and try common pairs such as th, ch, sh, st.
  4. Write the letters in a circle to see them in new orders.
  5. Check that every letter has been used once.

5.Tongue Twisters

A tongue twister is a phrase that is difficult to say quickly because it repeats similar sounds. Tongue twisters are used by actors, newsreaders and teachers to train clear speech. Say each one slowly first, then gradually faster. Tap the speaker to hear it, then try to match the voice.

Tips: say each twister three times without a mistake before you speed up, and record yourself for comparison. The speaker uses your device's voice. A famous early collection is Peter Piper's Practical Principles of Plain and Perfect Pronunciation, published in 1813.

6.Puns

A pun plays on two meanings of a word, or on two words that sound alike. Puns are common in headlines, adverts and jokes.

PunWhy it works
I used to be a baker, but I couldn't make enough dough.dough means both bread mixture and money
I'm reading a book about anti-gravity. It's impossible to put down.put down refers to gravity and to a gripping book
Why was the maths book sad? It had too many problems.problems means exercises and troubles
Why did the scarecrow win an award? He was outstanding in his field.outstanding means excellent and standing out in a field
What do you call fake spaghetti? An impasta.impasta sounds like impostor
I would tell you a construction joke, but I'm still working on it.working on it means building and still preparing
The graveyard is so crowded that people are dying to get in.dying to means very eager and literally dying

Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana.flies and like change their part of speech
Why can't you trust an atom? They make up everything.make up means form and invent
I was going to tell a joke about paper, but it is tearing me apart.tearing means ripping and causing distress
I wondered why the football was getting bigger. Then it hit me.hit me means struck and suddenly realised

7.Spoonerisms

A spoonerism swaps the first sounds of two words. It is named after the Reverend William Archibald Spooner (1844–1930) of Oxford, who is said to have made such slips. Many of the examples attributed to him are probably invented.

8.Malapropisms

A malapropism is the use of a wrong word that sounds like the right word, with a funny result. The term comes from Mrs Malaprop, a character in Richard Brinsley Sheridan's comedy The Rivals (1775), whose name comes from the French mal à propos, meaning "not suitable".

9.Pangrams and Lipograms

A pangram uses every letter of the alphabet at least once. The best pangrams are short.

  • The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.
  • Pack my box with five dozen liquor jugs.
  • Sphinx of black quartz, judge my vow.
  • Jackdaws love my big sphinx of quartz.
  • How vexingly quick daft zebras jump!
  • Waltz, bad nymph, for quick jigs vex.

A lipogram leaves out a letter. Ernest Vincent Wright wrote an entire novel, Gadsby (1939), without using the letter e, and the French writer Georges Perec did the same in La Disparition (1969).

11.Word Ladders

In a word ladder, you change one letter at a time to turn one word into another. Every step must be a real word. The puzzle was invented by Lewis Carroll in 1877, who called it "Doublets".

Start and endLadder
cold → warmcold → cord → card → ward → warm
head → tailhead → heal → teal → tell → tall → tail
cat → dogcat → cot → cog → dog

Try it yourself: turn cold into hard, or pig into sty. There may be more than one correct ladder.

12.Word Games for the Classroom

  • Crosswords. The first crossword was published in 1913 in a New York newspaper.
  • Scrabble. The board game was invented in the 1930s by the American architect Alfred Butts.
  • Hangman. A quick revision game for spelling.
  • Word chains. Each word begins with the last letter of the previous word.
  • Odd one out. Students find the word that does not belong to a group.
Key points to remember
  • Wordplay uses sounds, spellings and meanings for fun and learning.
  • A palindrome reads the same both ways; an anagram rearranges all the letters of another word.
  • A pangram uses every letter of the alphabet; a lipogram avoids one letter.
  • Tongue twisters train difficult sounds: start slowly and speed up.
  • A pun uses two meanings; a spoonerism swaps first sounds; a malapropism uses a wrong word that sounds similar.
  • Word ladders change one letter at a time and every step must be a word.
  • Use wordplay for short, enjoyable practice that supports spelling, vocabulary and speaking.
For teachers and parents

Activity: divide the class into teams. Round 1: write as many palindrome words as possible in two minutes. Round 2: solve ten anagrams. Round 3: each team performs one tongue twister, and the class judges clarity. Round 4: students write their own pun or riddle. Younger students can use pictures and short words, and older students can invent a short palindromic sentence.

Please note: this guide uses British spelling. Some anagrams, such as phrase anagrams, can vary, and some origins, such as the spoonerisms attributed to Spooner, are traditional and may not be exact.
