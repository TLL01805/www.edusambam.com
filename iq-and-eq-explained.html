IQ and EQ Explained: Intelligence, Emotions and Success

One helps you solve problems. The other helps you understand people and yourself. Learn what IQ and EQ really measure, what research says, which popular claims are myths, and how to build both.

Last updated: 12 October 2026. Written by the EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team. This article is for general education and does not replace advice from a qualified professional.

Two students score the same marks in a mathematics test. One panics before every exam, snaps at friends and gives up quickly. The other feels nervous too, but breathes, asks for help and tries again. Both are clever, yet the way they handle feelings changes how school, friendships and later life feel. Psychologists describe this difference using two words: IQ, which is about how well we reason, and EQ, which is about how well we understand and manage emotions. This guide explains what each one is, what the research really says, which popular claims are myths, and how you can build both.

Key Takeaways

IQ is a test-based estimate of reasoning ability, with an average of 100. EQ is the ability to notice, understand and manage emotions in yourself and others. They are different skills, and both are useful. IQ stays fairly stable, while emotional skills can be practised and improved at any age. No single number or test can describe a whole person.

1.Intelligence, IQ and EQ: What Do the Words Mean?

Intelligence is the ability to learn, reason, solve problems and adapt to new situations. There is no single agreed definition, and psychologists continue to debate it. IQ (intelligence quotient) is a score from a standardised test that estimates certain reasoning abilities by comparing you with other people of your age. EQ (emotional quotient) or emotional intelligence is the ability to recognise, understand, use and manage emotions.

TermSimple meaningEveryday question it answers
IQA score for reasoning skills such as logic, memory and pattern findingHow well can I work out and solve a problem?
EQThe skill of understanding and handling feelingsHow well do I understand what I and others feel, and what to do about it?
IntelligenceThe wider ability to learn and adaptHow well do I learn from experience?

It helps to remember that IQ and EQ are tools for describing abilities, not labels that decide who a person is. A score is a snapshot, taken on one day, with one test.

2.A Short History of IQ Testing

The idea of measuring intelligence began with a practical need. In the early 1900s, France made school compulsory, and teachers needed a fair way to find children who needed extra help. In 1905 the psychologist Alfred Binet and his colleague Théodore Simon created the first practical test for this purpose. Binet stressed that the test was meant to help children get support, not to rank them for life.

YearPersonWhat happened
1904Charles SpearmanSuggested a general intelligence factor, called g, behind performance on many different mental tasks
1905Alfred Binet and Théodore SimonCreated the first practical intelligence test to identify children who needed extra help
1912William SternProposed the "intelligence quotient": mental age divided by actual age, multiplied by 100
1916Lewis TermanPublished the Stanford–Binet test in the United States
1939David WechslerCreated adult and child scales that compare a person with others of the same age, the model still used today

The early formula, mental age divided by actual age, worked poorly for adults, so modern tests no longer use it. Instead they compare your result with a large, carefully chosen group of people of the same age. This is why a modern IQ is called a deviation score.

3.How IQ Scores Work

Modern IQ tests are designed so that the average score is 100, and the standard deviation (a measure of how spread out the scores are) is 15. When scores from a large population are plotted, they form a smooth, symmetrical shape called a bell curve (or normal distribution). Most people score near the middle, and the further a score is from 100, the fewer people have it.

The IQ bell curve: about 68% of people score between 85 and 115, and about 95% between 70 and 130.
Score rangeDescriptionRoughly how many people
130 and aboveVery highAbout 2 in 100
115 to 129Above averageAbout 14 in 100
85 to 114Average rangeAbout 68 in 100
70 to 84Below averageAbout 14 in 100
Below 70Very lowAbout 2 in 100

Because the numbers come from a statistical pattern, a score is best read as a range, not an exact point. A person who scores 108 on one day might score 104 or 112 on another. Differences of a few points are not meaningful, and a good psychologist always reports a margin of error.

4.What Do IQ Tests Actually Measure?

A full IQ test is not one question but a group of different tasks, given one to one by a trained examiner. The tasks are grouped into areas of thinking. Names differ between tests, but most cover the following.

AreaWhat it checksExample task
Verbal reasoningUnderstanding words and ideasExplaining how two things, such as "river" and "road", are alike
Visual and spatial reasoningSeeing patterns and shapesCompleting a pattern of blocks or pictures
Fluid reasoningSolving a new problem without prior knowledgeFinding the rule in a series of shapes and choosing the next one
Working memoryHolding and using information for a short timeRepeating a list of numbers backwards
Processing speedHow quickly simple tasks are done accuratelyMatching symbols against a key as fast as possible

Fluid and crystallised intelligence

The psychologist Raymond Cattell separated two kinds of reasoning. Fluid intelligence is the ability to solve new problems on the spot, and it tends to be strongest in early adulthood. Crystallised intelligence is the knowledge and vocabulary you have built through learning and experience, and it can keep growing for decades. This is one reason an older person can be wiser and more knowledgeable even if they are slower at a puzzle.

5.What IQ Can and Cannot Tell You

IQ is one of the most studied measures in psychology, and it has real strengths. It is a fairly good predictor of school performance, and it is linked, on average, to how quickly people learn complex tasks. Teachers and psychologists also use it to identify children who need learning support or enrichment.

But it has clear limits. An IQ score does not measure creativity, honesty, kindness, curiosity, determination, leadership, wisdom or practical skill. Two people with the same score can lead very different lives. Other things that matter a great deal are effort, health, the quality of teaching and the opportunities a person is given.

IQ can help withIQ cannot tell you
Understanding learning strengths and difficultiesHow hard someone is willing to work
Predicting average school results across large groupsWhat one individual will achieve in life
Identifying a need for extra supportHow kind, honest or creative someone is
Research on how thinking worksA person's value or worth
Fairness Matters

Tests are written in a particular language and use ideas from a particular culture. A child who is tested in a second language, who has missed schooling, or who is tired, hungry or anxious may score below their real ability. For this reason, scores should always be interpreted carefully by a trained professional and never used to label a child.

6.Can IQ Change? Nature, Nurture and the Flynn Effect

Intelligence is shaped by both nature (the genes you inherit) and nurture (your environment, such as food, health, schooling, language and stress). Research on twins suggests that genes play a growing role as people get older, but scientists warn that "heritability" describes patterns in a population. It does not mean that any one person's intelligence is fixed, and it says nothing about how much a good environment can help.

Evidence for the power of environment comes from the Flynn effect, named after the researcher James Flynn. Through most of the twentieth century, average raw scores on IQ tests rose by about three points per decade in many countries, so the tests had to be re-set again and again to keep the average at 100. Genes cannot change that fast, so the explanation must be environmental. Suggested causes include better nutrition, better health care, longer schooling and more practice at abstract thinking. In some countries the trend has slowed or even reversed in recent years, which shows that these gains are not automatic.

Real-World Example

Studies of children in poor regions have found that improving nutrition, treating infections and giving early learning support can raise measured scores and school success. A healthy body and a stimulating childhood are building blocks for a thinking mind.

Learning also strengthens the brain. A person who reads widely, learns a new language or practises music is training the connections that support reasoning and memory. A learner who believes that abilities can grow, sometimes called a growth mindset, tends to keep trying after mistakes, and that persistence is often more useful than a single score.

7.Other Ways of Thinking About Intelligence

Many scholars feel that a single number is too narrow. Two well-known alternative ideas are described below.

IdeaWho proposed itMain claimFair comment
Multiple intelligencesHoward Gardner (1983)There are several separate kinds of intelligence, such as linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, bodily, interpersonal and othersPopular in schools because it values different talents, but most psychologists say there is little scientific evidence for fully separate intelligences
Triarchic theoryRobert Sternberg (1985)Intelligence has analytical, creative and practical partsDraws attention to creativity and real-life problem solving that IQ tests miss

You can safely take one useful lesson from these ideas: people are good at different things, and school tests cover only some of them. You do not need to believe in "learning styles" or fixed intelligence types to value that lesson.

8.What Is Emotional Intelligence (EQ)?

Emotional intelligence is the ability to notice and understand emotions, to use that understanding when you think and decide, and to manage emotions in yourself and in your relationships. The idea has a short history.

YearPersonContribution
1985Reuven Bar-OnUsed the phrase "emotional quotient" (EQ) in his academic work and later developed a well-known questionnaire
1990Peter Salovey and John MayerPublished the first scientific model of emotional intelligence
1995Daniel GolemanWrote the bestselling book Emotional Intelligence, which made the idea famous around the world

The Salovey and Mayer model: four abilities

  1. Perceiving emotions: noticing feelings in your own body, in faces, in voices and in body language.
  2. Using emotions to think: letting feelings guide attention, for example using calm to concentrate or excitement to be creative.
  3. Understanding emotions: knowing what causes a feeling and how feelings can change or combine, such as how disappointment can turn into anger.
  4. Managing emotions: handling feelings so that they help you reach your goals and protect your relationships.

Two ways of measuring EQ

Researchers separate ability tests, which ask you to solve emotional problems that have better and worse answers (for example, the MSCEIT test), from self-report questionnaires, which ask you how you usually behave. Self-report scores show how people see themselves, which is not always the same as how skilled they are. This is one reason why EQ is harder to measure precisely than IQ.

9.The Five Components of EQ, One by One

Goleman described emotional intelligence in five parts. The first three are about yourself, and the last two are about other people.

Goleman's five components of emotional intelligence. The first three are about yourself; the last two are about other people.
ComponentWhat it meansWhat it looks likeExample
Self-awarenessKnowing what you feel, why you feel it, and your strengths and limitsHonest about mistakes; can describe feelings in words"I am not really angry with my brother. I am worried about tomorrow's test."
Self-regulationManaging strong feelings and impulsesStays calm under pressure; thinks before actingTaking three slow breaths before answering an unfair comment
MotivationAn inner drive to improve, with hope and persistenceSets goals; keeps going after failureRevising again after a poor test result and asking the teacher for advice
EmpathyUnderstanding how other people feel and whyListens well; notices when someone is upsetAsking a quiet classmate, "Are you okay?"
Social skillsCommunicating, cooperating and solving disagreementsWorks well in a team; resolves conflict fairlyHelping two friends who are arguing to explain their sides

Notice that empathy is different from sympathy. Empathy means understanding how another person feels. Sympathy means feeling sorry for them. You can show empathy without agreeing with the person, and being empathic does not mean you must fix their problem. Often the best response is simply to listen.

10.Emotions in the Brain and Body

Emotions are not a weakness or a mistake. They are signals produced by the brain and body to help us react to the world. Two brain areas are especially important for understanding EQ. The amygdala, a small almond-shaped structure deep in the brain, is quick to detect possible threats and can trigger a fast fear or anger response. The prefrontal cortex, at the front of the brain, helps with planning, judging and self-control, and it can calm and guide the response. Goleman used the phrase "amygdala hijack" for moments when a strong emotional reaction takes over before careful thinking can start.

The body joins in as well. When you are frightened, your heart beats faster, your breathing quickens and your muscles tighten. Learning to notice these body signals early is the first step towards self-awareness. Slow, deep breathing, a short walk or a drink of water can help the thinking part of the brain catch up with the feeling part.

The emotional pause: a small gap between what happens and what you do next.
Worked Example

A friend shares a secret that you trusted her with. Reaction without EQ: you shout at her in the corridor and refuse to talk to her again. Reaction with EQ: you notice your face is hot and your heart is racing (trigger), you take slow breaths for a minute (pause), you tell yourself, "I feel betrayed and embarrassed" (name it), and later you say calmly, "I was hurt when you shared that. Can we talk about it?" (respond). You still felt angry. The difference is what you did with the anger.

11.IQ and EQ Side by Side

IQ and EQ are different abilities that work best together.
FeatureIQEQ
Main focusReasoning, logic, memory and learningUnderstanding and managing emotions and relationships
How it is measuredStandardised tests given by a trained examinerAbility tests, questionnaires and observation
ScaleAverage 100, standard deviation 15Different tests use different scales, so numbers cannot be compared easily
StabilityFairly stable after childhoodCan grow with practice at any age
Helps most withStudying, analysis, complex problem solvingTeamwork, stress, friendships, leadership
Main weaknessMisses character, creativity and empathyHarder to measure; some tests are less reliable

It is important to say that IQ and EQ are not opposites and you do not have to choose between them. Studies generally find only a small to moderate link between the two. A person can be strong in both, strong in one, or still developing in both. Each can be improved in its own way.

12.Why EQ Matters: What the Evidence Says

Research suggests that emotional skills are linked with better relationships, lower stress and better teamwork. Reviews of school programmes that teach social and emotional learning found improvements in behaviour and attitudes, and an average gain in academic results of around 11 percentile points. A large long-term study in New Zealand followed more than a thousand children from birth into adult life. It found that children with better self-control at ages three to eleven were, on average, healthier and less likely to have money problems or trouble with the law when they reached their thirties, even after taking account of intelligence and family background.

Real-World Example

A hospital doctor needs strong reasoning to diagnose an illness (IQ) and strong emotional skills to explain frightening news calmly, listen to a worried family and work with nurses under pressure (EQ). A patient is best served when the doctor has both.

However, a careful reader should avoid exaggeration. You may see claims such as "IQ accounts for only 20% of success and EQ for 80%". There is no solid scientific evidence for such numbers. In studies of workplaces, emotional intelligence has a modest link with job performance, and its effect depends on the job. The honest summary is that both abilities matter, their importance changes from one situation to another, and neither one guarantees success.

Another popular story concerns the "marshmallow test", in which young children were offered one sweet now or two if they could wait. Early results suggested that children who waited did better later. A later, larger study found that the link was much smaller once family background was taken into account. The lesson is that self-control matters, but it is not destiny, and it is shaped by circumstances as well as by character.

13.How to Build Your IQ and Learning Power

You cannot change your score by willpower alone, but you can strengthen the habits and knowledge that support thinking. Try the following.

  • Read widely and often. Reading builds vocabulary, background knowledge and concentration, which feed crystallised intelligence.
  • Practise problem solving. Mathematics, logic puzzles, chess and coding train step-by-step reasoning.
  • Learn something new and hard. A language, an instrument or a new subject challenges the brain in fresh ways.
  • Ask "why" and "how". Understanding an idea deeply is better than memorising it.
  • Test yourself. Recalling information from memory, rather than just re-reading, makes learning stronger.
  • Protect sleep, food and exercise. A tired or hungry brain does not work at its best, and regular exercise is linked with better memory and attention.
  • Welcome mistakes. Each error shows you what to practise next.

14.How to Build Your EQ

Emotional skills are learned the way other skills are learned: by understanding the idea, practising often, and getting feedback. Here are seven practical habits.

  1. Keep a feelings journal. Each evening, write one or two lines: what did I feel today, and what caused it?
  2. Widen your feelings vocabulary. The more exact your words, the better you understand yourself. Use the table below.
  3. Use the pause. When you feel upset, take three slow breaths before you speak or send a message.
  4. Listen fully. Let the other person finish, then repeat back what you heard before you reply.
  5. Take their point of view. Ask yourself, "How would I feel in their place?"
  6. Welcome feedback. Treat criticism as information, not as an attack, and say thank you.
  7. Look after your body. Sleep, food, exercise and time with friends all make emotional control easier.
Basic wordMore exact words
AngryAnnoyed, frustrated, irritated, resentful, furious
SadDisappointed, lonely, hurt, discouraged, hopeless
ScaredNervous, worried, overwhelmed, insecure, panicky
HappyProud, grateful, relieved, hopeful, excited
BadEmbarrassed, guilty, ashamed, tired, left out

Handling anger, step by step

  1. Notice the warning signs in your body, such as a hot face, a tight jaw or fast breathing.
  2. Stop and breathe slowly: in for four counts, out for six.
  3. Name the feeling and its cause in one sentence.
  4. Decide what you really want to happen.
  5. Speak calmly using an "I" statement, such as "I felt left out when the plan changed."

If strong feelings such as sadness, worry or anger stay with you for weeks, or make everyday life hard, it is a sign of strength, not weakness, to talk to a parent, teacher, school counsellor or doctor.

15.Common Myths and Mistakes

MythFact
IQ is fixed for lifeIt is fairly stable, but health, schooling and environment can change it, as the Flynn effect shows
A high IQ guarantees successEffort, character, opportunity and emotional skills also matter a great deal
High EQ means being nice to everyone all the timeIt includes setting boundaries and saying difficult things respectfully
EQ means hiding your feelingsIt means understanding and managing feelings, not pretending you have none
Clever people cannot be emotionally skilledThe two abilities are separate, and many people are strong in both
EQ is 80% of successThere is no reliable evidence for any such figure
Online IQ quizzes give your real IQMost are for entertainment and are not standardised or supervised
One test decides your futureNo test can measure creativity, kindness or potential

Small daily actions work better than occasional big efforts. Use this simple plan for one week, then repeat the parts you enjoyed.

  • Can you explain the difference between IQ and EQ in your own words?
  • Can you name the five components of EQ and give an example of each?
  • Do you know why a single IQ score cannot describe a person?
  • Have you chosen at least one habit to practise this week?

A Closing Thought

IQ helps you work out what to do, and EQ helps you work out how to do it with other people and with yourself. A test score cannot measure your whole mind, and no one is limited to the abilities they have today. The skills in this article, such as curiosity, careful thinking, naming feelings, pausing, listening and trying again, are all trainable. Choose one habit this week, practise it every day, and notice what changes.
