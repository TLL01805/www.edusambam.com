Directed Writing from Source Texts — Using, Developing & Evaluating Information

A comprehensive Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 unit on directed writing: reading source texts critically, selecting useful information, developing and evaluating ideas, adapting register and form, and writing effectively for a specific audience and purpose.

1. What Directed Writing from Source Texts Really Means

Unit 44 develops the source-based writing skill at the centre of Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 Paper 2 Section A. In this task, you read one or two source texts, select useful information, then use, develop and evaluate that information to create a discursive, argumentative or persuasive speech, email, report, letter or article. The challenge is not simply to copy the source: you must transform reading into purposeful writing for a particular audience, purpose and form.

2. The Two Jobs You Must Do

For the 2027–2028 syllabus, Paper 2 Section A asks candidates to use, develop and evaluate information from one or two texts of approximately 400–450 words in total. The response is about 250–350 words and may take the form of a speech, email, report, letter or article.

Directed writing is writing with a destination. The source text gives you information, facts, ideas and opinions, while the task tells you what you must do with them. Your response must therefore satisfy two demands at the same time: read accurately and write deliberately.

3. Read the Task Before the Source

Cambridge assesses the directed-writing task through R3 and R5 on the reading side and W1–W5 on the writing side.

  • Reading job: understand, select, analyse, evaluate and develop information.
  • Writing job: organise ideas, choose suitable vocabulary and sentence structures, establish the right register, and maintain accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar.

Think of the task as having two linked jobs. First, you must identify and select information that serves the task. Second, you must turn that information into an effective piece of writing rather than a list of copied points.

4. Audience, Purpose and Form

A useful planning line is: Who am I? → Who am I writing to? → Why am I writing? → What form must I use? → What information must I use or develop?

Before you start extracting information, read the whole question. Identify the role you are being asked to adopt, the audience you are addressing, the purpose of the writing, the form you must produce, and every instruction about what you must include.

5. What the Source Is — and What It Is Not

Do not treat form as decoration. Cambridge expects candidates to understand the conventions and features of the text type they are asked to produce.

These three elements work together. A report written for a school committee is not organised or phrased in exactly the same way as an article written for young readers. A persuasive speech needs a voice suited to listeners; an email may need a direct opening and closing; a report may use headings to make information easy to locate.

6. Selecting Information for a Specific Purpose

Copying a source sentence can preserve information, but it does not automatically demonstrate that you have understood, developed or evaluated it. Strong directed writing shows controlled transformation.

The source is a bank of usable material, not a script that you must reproduce. It may contain facts, examples, explanations, claims, viewpoints, benefits, drawbacks, causes, effects or suggestions. Your task is to decide which material matters and how it should function in your response.

7. Selecting Is Not Copying

For example, if a task asks you to argue for a school recycling programme, a source statistic about reduced waste may be useful evidence. A colourful anecdote about a recycling bin may be useful only if it supports the argument. A historical detail about when recycling was first introduced may be irrelevant unless the task specifically asks for background.

Selection means choosing information because it helps answer the task. Mark each source point mentally as useful, possibly useful or irrelevant. Then connect the useful points to the exact wording of the question.

8. R3: Analyse, Evaluate and Develop

Use short planning notes such as cost saving, less waste, student responsibility, or possible objection: inconvenience rather than copying full source sentences during planning.

Copying follows the source. Selecting follows the task. A candidate who copies every interesting point may produce a response that is crowded but unfocused. A candidate who selects only the strongest relevant material has more space to explain why the information matters.

9. How to Develop a Source Point

These operations help turn a source point into meaningful writing.

  • Analyse: identify what an idea means, how its parts connect, or what its implications are.
  • Evaluate: judge its strength, importance, usefulness or limitations.
  • Develop: extend the idea logically by explaining consequences, reasons, applications or implications.

R3 is more demanding than simple retrieval. Cambridge describes it as the ability to analyse, evaluate and develop facts, ideas and opinions, using appropriate support from the text.

10. How to Evaluate an Idea

Development must remain logically connected to the source. Do not invent statistics, quotations or facts and present them as though they came from the source.

A useful development chain is point → explanation → consequence → relevance. Suppose a source says that flexible school hours can reduce peak-time travel. You could explain that fewer people travelling simultaneously may reduce pressure on crowded transport, then connect this to the task’s purpose.

11. Facts, Ideas and Opinions Are Different

Useful questions include: How strong is this claim? Why? What condition might limit it? Who benefits? What problem could arise? Is there a trade-off?

Evaluation asks whether an idea is convincing, useful, practical, fair, significant or limited. The evaluation should be supported by reasoning and, where appropriate, evidence from the source.

12. Use the Source, But Make the Writing Your Own

When developing a response, make your own stance clear without pretending that a source opinion is an established fact. Accurate attribution can be especially useful when you are discussing competing viewpoints.

A source may mix factual information with interpretations and opinions. Do not automatically treat every statement as equally certain. Notice language such as research shows, experts argue, some people believe, or the writer suggests.

13. Avoid Mechanical Paraphrasing

Instead of copying two separate source sentences, you might combine their ideas into one sentence, explain the relationship between them, and then connect the result to the purpose of your writing.

Effective directed writing usually involves paraphrasing, combining related information and restructuring ideas. The goal is not to replace every word with a synonym. The goal is to preserve the relevant meaning while expressing it naturally in a form suited to the new task.

14. Group Related Information

Mechanical paraphrasing changes vocabulary while leaving the original structure almost untouched. It can sound unnatural and may even change the meaning. A better method is to read the source point, look away from it, state its meaning in your own words, and then check the source again for accuracy.

15. Build a Clear Line of Argument

Grouping also reduces repetition. If three source points all support the same broader idea, combine them intelligently instead of discussing each one as though it were unrelated.

Source texts do not have to determine the order of your response. Group information according to the purpose of your writing. Several source paragraphs might contain ideas about cost, while another paragraph introduces a concern about fairness. Your response may be clearer if related ideas are brought together.

16. Persuasion: More Than Strong Vocabulary

This does not mean every paragraph must follow a rigid formula. It means the relationship between ideas should be visible.

For discursive or argumentative tasks, the reader should be able to follow your reasoning. A useful structure is claim → evidence or source support → explanation → implication → response to an alternative view.

17. Discursive Writing: Consider More Than One Side

Questions such as What would this mean for us?, What is the practical benefit? and What objection might a reader raise? can help you move from assertion to convincing reasoning.

Persuasive writing is not simply emotional language. Strong persuasion can combine relevant evidence, logical reasoning, acknowledgement of concerns, carefully chosen examples and a clear sense of audience.

18. Argumentative Writing: Take a Defensible Position

You can acknowledge an opposing point and then assess it: Although the proposal could increase initial costs, the source indicates that the longer-term savings may outweigh that disadvantage. The evaluation is more important than simply saying that both sides exist.

Discursive writing explores an issue by considering relevant arguments and perspectives. If the source contains competing views, do not erase the disagreement merely to make your response sound confident.

19. The Five Required Text Types

A useful test is: Could a reader identify what I believe by the end of the introduction? If not, the response may need a clearer controlling idea.

An argumentative response normally benefits from a clear position. This does not require ignoring opposing evidence. Instead, use relevant counterarguments to demonstrate why your position remains stronger, more practical or better supported.

20. Register: Formal, Neutral or More Personal?

  • Speech: sounds natural when spoken and addresses listeners directly.
  • Email: establishes a clear relationship with the recipient and uses an appropriate opening and closing.
  • Report: presents information in an organised, functional way and may use headings where appropriate.
  • Letter: follows conventions appropriate to its relationship, purpose and level of formality.
  • Article: engages a defined readership while presenting ideas in an appropriate journalistic or informative style.

For Paper 2 Section A, the syllabus identifies five possible forms: speech, email, report, letter and article. Each can carry a discursive, argumentative or persuasive purpose, so form and purpose must be considered together.

21. Structure the Response Before You Draft

Register affects vocabulary, sentence structure, contractions, greetings, closings, degree of directness and even how strongly an opinion is expressed. Avoid making every task sound like the same generic essay.

Register is the level and style of language appropriate to the context. A formal report to a school authority requires different choices from a friendly email to a classmate.

22. Introductions That Establish Direction

Planning should be brief enough to save time rather than consume it. The aim is to know where the important ideas are going before you begin writing full sentences.

Spend a short period planning. A practical plan can contain five elements: purpose, main position, selected source points, development/evaluation, and conclusion or closing move.

23. Paragraphs Should Have a Job

For a persuasive task, the introduction can establish the issue and your central position. For an article, it can create interest while making the subject clear. For a report, it can identify the purpose and scope.

A good opening establishes the context and gives the reader a reason to continue. It does not need to repeat the question word for word.

24. Cohesion: Make the Connections Visible

Topic sentences can help readers understand the direction of a paragraph, but variety is also useful. Organisation should feel controlled rather than mechanical.

Each paragraph should contribute something identifiable: a major argument, a supporting idea, an evaluation, a counterargument, a practical implication or a conclusion. Avoid paragraphs that contain several unrelated points simply because they all came from the same part of the source.

25. Use Evidence Without Overloading the Response

Pronouns, repeated key terms, substitution, parallel structures and carefully ordered sentences can also connect ideas. The reader should not have to guess why one paragraph follows another.

Cohesion comes from logical relationships as well as linking words. Words such as however, therefore, for example and in contrast can help, but they should not be used as decoration.

26. Do Not Invent Source Evidence

When a source gives a precise figure, preserve it accurately if it matters. When the exact detail is unnecessary, a concise paraphrase may communicate the point more effectively.

Source support should be relevant and purposeful. You do not need to reproduce large quotations or every statistic. Select the evidence that strengthens the point you are making, then explain its significance.

27. A Worked Mini-Example

You may develop an idea through your own reasoning, but make the distinction clear. Your reasoning should grow logically from the information provided rather than pretending to be additional evidence from the source.

This is a crucial discipline. If the source says that a programme may reduce costs, do not turn that into a claim that it will definitely reduce costs. If the source gives one example, do not invent two more and present them as source evidence.

28. A Second Example: Turning a Fact into Development

Stronger approach: select the increased study opportunity, explain why access matters to students, acknowledge the staffing-cost concern, and evaluate whether the educational benefit justifies the cost. The response uses the source but creates a new argument for a new audience.

Weak approach: repeat both source sentences and finish with “I think it is good.”

Task: Write an article for local residents arguing whether extended opening hours should continue.

Source information: A community library has extended its opening hours. The change gives students more time to study, but the library must manage higher staffing costs.

29. Handling One Source Versus Two Sources

The development is acceptable because it is a logical extension of the source idea, not a fabricated statistic or invented claim attributed to the source.

Development: the garden may provide practical learning as well as produce, because students can observe how food is grown and discuss responsibility for resources.

Selection: useful if the task concerns benefits of the garden.

Source point: A school garden can provide fresh produce for a small number of students.

30. When Sources Disagree

Do not give equal space to both sources simply because there are two. Relevance determines selection. If one source contains stronger evidence for a particular point, use it accordingly.

If a task provides two texts, treat them as two evidence pools. Identify where they agree, where they differ, and which information is most useful for the specific task.

31. Command Words You Should Recognise

You can write: While Source A emphasises the immediate cost, Source B highlights the longer-term benefit. Then go further by judging which argument is more convincing and why.

Disagreement can be valuable. It gives you material for evaluation. Ask whether the difference comes from different evidence, different priorities, different assumptions or different perspectives.

32. The 250–350 Word Target

Although the exact wording of a question varies, understanding the command word can help you decide how much analysis or judgement your response needs.

The 2027–2028 syllabus includes command words to help candidates understand what is expected. Consider means review and respond to given information; evaluate means judge the quality, importance, amount or value of something; explain means make purposes, reasons or relationships clear and support them with relevant evidence; and identify means name, select or recognise.

33. Accuracy Still Matters

If your response is too short, check whether you have developed important ideas. If it is too long, remove repetition and weaker material rather than cutting essential reasoning.

The syllabus specifies about 250–350 words for Paper 2 Section A. This is not a reason to add filler. The aim is to use the available space to select, develop and evaluate relevant information while maintaining control of the writing.

34. Common Directed-Writing Mistakes

Also check names, figures and source details. Accuracy is part of W5, so proofreading is not an optional final decoration.

A sophisticated argument loses force when sentences are difficult to understand. Proofread for sentence boundaries, subject–verb agreement, tense consistency, punctuation, spelling, apostrophes and commonly confused words.

35. A Reliable Five-Stage Method

  • Ignoring the audience and writing a generic essay.
  • Copying the source instead of transforming it.
  • Using every source point instead of selecting relevant ones.
  • Making unsupported claims and presenting them as source facts.
  • Explaining an idea without evaluating it when evaluation is needed.
  • Giving opinions without reasoning or support.
  • Using the wrong form or weak conventions.
  • Using an unsuitable register.
  • Repeating the same argument in several paragraphs.
  • Writing a long introduction and leaving too little space for development.
  • Adding decorative vocabulary that reduces clarity.
  • Failing to proofread.

36. A Fast Examination Planning Grid

  1. Decode: identify role, audience, purpose, form and instructions.
  2. Select: extract the source information that directly serves the task.
  3. Develop: explain, connect and extend important ideas without inventing evidence.
  4. Organise: build a logical sequence suited to the reader and purpose.
  5. Refine: check register, accuracy, cohesion, relevance and word length.

37. Self-Assessment Against the Cambridge Objectives

This compact grid helps prevent a common problem: understanding the source perfectly but forgetting what the actual writing task requires.

Before drafting, mentally complete: Audience | Purpose | Form | Position | Source points | Development | Counterpoint | Ending.

38. Final Checklist Before You Submit

  • R3: Have I analysed, evaluated or developed the source ideas?
  • R5: Have I selected information for the specific purpose?
  • W1: Does my writing communicate thought, feeling or experience where appropriate to the task?
  • W2: Are my ideas organised deliberately?
  • W3: Are vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate and varied?
  • W4: Is my register suitable for the audience and context?
  • W5: Is my spelling, punctuation and grammar accurate?

  • Have I used the relevant source information accurately?
  • Have I selected rather than copied?

  • Have I made my position or purpose clear?
