Character Development: Writing People Readers Care About

Plot gets readers to turn the page once. Character is what makes them come back. Learn how to build people who feel real — flaws, wants and all.

Many readers forget the details of a plot but remember a person: how they spoke, what they wanted, and what it cost them. A character worth caring about is built from want, flaw and change, not only from a name and a description.

It is tempting to think that character development means listing physical traits: hair colour, height, favourite food. But surface details rarely make a reader care. What makes a character feel alive is what they want, what stands in their way, and how they are forced to change as the story goes on.

This guide covers the building blocks of character: desire, flaw, backstory, voice and arc. It also shows how to reveal character through action, how to build a character sheet, and how to avoid common mistakes. It ends with worked examples, a glossary and ten practice questions.

1.What Makes a Character Feel Real?

Real people are made of contradictions. They are brave in one situation and afraid in another. They want things they cannot have, and they sometimes act against their own interests. A character who feels real has the same mixture.

The novelist E. M. Forster described two kinds of character. A flat character is built around one idea or quality and is easy to sum up in a sentence. A round character has several sides and can surprise the reader. Flat characters are useful in small roles, such as a shopkeeper who appears once. Your main characters should usually be round.

Stories also use some standard roles. The protagonist is the main character. The antagonist is the person or force that opposes them. A foil is a character who contrasts with another, so that each stands out more clearly: a careless friend placed beside a careful one shows both traits.

2.Desire: What Do They Want?

Every compelling character has a goal that drives their actions: something they consciously want. This is their external desire. Without it, a character drifts through events instead of pursuing them, and readers lose interest.

Example

A young chef wants to win a famous cooking competition to save her family's failing restaurant. That single, clear desire gives every scene a purpose.

Many writers add three ideas to the want. Motivation is the reason behind it: why does she want this? The stakes are what she will lose if she fails: the restaurant, her family's income. And the need is something inside her that she must face in order to grow, often something she does not know she needs. This want and need model is a popular craft tool, not a law, but it helps to create depth.

3.The Flaw: What Holds Them Back?

A flaw is not a weakness added for comic relief. It is an internal obstacle that makes the want hard to achieve. Often the flaw and the want are tied together: the thing a character must overcome is tangled up with what they want most.

Example

The chef is brilliant but arrogant, and she refuses to accept help. Her arrogance repeatedly threatens the success she is chasing, until she learns to trust her team.

Useful flaws have a cost. Stubbornness, fear of failure, pride, jealousy and a habit of not trusting people are all flaws that can damage the character's plans and relationships. A flaw gives a character somewhere to grow. Without one, there is no arc, only a person to whom things happen.

Characters also need strengths. Readers do not need a character to be perfect. They need to see them try, to see why they act as they do, and to understand what is at stake.

4.Backstory: Just Enough, Not Everything

Backstory is what happened before the story began. It explains why the character wants what they want and why their flaw exists. The common mistake is to dump all of it on the page at once. Readers need only the fragments that matter in the moment. The rest can stay in the writer's notes.

Example

Instead of a full paragraph about the chef's difficult childhood, one line does the same work: "She had learned young that asking for help meant admitting you had already failed."

Good ways to reveal backstory in small pieces are a line of dialogue, an object the character keeps, a reaction that seems too strong for the moment, or a short memory that interrupts the action.

5.Showing Character Through Action and Choice

You can say that a character is brave, or you can show them doing something brave. Showing is more convincing. The best moments for showing character are choices made under pressure, because when something is at risk, people reveal what they value. This is the same idea as in Show, Don't Tell.

TraitToldShown
NervousShe was nervous.She checked the exits twice and kept her bag on her knees.
GenerousHe was generous.He gave away the last sandwich and said he was not hungry.
ProudShe was too proud to ask.She spent an hour struggling with the lock rather than call for help.
DishonestHe was a liar.He answered quickly, before the question was finished.

6.Voice and Point of View

Voice is the distinct way a character thinks, speaks and notices the world. Two characters in the same situation should react differently, and describe their reactions differently. Word choice, rhythm and what a character notices all shape their voice.

Example

A nervous character might notice the exits in a room, while a confident one might notice who is watching them. Same room, different observations, different voice.

For how to write speech that sounds different for each person, see Dialogue and Voice. The choice of point of view, such as first person ("I") or third person ("she"), controls how close the reader gets to a character's thoughts. See Author's Purpose, Tone and Point of View for more.

7.The Arc: How They Change

A character arc follows the internal change a character goes through while they pursue their external goal. Most arcs fall into a few broad shapes.

Three kinds of character arc
Watch each line draw itself from the beginning to the end of the story.

Diagram 1. A positive arc rises after a low point. A negative arc falls. A flat arc stays level.

Arc typeWhat happensExample
Positive changeThe character overcomes the flaw and growsLearns to trust others
Negative changeThe flaw wins and the character declinesAmbition turns into ruin
Flat arcThe character holds firm and changes the world around themA steady mentor who keeps to their beliefs

Be careful with the words "flat character" and "flat arc". A flat character is simple and has few sides. A flat arc is a character who does not change inside, often because their belief is true and tested. A flat arc can belong to a very round character.

Change must be earned. A character who suddenly becomes kind on the last page does not convince the reader. Real change comes through repeated pressure, a crisis and a choice.

8.Supporting Characters and Villains

Minor characters should have their own small wants, so they do not exist only to help the hero. Give each important character a different voice, and use names that do not look alike on the page: "Sara" and "Sana" are easy to confuse. An antagonist should have a reason for what they do. A villain who is simply "evil" is less frightening and less interesting than one whose actions make sense to them, even if the reader disagrees.

9.Build a Character Sheet

A character sheet is a short list of notes that helps you keep a character consistent. Fill it in before you write, and change it as the story grows. You will not use every detail in the story.

FieldQuestion to answer
Name and roleWho are they in the story?
WantWhat do they consciously chase?
NeedWhat must they learn or face?
StakesWhat will they lose if they fail?
FearWhat are they afraid of?
FlawWhat gets in their own way?
StrengthWhat are they good at?
VoiceHow do they speak and think?
Habit or detailWhat small, specific thing do they do?
ArcDo they change, and how?

10.Worked Examples

Example A: Building a character from nothing

1Give a clear want. Imran, aged 15, wants to win the school robotics contest.
2Set the stakes. If his team loses, the robotics club may be closed.
3Choose a flaw that blocks the want. He refuses help and wants all the credit.
4Add a backstory fragment. Two years ago he shared a project and watched someone else collect the prize.
5Give a voice and a habit. Short sentences. He counts screws when he is anxious.
6Plan the moment of change. Under time pressure, he accepts help from a teammate.

The scene.

Imran counted the screws again. Forty-two. The robot still twitched at the start line, and the clock above the judges' table said nine minutes left.

"Let me look," said Zara.

"I have got it." He pulled the panel closer. Two years ago he had shared a project and watched someone else collect the prize. He had not shared one since.

The robot twitched again. Forty-two screws. Nine minutes. He looked at the pliers in Zara's hand, and then at the clock.

"Fine," he said, and the word cost him more than all the screws. "Hold this."

Notice that the passage never says "Imran was proud". His pride shows in his refusal, his backstory appears in two sentences, and his change happens in a single choice.

Example B: Bringing a flat character to life

Flat version. "Maya was kind, clever and popular. Everyone liked her."

1Give her a want. Maya wants to win the school debating cup.
2Give her a flaw. She cannot admit when she is wrong.
3Show it through a choice. A teammate points out a mistake in her speech.
4Add a telling detail. She uses a polite smile to avoid the subject.

Revised version.

Maya smiled when Hamza pointed out the mistake in her speech. It was the smile she used when a teacher called her name. "That is a good point," she said, and moved her notes to the other side of the table. She did not change a word.

Try it yourself: turn "Omar was brave" into a scene that shows it. Sample answer: When the lights went out, Omar's hands were shaking, but he stepped in front of the younger children. "Stay behind me," he said, "and hold the rail."

11.Character in Short Exam Stories

In an exam you may have only a few paragraphs, so keep your characters simple. Use one main character with one clear want, one flaw or fear, and one telling detail. Show them making one important choice. Avoid a crowd of characters and long backstory, and keep the point of view consistent. A short story with a single strong character is usually more effective than one with many thin ones.

12.Common Mistakes

  • Listing traits instead of showing them. "She was kind and clever" tells the reader nothing about the person.
  • A character with no want. Events happen to them, and the reader has no reason to follow.
  • A perfect character. Without a flaw, there is nothing to overcome.
  • Too much backstory at once. It slows the story and tells the reader more than they need.
  • Behaviour that does not fit. A cautious character who suddenly takes a huge risk needs a reason.
  • Sudden change. Growth that happens in one line is not believed.
  • Everyone sounds the same. Cover the names and see if you can tell who is speaking.
  • A villain with no reason. The antagonist should make sense from their own point of view.

13.Common Myths About Characters

MythA character is their description.
FactA character is shown mainly by what they want, choose and do.
MythA likeable character must be perfect.
FactReaders often connect more with characters who try, fail and try again.
MythEvery character must change in every story.
FactA flat arc is a valid choice when the character stays true and the world changes.
MythVillains must be purely evil.
FactBelievable antagonists usually have understandable reasons for what they do.

14.Key Terms

Protagonist: the main character of the story.
Antagonist: the character or force that opposes the protagonist.
Foil: a character who contrasts with another to bring out their qualities.
Desire (want): the goal a character consciously pursues.
Need: what a character must learn or face in order to grow.
Stakes: what the character stands to lose.
Flaw: an inner weakness that blocks the character.
Backstory: events that happened before the story begins.
Voice: the way a character thinks, speaks and notices.
Character arc: the inner change a character goes through.
Flat and round character: a simple, one-sided character, and a complex, many-sided one.
Point of view: the angle from which the story is told.

15.Why This Matters

Story Structure gives a story its shape, but character is what makes readers feel that the shape matters. A well-plotted story with an empty main character still feels flat, while even a simple plot can stay in the mind when it is driven by a character the reader wants to see succeed. After you draft, use Editing Your Own Work to check that each character acts consistently and has a distinct voice.

Key points to remember
  • A strong character has a clear want, a flaw that gets in the way, and stakes.
  • Round characters have several sides. Flat characters are simple and work best in small roles.
  • Reveal character through choices made under pressure, not through lists of traits.
  • Give backstory in small pieces, when it matters.
  • An arc can be positive, negative or flat, and change must be earned.
  • Give each character a different voice, and make villains understandable.
For teachers and parents

Activity: ask students to fill in the character sheet for a person from a story they know, then for a new character of their own. Then ask them to write a short scene in which the character must make a choice that shows their flaw, and let a partner guess the want and the flaw from the scene alone.

Please note: all examples here are original, written for teaching. The want and need model and the three arc shapes are common craft tools, and writers and teachers use them in slightly different ways.
