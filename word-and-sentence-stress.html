Word & Sentence Stress Patterns

English is a stress-timed language — and stress placement changes meaning, part of speech, and how natural you sound. A closer look at the rules and patterns.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 12 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | English Language

Say the word "photograph", then "photographic", then "photography". The loudest, longest part of the word moves each time, even though the root hardly changes. That movement is not random. It follows patterns that you can learn.

English words have a shape made of strong and weak syllables, and English sentences have a rhythm made of strong and weak words. Both are called stress. Learning where stress falls helps you speak so that people understand you easily, and it helps you understand fast speech too.

This guide explains word stress, the rules for common word endings, noun and verb pairs, sentence stress and rhythm, and how moving the stress changes meaning. It ends with worked examples, a glossary and ten practice questions.

🔊 Tap a "Listen" button to hear a word or phrase read by your device's built-in voice. Voices differ between phones and browsers, so use them as a guide and check difficult words in a dictionary.

1.What Is Stress?

A syllable is one beat of sound in a word. "Banana" has three syllables: ba-na-na. In most English words with two or more syllables, one syllable is stressed. It is said with more force: it is usually longer, a little louder, often higher in pitch, and it has a clear vowel. The other syllables are unstressed. They are shorter and quieter, and their vowels are often weak.

The most common weak vowel is called the schwa. It is the short "uh" sound at the start of "about" and at the end of "sofa". Many unstressed syllables in English contain a schwa.

Example

ba-NA-na: the middle syllable is stressed. The first and last syllables are short and weak, close to "buh-NAH-nuh".

Dictionaries show stress with a small mark (ˈ) placed before the stressed syllable. For example, the verb "record" is written /rɪˈkɔːd/. The mark comes before "kɔːd", so the second syllable is stressed.

Stress matters because listeners recognise words partly by their stress pattern. If you put the stress on the wrong syllable, a word can be hard to recognise even when every sound is correct.

2.English Rhythm: Stress-Timing

English is often described as a stress-timed language. This means that stressed syllables tend to come at fairly regular intervals, and the unstressed syllables are squeezed into the gaps between them. Many other languages, such as Spanish, are usually described as syllable-timed, where syllables take more equal amounts of time.

The diagram below shows the idea. Sentence A has three syllables and sentence B has six, but both have three stressed beats. In normal speech, B does not take twice as long as A, because the small words are said quickly between the beats.

Same beats, different number of syllables
Watch the gold rings pulse on beats 1, 2 and 3.

Diagram 1. Red words carry the beat. Grey words are unstressed and are said quickly between the beats.

Researchers who measure real speech have found that the timing is not perfectly even. So think of stress-timing as a strong tendency, not a stopwatch rule. The practical lesson is the same: do not give every syllable the same length and weight.

Try it

Tap a steady beat on your desk, three taps in all. Say "Dogs eat bones" on the taps. Then say "The dogs will eat the bones" on the same three taps, fitting "The", "will" and "the" between them.

3.Word Stress: Patterns, Not One Fixed Rule

Some languages always stress the same position in a word. English does not. The position changes from word to word, but many word endings (suffixes) follow reliable patterns. Look again at the "photo" family.

PHO-to-graph (noun)STRESS-weak-weak
pho-to-GRAPH-ic (adjective)weak-weak-STRESS-weak
pho-TOG-ra-phy (noun)weak-STRESS-weak-weak

The root is the same, but the stress moves because the ending changes. Adding -ic puts the stress on the syllable just before it. Words ending in -graphy also put the stress on the syllable just before the ending: pho-TOG-ra-phy, ge-OG-ra-phy, bi-OG-ra-phy.

The red syllable is stressed.

These are guides, not laws, and there are exceptions. For example, "television" is TEL-e-vi-sion, with the stress at the start, even though it ends in -sion. When you meet a new word, check a dictionary.

Stress also changes when one word grows into a family. Notice how it moves in these sets: e-CON-o-my and ec-o-NOM-ic; PER-son, PER-son-al and per-son-AL-i-ty. Knowing the suffix rules lets you predict most of these shifts. You can read more about word endings in Prefixes, Suffixes and Root Words.

Two sources, two answers

Not everyone stresses every word the same way. "Advertisement" is ad-VER-tise-ment in British English and ad-ver-TISE-ment in American English. "Laboratory" is la-BOR-a-try in British English and LAB-ra-tor-y in American English. Both are correct. Choose one accent and stay consistent, and use a dictionary that shows the accent you want.

4.The Noun and Verb Stress Shift

In some two-syllable words, the noun and the verb are spelled the same but stressed differently. The noun is usually stressed on the first syllable and the verb on the second. The stress is what tells the listener which word you mean.

REC-ord (noun: a written or recorded account)NOUN: first syllable
re-CORD (verb: to store sound or information)VERB: second syllable
Noun (first syllable)Verb (second syllable)
PRES-ent (a gift)pre-SENT (to give or show)
OB-ject (a thing)ob-JECT (to say you disagree)
IN-crease (a rise)in-CREASE (to grow)
DES-ert (dry sandy land)de-SERT (to abandon)
PROD-uce (fruit and vegetables)pro-DUCE (to make)
IM-port (goods brought in)im-PORT (to bring goods in)
CON-tract (an agreement)con-TRACT (to become smaller)
PER-mit (an official paper)per-MIT (to allow)

Do not apply this as a rule for every word. Many two-syllable words keep the same stress as both noun and verb, such as "visit", "answer" and "promise". Also, in many two-syllable words, nouns and adjectives take first-syllable stress (TA-ble, HAP-py) and verbs take second-syllable stress (be-GIN, ad-MIT), but there are many exceptions, such as the nouns "ho-TEL" and "ma-CHINE". Treat the pattern as a useful tendency.

5.Sentence Stress: Content Words and Function Words

In a sentence, stress works on whole words. Content words carry the main meaning, and they are usually stressed. Function words do grammar jobs, and they are usually unstressed and said quickly.

Content words (usually stressed)Function words (usually unstressed)
Nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question wordsArticles (a, the), prepositions (of, to, at), pronouns (he, them), auxiliary verbs (is, have, can), conjunctions (and, but)
Example

"I WENT to the STORE to BUY some BREAD." The four stressed words carry the message. The rest is squeezed between them.

Unstressed function words often change their sound. They use weak forms, usually with a schwa. In normal speech, "to" sounds like "tuh", "of" like "uhv", and "and" is often just a quick "n". This is why "bread and butter" can sound like "bread 'n' butter". It is natural, not careless.

In a neutral statement, the last content word often carries the strongest stress: "She bought some fresh FRUIT." Function words can be stressed when you want to stress them, for example: "I did NOT take it." or "I CAN'T come."

6.Moving the Stress to Change the Meaning

You can choose which word to stress in order to show what you mean. The words stay the same, but the message changes. The sentence below can have seven readings, depending on which single word is stressed.

"I didn't say she stole the money."
Stress one word at a time. The meaning changes each time.
I didn't say she stole the money.
Someone else said it.
I DIDN'T say she stole the money.
I strongly deny saying it.
I didn't SAY she stole the money.
I may have hinted or written it, but I did not say it aloud.
I didn't say SHE stole the money.
Someone else stole it.
I didn't say she STOLE the money.
She did something else with it, such as borrow it.
I didn't say she stole THE money.
She stole some other money. (This reading is less common.)
I didn't say she stole the MONEY.
She stole something else.

A built-in device voice cannot be told to stress one chosen word, so there are no Listen buttons here. Read the sentences aloud yourself, and ask a friend which meaning they hear.

7.Compound Nouns and Adjective Plus Noun

Stress can also separate a single compound noun from a phrase of two words. A compound noun is two words joined into one idea. Its stress is usually on the first part. In a phrase of an adjective and a noun, the stress is usually on the noun.

Compound noun (first part stressed)Adjective + noun (noun stressed)
BLACK-board: the board in a classroom (it can be green)black BOARD: any board that is black
GREEN-house: a glass building for plantsgreen HOUSE: a house painted green
HOT dog: a sausage in a bunhot DOG: a dog that is too warm

8.Worked Examples

Example A: Finding the stress in "electricity"

1Split it into syllables. e-lec-tric-i-ty. That is five syllables.
2Find the ending. The word ends in -ity.
3Apply the rule. For -ity words, the stress falls on the syllable just before the ending. That syllable is "tric".
4Check and say it. e-lec-TRIC-i-ty. Confirm it in a dictionary, then say the word aloud with a clear beat on "TRIC".

Try it yourself: mark the stress in "politician" and "popularity". Sample answer: po-li-TI-cian (the ending is -ian) and pop-u-LAR-i-ty (the ending is -ity).

Example B: Marking sentence stress

1Read the sentence. "She bought some fresh fruit at the market."
2Find the content words. bought (verb), fresh (adjective), fruit (noun), market (noun).
3Mark the rest as weak. She, some, at and the are function words.
4Say it on a beat. She BOUGHT some FRESH FRUIT at the MARKET. Tap four beats and fit the small words between them.

Try it yourself: mark "The children played football in the garden." Sample answer: The CHIL-dren PLAYED FOOT-ball in the GAR-den. The content words are children, played, football and garden. Each has its stressed syllable marked.

9.Common Mistakes

  • Stressing every syllable or word equally. This makes speech sound flat and robotic, and it is hard to follow. Let the weak syllables be weak.
  • Stressing small function words. Saying "I WENT TO THE STORE" with a beat on every word hides the important words.
  • Using the wrong stress on word endings. For example, stressing the first syllable of "in-for-MA-tion" or "eco-NOM-ic".
  • Ignoring weak vowels. Saying every vowel in full, as in spelling, makes words sound unnatural. Use the schwa where English does.
  • Learning one rule and applying it to every word. Stress rules are patterns with exceptions, so check difficult words.

10.How to Practise

  • Check stress in a dictionary. Every time you learn a new long word, look for the stress mark and listen to the recording.
  • Tap the beats. Tap on each stressed syllable or word and keep the taps steady while you speak.
  • Mark a short text. Underline the content words in a paragraph, then read it aloud.
  • Record yourself. Use your phone, listen back, and compare with a recording of a fluent speaker.
  • Use rhythm material. Rhymes, poems and chants have strong beats and are good for practising stress.

For more practice, read Fluency Building Techniques, Common Pronunciation Mistakes and Spoken English and Pronunciation.

11.Common Myths About Stress

MythStress just means saying a syllable louder.
FactStress usually combines length, loudness, pitch and a clearer vowel.
MythEnglish has one simple stress rule.
FactEnglish has useful patterns, such as those for suffixes, but also many exceptions. Dictionaries are the final check.
MythAll native speakers stress words in the same way.
FactAccents differ. "Advertisement" is stressed differently in British and American English.
MythStress only matters in pronunciation exams.
FactStress helps you understand fast speech and helps others understand you in everyday life.

12.Key Terms

Syllable: one beat of sound in a word. "Banana" has three.
Stress: extra force on a syllable or word, usually through length, loudness, pitch and a clear vowel.
Unstressed: weaker and shorter, often with a weak vowel.
Schwa: the weak "uh" vowel in "about" and "sofa".
Suffix: an ending added to a word, such as -ic or -tion.
Stress-timed: a description of English rhythm in which stressed syllables come at fairly regular intervals.
Content words: words that carry the main meaning, such as nouns, main verbs, adjectives and adverbs.
Function words: small grammar words such as the, of, to, and can.
Compound noun: two words joined into one idea, such as greenhouse.

13.Why This Matters

Stress is part of how English carries meaning. Correct word stress helps people recognise your words. Correct sentence stress shows what is important and keeps your speech in a natural rhythm. Together with the pitch patterns in Intonation in Conversation and the habits in Fluency Building Techniques, stress is what makes practised speech sound natural instead of memorised.

Key points to remember
  • A stressed syllable is longer, louder, often higher, and has a clear vowel. Unstressed syllables are weak.
  • English rhythm is built around stressed beats, with small words squeezed in between.
  • For -ic, -tion, -ity and -graphy words, the stress falls on the syllable just before the ending.
  • Many noun and verb pairs change stress: REC-ord (noun) and re-CORD (verb).
  • In sentences, content words are stressed and function words are weak.
  • Moving the stress changes the meaning, so use it on purpose. Check difficult words in a dictionary.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give learners five short sentences and ask them to underline the content words and tap the beats while reading. Then ask them to say the same sentence with the stress on different words and let a partner say what meaning they heard.

Please note: all examples here are original, written for teaching. Pronunciations follow British English, and learners may meet other accents, which is normal.
