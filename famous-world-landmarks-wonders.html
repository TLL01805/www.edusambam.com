Famous World Landmarks & Wonders

The ancient wonders and how each was lost, UNESCO World Heritage, famous landmarks of every continent, and the treasures of Pakistan and South Asia.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 22 min read | General Knowledge |Last updated: October 2026

Some structures are so large, so old or so beautiful that people have been naming them "wonders" for more than 2,000 years. This article tells the story of the original Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, why only one survives, how UNESCO's World Heritage list came about, the great landmarks of each continent, and the treasures of Pakistan and South Asia, including Mohenjo-daro, Taxila, Rohtas Fort and the Lahore Fort. The 2007 "New Seven Wonders" vote has its own article, so here it gets only a short summary.

In this article

Wonders, landmarks and heritage sites · the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World and how each was lost · the 2007 vote in brief · UNESCO World Heritage · landmarks of the world · the heritage of Pakistan and South Asia · myths corrected · four worked examples · key terms · practice questions and a quiz.

1.Wonders, Landmarks and Heritage Sites

These three terms overlap but are not the same.

  • A wonder belongs to a famous list, such as the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. Lists are chosen by particular people at particular times.
  • A landmark is any easily recognised structure or place that stands for a city or country, such as the Eiffel Tower for Paris.
  • A World Heritage Site is a place officially listed by UNESCO as having outstanding value for all humanity.

2.The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World

The idea of a list of "wonders" (in Greek, theamata, "things to be seen") comes from Greek writers of the Hellenistic era, among them the poet Antipater of Sidon and the engineer Philon of Byzantium, who noted the most impressive sights known to travellers around the Mediterranean and the Near East. The number seven was popular in the ancient world, and the list became fixed at seven. Only one of the seven survives.

Figure 1. Roughly how long each wonder stood, in years. Dates are approximate and some are debated.

1. The Great Pyramid of Giza (Egypt)

Built for the pharaoh Khufu (Cheops) about 2560 BC, it is the oldest of the seven and the only one still standing. It was originally about 147 m high and is about 139 m today, because its smooth outer casing and the very top have been lost. It is estimated to contain about 2.3 million blocks of stone, and for roughly 3,800 years it was the tallest human-made structure in the world, until the spire of Lincoln Cathedral in England was completed in 1311.

2. The Hanging Gardens of Babylon (Iraq)

Later writers described terraced gardens built by King Nebuchadnezzar II about 600 BC, but no archaeological remains have ever been confirmed, and ancient accounts disagree. Some scholars, such as the Assyriologist Stephanie Dalley, suggest the gardens described were actually in the Assyrian city of Nineveh, built by King Sennacherib. Historians still debate whether they existed as described.

3. The Temple of Artemis at Ephesus (Turkey)

A huge marble temple to the goddess Artemis, begun about 550 BC. It was burned down in 356 BC by a man named Herostratus who wanted fame, rebuilt on a grander scale, and finally destroyed, traditionally by the Goths in AD 262. Only the foundations and one re-erected column remain.

4. The Statue of Zeus at Olympia (Greece)

A seated statue about 12 m high made by the sculptor Phidias, around 435 BC, from ivory and gold over a wooden frame. It stood in the temple at Olympia, where the ancient Olympic Games were held. It was lost sometime in late antiquity, probably in the 5th century AD.

5. The Mausoleum at Halicarnassus (Turkey)

The tomb of Mausolus, ruler of the region of Caria, built around 350 BC. Its name gave us the word mausoleum. Earthquakes brought it down over the centuries, and in the 1400s the Knights of St John used its stones to build a castle at Bodrum.

6. The Colossus of Rhodes (Greece)

A bronze statue of the sun god Helios, about 33 m high, completed about 280 BC to celebrate the island's survival of a siege. It was toppled by an earthquake around 226 BC, after standing for only about 54 years. Its broken pieces lay there for centuries.

7. The Lighthouse of Alexandria (Egypt)

Built on the island of Pharos about 280 BC, it was one of the tallest structures in the world, reaching about 100 m or more according to ancient accounts. It guided ships for centuries until earthquakes damaged it in 956, 1303 and 1323. In 1477 the Sultan of Egypt built Qaitbay Fort on the site, using stones from the lighthouse.

Worked example 1: Cleopatra, the Pyramid and the Moon

Question: Cleopatra died in 30 BC. Was she closer in time to the building of the Great Pyramid (about 2560 BC) or to the Moon landing (AD 1969)?

Step 1. From the pyramid to Cleopatra: 2560 − 30 = 2,530 years.

Step 2. From Cleopatra to the Moon landing: 30 + 1969 = 1,999, minus 1 because there is no year 0, so about 1,998 years.

Answer: She lived about 2,500 years after the pyramid, but only about 2,000 years before the Moon landing, so she was closer in time to the Moon landing.

3.The 2007 Vote in Brief

In 2007 a private foundation called New7Wonders held a worldwide public vote and announced a new list: the Great Wall of China, Petra, the Colosseum, Chichen Itza, Machu Picchu, the Taj Mahal and Christ the Redeemer, with the Great Pyramid of Giza as an honorary candidate. The vote was not run by UNESCO. The full story, with maps, timelines and each site in depth, is in our article The Seven Wonders of the World.

4.UNESCO World Heritage

UNESCO (the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization) adopted the World Heritage Convention in 1972, and the first sites were listed in 1978. Today the list has more than 1,200 sites in about 170 countries. Sites fall into three groups: cultural (such as temples and ancient cities), natural (such as national parks and coral reefs) and mixed. A site can also be placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger if it is threatened by war, disaster, neglect or development.

Being listed does not make a site belong to UNESCO. It remains the responsibility of the country where it is located, but listing brings international attention, expertise and sometimes funding for protection.

Worked example 2: How many Great Pyramids tall?

Question: The Burj Khalifa is about 828 m tall and the Great Pyramid is about 139 m tall today. How many times taller is the Burj Khalifa?

Step 1. Divide: 828 ÷ 139 ≈ 5.96.

Answer: About 6 times taller. Some very tall modern buildings are so much taller than ancient wonders that the pyramid held the "tallest" title for thousands of years, until about the 14th century.

6.The Heritage of Pakistan and South Asia

Pakistan has six UNESCO World Heritage Sites, all cultural.

Figure 4. Pakistan's six World Heritage Sites.

Mohenjo-daro is among the best known. It was one of the largest cities of the Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilisation, which flourished in what is now Pakistan and north-west India about 4,500 years ago. Excavations began in the 1920s. Its straight streets, brick houses and covered drains show advanced town planning, and its writing has still not been deciphered.

Other famous buildings in Pakistan include the Badshahi Mosque in Lahore (built 1671–73 under the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb), the Minar-e-Pakistan in Lahore, which commemorates the 1940 Lahore Resolution, and the Faisal Mosque in Islamabad, completed in 1986, whose four 90 m minarets make it one of the most recognisable modern buildings in the country.

Worked example 3: How old is Mohenjo-daro?

Question: If Mohenjo-daro was built about 2500 BC, about how many years ago was that, in 2026?

Step 1. Add the BC year to the current year: 2500 + 2026 = 4,526.

Step 2. Subtract 1, because there is no year 0 between 1 BC and AD 1: 4,525.

Answer: About 4,500 years ago, nearly as old as the Great Pyramid.

7.Myths Corrected

"Napoleon's soldiers shot the nose off the Sphinx"

A very popular story told to tourists in Egypt.

The fix: Drawings of the Sphinx made decades before Napoleon's arrival in 1798 already show the nose missing. Medieval writers described its damage, and some accounts blame a 14th-century religious zealot. Napoleon's troops did not cause it.

"The pyramids were built by slaves"

This idea appears in films and popular books.

The fix: Archaeologists have found the remains of a workers' town near the Giza pyramids, with bakeries, housing and tombs. Evidence suggests the builders were organised Egyptian labour teams who were fed and cared for, and some were buried near the pyramids with honour. The exact organisation is still being studied.

"The Hanging Gardens of Babylon are a proven historical fact"

They appear on every list of the ancient wonders.

The fix: No archaeological remains of the gardens have ever been confirmed in Babylon, and the ancient descriptions were written long after they were supposed to exist. Some historians think the gardens are a real place described incorrectly, perhaps in Nineveh, and others think they are partly legend.

Worked example 4: How fast was the Great Pyramid built?

Question: If the Great Pyramid has about 2.3 million blocks and took about 27 years, how many blocks were placed per year, and per day?

Step 1. Per year: 2,300,000 ÷ 27 ≈ 85,200.

Step 2. Per day: 85,200 ÷ 365 ≈ 233.

Answer: About 85,000 blocks a year, or more than 230 a day, on average. (These are estimates, because both the number of blocks and the building time are uncertain.)

Real-World Example

When a heritage site is damaged, for example by an earthquake, flood or war, the world often helps to rebuild it. The 1960s rescue of the Abu Simbel temples, which were cut into blocks and moved to higher ground before the Aswan High Dam flooded the valley, helped inspire the creation of UNESCO's World Heritage Convention.

Did You Know?

The word "mausoleum" comes from the name of Mausolus, the ruler for whom the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus was built. We still use his name for any grand tomb, including the Taj Mahal.

9.Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up the ancient Seven Wonders and the 2007 New Seven Wonders.
  • Saying more than one of the ancient wonders survives. Only the Great Pyramid does.
  • Treating UNESCO as the owner of heritage sites. The countries own and protect them.
  • Thinking the Taj Mahal is the only Mughal wonder. Lahore has major Mughal works too, including the Lahore Fort, the Shalimar Gardens and the Badshahi Mosque.
  • Repeating stories such as the Sphinx's nose or the "slave-built" pyramids as facts.

10.Summary

  • The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World were compiled by Greek writers; only the Great Pyramid survives.
  • The 2007 New Seven Wonders were chosen by a public vote, not by UNESCO.
  • UNESCO's World Heritage list began in 1972 and now has more than 1,200 sites.
  • Pakistan has six World Heritage Sites: Mohenjo-daro, Taxila, Takht-i-Bahi, the Lahore Fort and Shalimar Gardens, Makli and Rohtas Fort.
  • Many popular stories about famous monuments are myths.
