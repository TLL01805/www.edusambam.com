Commonly Confused Words in English: Meanings and Examples

Affect or effect? Principal or principle? Stationary or stationery? Learn the meanings, spellings and memory tricks that stop the most common word mix-ups.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 24 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Vocabulary

Is it affect or effect? Principal or principle? Stationary or stationery? Confused words are among the most common causes of mistakes in essays, emails and examinations, and spell-checkers do not catch them, because each wrong word is still a real word.

This guide explains why words are confused, the main types of confusion, and how to tell each pair apart. Its main part gives 212 words in three groups (sound-alike words, look-alike words and confusions of usage), each with its meaning and an example sentence. It also includes memory tricks, a spot-the-error table, key points, ten practice questions and a teacher activity.

It builds on Parts of Speech and connects with Common Errors in English, Singular and Plural Nouns and Idioms in English. Spelling in this guide follows British English.

1.Why Are Words Confused?

Words become confused because they sound the same, look similar or have related meanings. Many came into English from different languages and ended up sounding alike, and some are separated only by a single letter.

Type of confusionWhat it meansExamples
HomophonesWords that sound the same but differ in spelling and meaningmeat / meet, peace / piece, their / there
Near-homophonesWords that sound almost the sameaccept / except, affect / effect, quiet / quite
Look-alikesWords that look alike but have different meaningsadapt / adopt, emigrate / immigrate, eminent / imminent
HomographsWords with the same spelling but different meanings or soundslead (a metal) and lead (to guide)
Usage confusionsWords with related meanings used in different wayslend / borrow, fewer / less, between / among

2.How to Avoid Confusion

  • Check the part of speech. Is the word a noun, a verb or an adjective? Advice (noun) and advise (verb) depend on this.
  • Learn pairs together. Study both words side by side, with an example sentence for each.
  • Use memory tricks. Link the spelling to a clue, as the table in section 6 shows.
  • Read your work aloud, then check each homophone separately.
  • Do not rely on spell-check. It accepts any word that exists.
  • Keep a personal list of the pairs that you confuse most often and revise it weekly.

6.Memory Tricks for the Hardest Pairs

PairMemory trick
principal / principleThe principal is your pal; a principle is a rule.
stationary / stationeryStationery has an e, as in envelope; stationary has an a, as in stand.
desert / dessertDessert has two s's, because you want a second helping.
affect / effectAffect is an action (a verb); effect is the end result (a noun).
complement / complimentA complement completes; a compliment is kind, like I like praise.
advice / adviseAdvice is a noun, like ice; advise is a verb, like wise.
practice / practiseIn British English, the noun has c, like advice; the verb has s, like advise.
their / thereThere has here inside it, so it points to a place.
loose / loseLoose has a loose extra o; lose has lost one o.
weather / whetherWeather has eat in it, and the weather has an effect on how we eat; whether asks a choice, like which.
to / too / twoToo has too many o's; two is a number and has a w, like twin.

7.Spot the Error

Each sentence below contains a confused word. The right-hand columns give the correction and the reason.

IncorrectCorrectWhy
The weather will effect our plans.The weather will affect our plans.Affect is the verb.
She gave me good advise.She gave me good advice.Advice is the noun.
I need some new stationary.I need some new stationery.Stationery means paper and pens.
The principle of the school spoke first.The principal of the school spoke first.Principal means head.
Their going to arrive late.They're going to arrive late.Use they're for they are.
Its a lovely day.It's a lovely day.Use it's for it is.
Who's book is this?Whose book is this?Whose shows belonging.
I will loose my keys.I will lose my keys.Lose is the verb.
Can you borrow me a pen?Can you lend me a pen?You lend to others and borrow from others.
There were less students.There were fewer students.Students can be counted.
The amount of students grew.The number of students grew.Students can be counted.
He is taller then me.He is taller than me.Use than in comparisons.
She was an eminent danger.She was in imminent danger.Imminent means about to happen.
They flaunt the rules.They flout the rules.Flout means to disobey.
He was suppose to come.He was supposed to come.Use the past participle.
I could of won.I could have won.Use have, not of.
The affects were severe.The effects were severe.Effects is the noun.
I am quiet sure.I am quite sure.Quite means fairly or completely.
She practiced the piano (British).She practised the piano.British verb: practise.
Lay down for a rest.Lie down for a rest.Lie needs no object.
Key points to remember
  • Confused words are real words used in the wrong place, so spell-check will not catch them.
  • Decide the part of speech first: advice (noun) and advise (verb), practice (noun) and practise (verb) in British English.
  • Learn the words in pairs, with an example sentence for each.
  • Use memory tricks for the hardest pairs, such as principal/principle and stationary/stationery.
  • Use fewer with countable nouns and less with uncountable nouns.
  • Read your writing aloud and check every homophone.
For teachers and parents

Activity: write ten sentences with the confused word missing, such as The ___ of the school gave a speech, and ask students to choose between two words and say why. Then ask them to write their own sentences for three pairs and to read them aloud to a partner, who decides whether the right word was used.

Please note: this guide uses British spelling. Some pairs differ between British and American English, for example practice / practise and storey / story, and some words are used differently in informal speech.
