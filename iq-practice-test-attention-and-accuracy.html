About this attention and accuracy test

Careful attention and accuracy support almost every kind of learning. This test has you count letters and digits, compare codes, apply simple coding rules, follow multi-step instructions and check order. All the information stays on screen, so it measures concentration and care, not memory.

How to get the most from it

Slow down on the counting and comparison items, because mistakes come from rushing. Tick or point to each item as you count. If you cannot be sure, count again from the start.

What this test practises

Attention here means keeping your mind on a small task long enough to finish it without slips. In school it decides whether you copy a number correctly, notice the word “not” in a question, or catch a mistake in your own working. The items are short and rule-based, so a wrong answer usually comes from a lapse, not from missing knowledge. That makes this test a useful way to see how careful you are when nobody is checking over your shoulder.

A worked example

Question A: How many times does the digit 7 appear in 4 7 1 7 7 3 9 7 2?

Answer: 4. Touch each digit in turn and say the count aloud: the 7s are the second, fourth, fifth and eighth digits. Then count a second time from the start to confirm.

Question B: In a code, each letter is replaced by the next letter of the alphabet. What is the code for DOG?

Answer: EPH. Apply the rule to one letter at a time: D becomes E, O becomes P and G becomes H.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Counting by eye without marking, which makes you lose your place in a long string.
  • Reading only half of a multi-step instruction and then doing the steps in the wrong order.
  • Changing the rule halfway through a coding question without noticing.
  • Rushing the last few questions because the timer is running.

Attention improves when you practise the exact skill, for example by checking your homework line by line. For more on puzzles and memory games, see Brain Training: Puzzles, Memory and Logic Skills.

What your score does and does not mean

Your mark out of 100 shows how you did on these 50 questions on one day. It is not an IQ number, because a real IQ score comes from a standardised test given and interpreted by a qualified psychologist and compared with a large norm group. Use the section scores to choose what to practise, and retake the test after a week or two to see progress. Tiredness, noise and stress change results, so one low score is not a verdict on your ability.
