Animals & Ecosystems: Wildlife GK

From conservation success stories in Pakistan's mountains to the true reason chameleons change colour — the wildlife facts, records, and ecosystem basics that show up everywhere in GK tests.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 20 min read | General Knowledge |Last updated: October 2026

A single mountain valley in northern Pakistan can hold grass, insects, birds, wild goats, a rare cat and the soil bacteria that recycle them all. These living things, together with the water, rock and weather around them, form an ecosystem. This article explains how ecosystems work, how energy moves through them, why some species disappear and others recover, and how conservation status is measured, with a strong focus on the wildlife of Pakistan and South Asia.

In this article

What an ecosystem is · food chains, webs and energy · keystone species and relationships · biomes · biodiversity and threats · the IUCN Red List · conservation tools and success stories · wildlife of Pakistan · animal records · myths · four worked examples · key terms · practice questions and a quiz.

1.What Is an Ecosystem?

An ecosystem is a community of living things (biotic factors) interacting with their non-living surroundings (abiotic factors such as water, soil, sunlight, temperature and rock). Ecosystems can be as small as a pond or as large as a rainforest.

Ecologists describe life in levels, each one made of the one before it:

  • Organism — one living thing, such as a single markhor.
  • Population — all the members of one species in an area.
  • Community — all the populations living together.
  • Ecosystem — the community plus its non-living environment.
  • Biome — a large region with a typical climate and typical life, such as desert or tundra.
  • Biosphere — all of the places on Earth where life exists.

Two related ideas often get confused. A habitat is where an organism lives (its "address"). A niche is its role in the ecosystem (its "job"): what it eats, what eats it, and how it affects its surroundings.

2.Food Chains, Food Webs and Energy

Energy enters almost every ecosystem as sunlight. Producers (plants, algae and some bacteria) capture it through photosynthesis and turn it into food. Consumers get energy by eating: primary consumers are herbivores, secondary consumers eat the herbivores, and apex predators sit at the top, with no natural predators of their own. Decomposers (fungi and bacteria) break down dead material and return nutrients to the soil.

A food chain shows one path of energy, for example grass → markhor → snow leopard. In reality, most animals eat several foods, so the paths link into a food web.

Figure 1. A simple mountain food web. Arrows show the flow of energy, from the one eaten to the one that eats it.

Each step in a food chain is called a trophic level. At each step, most of the energy is used up for living (movement, keeping warm, growing) or lost as heat. As a rough rule, only about 10% of the energy passes to the next level. This is why food chains are short, why there are far fewer predators than prey, and why top predators need a large territory.

Figure 2. An energy pyramid using the "10% rule" (a rough guide, not an exact law).
Worked example 1: The 10% rule

Question: A meadow's plants capture 10,000 kJ of energy. About how much reaches a snow leopard in the chain grass → markhor → snow leopard?

Step 1. Grass (producers) hold 10,000 kJ.

Step 2. The markhor (primary consumer) gets about 10%: 10,000 × 0.10 = 1,000 kJ.

Step 3. The snow leopard (secondary consumer) gets about 10% of that: 1,000 × 0.10 = 100 kJ.

Answer: About 100 kJ, only 1% of the original energy.

Worked example 2: What happens if one link is removed?

Question: What would happen in the web in Figure 1 if the snow leopard disappeared?

Step 1. Find what the snow leopard eats: markhor and ibex.

Step 2. With no predator, their populations may grow.

Step 3. More grazers eat more grass, so plants may be overgrazed, which can harm the soil and the other animals that depend on the plants.

Answer: The change spreads through the whole web. This is why every link matters.

3.Keystone Species and Relationships

A keystone species has a much bigger effect on its ecosystem than its numbers would suggest. Sea otters eat sea urchins, which protects kelp forests from being overgrazed. When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in the United States in the mid-1990s, scientists studied how they changed the behaviour of deer and the growth of plants along rivers, although the size of the effects is still debated.

Species also depend on each other in different ways, called symbiosis:

  • Mutualism: both benefit, such as bees and flowers (food for the bee, pollination for the flower), or clownfish and sea anemones.
  • Commensalism: one benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed.
  • Parasitism: one benefits at the other's expense, such as ticks on a mammal.

Competition (for food, water or space) and predation (one animal eating another) also shape who lives where.

Pakistan packs a wide range of these into one country: high mountains in the north, the Indus river plains, deserts such as the Thar and Cholistan, and a coast. The Indus Delta's mangrove forests are among the largest arid-climate mangrove forests in the world and shelter fish, crabs and migratory birds.

5.Biodiversity and the Threats to It

Biodiversity is the variety of life: the number of species, the variety of genes within them and the variety of ecosystems. About two million species have been formally described, and scientists think many more are still unknown. In 2019 a major UN-backed assessment (IPBES) warned that up to a million species may be threatened with extinction, many within decades unless action is taken.

The main causes are easy to remember with the word HIPPO:

  • Habitat loss: forests cleared, wetlands drained, land built on.
  • Invasive species: non-native species that outcompete or eat native ones.
  • Pollution: plastic, chemicals and waste in water, soil and air.
  • Population growth and demand for resources, including climate change.
  • Overharvesting: hunting, fishing or collecting faster than species can recover.

Extinction is natural over millions of years; the dinosaurs (other than birds) died out about 66 million years ago. But today's rate is far higher than the natural background rate. Well-known recent extinctions include the dodo (gone by the late 1600s), the passenger pigeon (the last one, Martha, died in 1914) and the thylacine or Tasmanian tiger (the last known one died in a zoo in 1936).

6.How Conservation Status Is Measured

The IUCN Red List is the world's most widely used system for classifying how threatened a species is. It looks at population size, trends, range and other evidence. The main categories, from least to most concerning:

CategoryMeaning
Least ConcernWidespread and abundant
Near ThreatenedLikely to become threatened soon
VulnerableHigh risk of extinction in the wild
EndangeredVery high risk of extinction in the wild
Critically EndangeredExtremely high risk of extinction in the wild
Extinct in the WildSurvives only in captivity
ExtinctNo individuals remain anywhere

Two further labels exist: Data Deficient (not enough information) and Not Evaluated.

Figure 3. Where some well-known species sit on the Red List scale (statuses as of the latest assessments we reviewed; the list is updated regularly).

Statuses can move in either direction, and they change as new evidence is reviewed. Always check the IUCN Red List website for the latest assessment.

7.Conservation Success Stories

Species can improve as well as decline.

  • The markhor, Pakistan's national animal, was moved from Endangered to Near Threatened in 2015. Conservationists credit community-based programmes in which local people receive a share of the fees from regulated trophy hunting, which gives them a reason to protect the animal.
  • The giant panda moved from Endangered to Vulnerable in 2016 after decades of habitat protection in China.
  • The snow leopard moved from Endangered to Vulnerable in 2017, although conservationists note the change reflects better data as much as population growth.
  • In October 2025, the IUCN moved the green turtle from Endangered to Least Concern after decades of work such as protecting nesting beaches and reducing accidental catches in fishing nets.
Real-World Example

Community-based conservation, like the trophy hunting programmes that helped the markhor recover, shows that success often depends as much on giving local communities a direct financial stake in a species' survival as on the biology itself.

The tools of conservation include protected areas such as national parks and wildlife sanctuaries; laws that restrict hunting and trade; CITES, an international treaty (agreed in 1973) that regulates trade in endangered species; the Ramsar Convention (1971), which protects wetlands; captive breeding and reintroduction; and education. In Pakistan, protected areas include Khunjerab National Park (home to the Marco Polo sheep and snow leopard), Deosai National Park (a high plateau with Himalayan brown bears), Chitral Gol National Park (markhor) and Hingol National Park on the Balochistan coast, which is among the country's largest.

Worked example 3: Who covers 1 km faster?

Question: How long does a cheetah (110 km/h) take to cover 1 km at top speed, compared with a peregrine falcon in a dive (300 km/h)?

Step 1. Time = distance ÷ speed. Cheetah: 1 ÷ 110 hours = 0.00909 hours.

Step 2. Convert to seconds: 0.00909 × 3,600 ≈ 33 seconds.

Step 3. Falcon: 1 ÷ 300 × 3,600 = 12 seconds.

Answer: About 33 seconds for the cheetah and 12 seconds for the falcon. (Real animals cannot keep top speed for long.)

10.Myth or Fact?

"Bats are completely blind, which is why they use echolocation"

The phrase "blind as a bat" has convinced most people this is literally true.

The fix: Bats are not blind. All bats can see, and many fruit bats have good eyesight and rely mostly on sight and smell. Many insect-eating bats use echolocation as an extra sense for hunting in the dark, but it does not replace their eyesight.

"Chameleons change colour mainly to camouflage themselves from predators"

This is the most commonly assumed reason, but it is not the main one.

The fix: Chameleons mainly change colour to communicate, signalling mood, readiness to mate or aggression, and to help regulate body temperature. Most species are already fairly well camouflaged by their normal colour.

"Camels store water in their humps"

This is a very familiar claim.

The fix: A camel's hump stores fat, which is an energy reserve. Camels survive long periods without water for other reasons, such as the ability to tolerate a large loss of body water and to drink a great deal at once.

"Goldfish only remember things for three seconds"

You may have heard this from adults and cartoons.

The fix: Experiments show that goldfish can learn and remember things, such as feeding times and simple tasks, for weeks or months.

Did You Know?

The Indus river dolphin is functionally blind. Unlike bats, this one really does have almost no eyesight. It swims on its side and navigates through the muddy Indus mostly by echolocation.

Worked example 4: Choose the Red List category

Question: Match each description to a category: (a) "Common and widespread." (b) "Only survives in zoos." (c) "No individual remains anywhere." (d) "Very high risk of dying out in the wild."

Step 1. (a) describes a species with no major risk: Least Concern.

Step 2. (b) survives only in captivity: Extinct in the Wild.

Step 3. (c) none remain: Extinct. (d) very high risk in the wild: Endangered.

Answer: (a) Least Concern, (b) Extinct in the Wild, (c) Extinct, (d) Endangered.

12.Summary

  • Ecosystems link living things with their non-living environment, and energy flows through food chains and webs, with only about 10% passed on at each step.
  • Keystone species and close relationships mean that losing one species can affect many others.
  • The main threats to wildlife are habitat loss, invasive species, pollution, population and climate pressures, and overharvesting.
  • The IUCN Red List classifies species from Least Concern to Extinct, and statuses can improve.
  • Pakistan has important wildlife, including the markhor, snow leopard and Indus river dolphin.
  • Community involvement and protected areas are key to conservation success.

13.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. What is the difference between a food chain and a food web?

A food chain shows one path of energy. A food web links many food chains together.

2. Why are there fewer top predators than herbivores?

Only about 10% of energy passes to each next level, so less energy is available at the top.

3. What does a keystone species do?

It has a much larger effect on its ecosystem than its numbers suggest, for example sea otters protecting kelp forests.

4. What does HIPPO stand for?

Habitat loss, Invasive species, Pollution, Population growth (and climate change), Overharvesting.

5. What is Pakistan's national animal, and how did its Red List status change in 2015?

The markhor, which moved from Endangered to Near Threatened.

6. What does a camel's hump store?

Fat, not water.

For Teachers and Parents

Suggested activities: have students draw a food web for a local habitat (a pond, a garden, a school field); hold a class "what if" game in which one species is removed and students predict the effects; and take a nature walk to count different species and talk about biodiversity. Conservation statuses are reviewed regularly by the IUCN, so please check the IUCN Red List website for the latest figures. This article is general education for students up to age 18.
