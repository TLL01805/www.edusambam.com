Author's Purpose, Tone & Point of View

Every piece of writing was created for a reason, in a particular voice, from a particular angle. Learn to detect all three, and no text can quietly slip past you unquestioned.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 20 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Reading Comprehension

No text is written by accident. Behind every article, advert and story, a writer has decided why to write, how to sound, and which side of the subject to show. A sharp reader can tell all three, even when the writer never states them.

This guide explains author's purpose (why a text exists), tone (the writer's attitude), point of view (the writer's angle on the subject) and bias (a one-sided slant). It shows how word choice reveals each one, how to read online sources like a professional fact-checker, and how to put the skills together. It has two worked examples, a checklist, common myths and ten practice questions. Spelling follows British English.

It builds on Main Idea & Supporting Details, Making Inferences, Text Structure & Organization and Context Clues. For narrators in stories, see Prose.

1.What Is Author's Purpose?

Author's purpose is the reason a text was written: what the writer wants the reader to do, know or feel. Every text has at least one purpose, even if it is never stated. Finding it helps you decide how to read: a recipe is read to be followed, an advert is read with care, and a poem is read for feeling.

A quick way to find it is to ask three questions. What does the writer want me to do? (buy, vote, act). What does the writer want me to know? (facts, steps, ideas). What does the writer want me to feel? (laugh, worry, wonder).

2.The Main Purposes: PIE and Beyond

Most texts fit one of three broad purposes, remembered as PIE: to Persuade, to Inform and to Entertain.

PIE: three main purposes
With typical clues for each.

Diagram 1. Clues are not rules: a persuasive text can use facts, and an informative one can be lively.

One topic, three purposes

Persuade: "Every town must plant more trees now, before our streets become unbearable."
Inform: "Trees lower the temperature of a street by providing shade and releasing water vapour."
Entertain: "The old oak had seen four mayors, two floods and one very unlucky pigeon."

Other purposes are often added to the list:

  • To explain how or why something works (a science explanation, a how-to guide). It is close to informing, but follows a process.
  • To describe a place, person or object so the reader can picture it.
  • To express the writer's own feelings or thoughts (a diary entry, a personal poem).

Texts can have more than one purpose. A funny advert entertains in order to persuade. A news feature informs, and also keeps you reading with a story. When two purposes appear, ask which one is the main reason the text exists.

3.How to Identify the Purpose

  • Publication and genre. A science journal, a novel and an opinion column point to different purposes. This is often the strongest single clue.
  • Word choice. Strong, emotional words ("shameful", "must") suggest persuasion. Precise, neutral words suggest information.
  • Type of evidence. Data, dates and named sources suggest information. Stories and personal appeals suggest persuasion or entertainment.
  • Structure. A problem followed by a single recommended fix often signals persuasion. Pure description often signals information. See Text Structure & Organization.
  • The ending. A call to action ("sign the petition", "join us") is a clear persuasive signal.
  • Who gains? If a company is selling something, its web page is likely to persuade, even if it looks like information.

4.What Is Tone?

Tone is the writer's attitude toward the subject or the reader, shown through word choice, sentence style and details. It answers the question: how does the writer feel about this? Tone is usually described with a word such as "admiring", "bitter" or "matter-of-fact".

GroupExample tone words
Positiveoptimistic, admiring, cheerful, playful, hopeful, grateful
Negativecritical, bitter, angry, mocking, pessimistic, scornful
Neutralobjective, matter-of-fact, formal, detached, informative
Complexironic, nostalgic, ambivalent, wistful, sarcastic, solemn

When you name a tone, choose the most exact word you can. "Negative" is a start, but "scornful" or "disappointed" shows closer reading.

5.Diction: How Word Choice Creates Tone

Diction means a writer's choice of words. Many words have two layers of meaning. The denotation is the dictionary meaning. The connotation is the feeling or association that comes with it.

talkativefriendly, chattynosy, gossipy

The facts stay the same, but the word chosen changes how the reader feels. Now watch one event told in three tones.

The council closes a library.

Diagram 2. The event is the same each time. The attitude lives in the choice of words.

Other tools that shape tone:

  • Sentence length: short sentences can feel urgent or blunt; long ones can feel calm, thoughtful or rambling.
  • Punctuation: exclamation marks suggest excitement or alarm; dashes and ellipses suggest hesitation.
  • Figurative language: "the city swallowed him" sounds ominous; "the city welcomed him" sounds warm.
  • Rhetorical questions: "Who could possibly agree with this?" signals scorn.
  • Irony and sarcasm: the words say one thing and mean the opposite. "Lovely weather for a picnic," said while soaked in a storm, is sarcasm. Read the situation as well as the words.

6.Tone and Mood Are Different

TermWhose feeling?Created by
ToneThe writer's attitudeWord choice, style
MoodThe reader's feelingSetting, imagery, atmosphere

A writer can use a calm, detached tone and still make the reader uneasy. A detached description of an empty house at night ("The rooms were silent. A tap dripped.") can create a tense mood without any emotional words.

7.Point of View

The phrase has two meanings, and it helps to keep them apart.

  • In stories: point of view is the narrator's position. First person uses "I" or "we". Second person speaks to "you". Third person uses "he", "she" or "they", and may know everyone's thoughts (omniscient) or only one character's (limited). See Prose.
  • In factual and opinion writing: point of view is the writer's stance or angle on the subject: what they believe, what they stress, and whose side they take.

Writing can be objective (it reports facts without showing a personal view) or subjective (it shows personal opinion or feeling).

Objective and subjective

Objective: "The policy reduced emissions by 15% over three years."
Subjective: "The policy's pitiful 15% reduction is an embarrassment."

The first contains a figure that can be checked. The second contains judgement words ("pitiful", "embarrassment") that tell you what the writer feels.

8.Fact and Opinion

A fact can be checked and proved true or false. An opinion is a belief or judgement. Opinion words often include best, worst, should, ought, beautiful, terrible, obviously.

  • Fact: "The library opens at nine." (check the door)
  • Opinion: "The library is the best place in town." (a judgement)
  • Mixed: "The library, which opens at nine, is the best place in town." One sentence can hold both.

An opinion is not a fault. Opinion pieces are meant to contain opinions. The question is whether they are supported with evidence, and whether the reader can tell fact from opinion.

9.Detecting Bias

Bias is a one-sided slant that treats a subject unfairly. Everyone has a point of view, and having one is normal. Bias becomes a problem when it is hidden or when it distorts the facts. Some warning signs:

  • Loaded language: emotional words chosen to steer your reaction ("regime" instead of "government", "swarm" instead of "crowd").
  • Selective evidence: only the facts that suit one side are given; important context is left out.
  • Unbalanced framing: one side gets more space, detail or sympathy than the other.
  • Sweeping generalisations: claims about whole groups without evidence ("everyone knows", "they always").
  • Name-calling and sneers: dismissing people instead of answering their arguments ("so-called experts").
  • Slanted headlines and images: the headline or photo gives a stronger impression than the article supports.
  • One-sided sources: every person quoted agrees with the writer.
Spotting bias

"The so-called 'experts' claim the new rule will help, but ordinary people know better."

Clues: "so-called" and the quotation marks sneer at the experts, "ordinary people" are set against them, and no evidence is given. The sentence never says "I disagree", yet its slant is clear.

Bias and purpose are linked. Writers who want to persuade often choose slanted words. Skilled persuaders may also adopt a calm, neutral tone to seem objective, so check the content, not just the mood.

10.Reading Online Sources Like a Fact-Checker

Online, a site can look professional while hiding its purpose. In a 2017 study, researchers at Stanford University watched 10 historians, 10 professional fact-checkers and 25 undergraduates judge live websites. The historians and students often trusted official-looking logos and web addresses. They stayed on the page to judge it, which the researchers called reading vertically. The fact-checkers did something different: after a quick look, they opened new tabs to find out who was behind the site and what others said about it. This is lateral reading. They reached better-supported conclusions in a fraction of the time.

In practice: before you trust a page, ask who runs it, who pays for it, and what do other, independent sources say about it?

11.Persuasive Appeals: Logos, Pathos and Ethos

More than two thousand years ago the Greek thinker Aristotle described three ways a speaker persuades an audience. They are still used to analyse adverts, speeches and opinion writing.

AppealIt works on…Example (invented)
LogosReason: facts, figures, logic"Pupils who eat breakfast scored higher in our class test."
PathosEmotion: pity, fear, pride, hope"Imagine a child walking to school hungry."
EthosTrust: the speaker's credibility"As a teacher for twenty years, I have seen it daily."

Most persuasive writing mixes all three. Ask which one dominates. An advert that is all emotion and no evidence leans on pathos. A report packed with numbers leans on logos. A claim backed only by "trust me, I'm an expert" leans on ethos, so look for the proof behind the title.

Irony and satire. Some writers persuade by pretending to say the opposite. Satire uses humour, exaggeration or irony to criticise something. If a text praises something in a way that is too extreme to be sincere, the real purpose may be to criticise it. Read the situation, not only the words.

12.How Purpose, Tone and Point of View Work Together

The three are linked. Purpose usually shapes tone, and tone often reveals point of view. A writer who wants to persuade you that a plan is flawed will probably sound critical or sceptical, and will choose words and evidence to match. A writer who wants only to inform will usually sound neutral. Reading all three together turns "a fact about a plan" into "one writer's specific argument about a plan".

Five questions to ask any text:

  1. Who wrote it, and who is it for?
  2. Why was it written? (purpose)
  3. How does the writer feel? Which words show it? (tone)
  4. Whose side is shown, and whose is missing? (point of view, bias)
  5. Can the claims be checked? (evidence)

13.Worked Examples

Example A: Analysing a paragraph

The paragraph is invented for practice:

"The council boasts that its new bus timetable is a triumph, yet commuters from the east side, conveniently left out of the glossy leaflet, will wait longer than ever. Ask any driver: nobody was consulted."

1Purpose: to persuade. The writer wants us to think the timetable is flawed.
2Tone: sceptical and sarcastic. "Boasts" (instead of "says"), "triumph" and "conveniently left out" suggest the writer does not trust the council.
3Point of view: subjective and against the council.
4Bias clues: loaded words ("boasts", "glossy"), an unsupported generalisation ("nobody was consulted"), and only one side's evidence. No figures or timetables are given.
5Reading it fairly: the complaint might be true, but you would want the actual waiting times and the council's reply before deciding.

Try it yourself. Name the tone: "The team trained for months, and on the day they gave everything. They lost, but nobody in that stadium will forget how they played."

Sample answer: admiring or proud. "Gave everything" and "nobody will forget" show respect for the effort, even in defeat. The purpose is to express admiration (and gently persuade the reader to share it).

Example B: Neutralising a biased sentence

Biased: "The greedy company dumped thousands of workers on the street."

1Find the loaded words: "greedy", "dumped", "on the street".
2Keep only what can be checked: a company ended the jobs of a number of workers. You would need the real number and the date.
3Rewrite neutrally, with a placeholder for facts you would look up: "The company ended the employment of [number] workers in [month, year]."
4Check: every word is now neutral and checkable. A persuasive writer could still add a reasoned argument afterwards, but the facts would be separate from the judgement.

Try it yourself. Rewrite neutrally: "The lazy students wasted another afternoon."

Sample answer: "The students did not attend the revision session on Tuesday afternoon." (Loaded words "lazy" and "wasted" removed; only a checkable fact remains.)

15.Common Myths

MythEvery text has just one purpose.
FactMany texts have two or more. Choose the main one, and mention the other if it matters.
MythBiased writing is always wrong or dishonest.
FactEveryone has a viewpoint. Bias is a problem when it is hidden or distorts the facts. Opinion pieces are open about their view.
MythA calm, polite tone means the writer is neutral.
FactSkilled persuaders often sound calm so they seem fair. Check the evidence and what is left out.
MythA professional-looking website must be reliable.
FactLogos and web addresses can be copied or designed to impress. Check what independent sources say.

16.Key Terms

Author's purpose: the reason a text was written.
PIE: persuade, inform, entertain.
Tone: the writer's attitude toward the subject.
Mood: the feeling created in the reader.
Diction: the writer's choice of words.
Connotation: the feeling attached to a word beyond its dictionary meaning.
Point of view: the angle or stance from which something is told or argued.
Bias: a one-sided slant, often hidden.
Loaded language: words chosen to steer the reader's feelings.
Lateral reading: leaving a page to check what other sources say about it.

17.Why This Matters

Understanding purpose, tone and point of view separates passive reading from critical reading. You stop asking only "what does this say?" and start asking "why was it written, how does the writer feel, and whose view is missing?" These questions protect you from persuasion you did not notice, and help you write with a clear purpose of your own. Continue with Making Inferences and Main Idea & Supporting Details to bring all your reading skills together.

Key points to remember
  • Purpose is why a text was written; PIE covers the main three: persuade, inform, entertain.
  • Publication, word choice, evidence, structure and endings are clues to purpose.
  • Tone is the writer's attitude, created mainly by diction and style.
  • Tone belongs to the writer; mood is the reader's feeling.
  • Fact can be checked; opinion is a judgement.
  • Bias shows in loaded words, selective evidence and missing sides.
  • Check online sources laterally: leave the page and see what others say.
For teachers and parents

Activity: choose three short texts on the same event (a neutral news report, an opinion piece and an advert). Ask pupils to name each text's purpose, tone and point of view, underline the loaded words, and rewrite one sentence neutrally. Then try lateral reading together: choose a web page and have pupils find three independent sources that mention the organisation behind it.

Please note: teachers use slightly different lists of purposes (PIE, PIES, or longer lists), so follow the one your syllabus uses. The examples in this guide are original. The description of the Stanford study follows the researchers' own reports.
