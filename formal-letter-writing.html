Formal Letter Writing

Structure, tone, and conventions for letters that need to be taken seriously — whether to an institution, an official, or someone you've never met.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 13 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Letter & Email Writing

A formal letter follows a predictable shape for a good reason. The reader should find what they need, such as who it is from, what it is about and what is being asked, without hunting for it.

You write a formal letter when you write to someone in an official role: a school principal, a company, a government office, a newspaper editor, a landlord or anyone you do not know personally. The tone is respectful and impersonal, the layout follows set rules, and the language avoids contractions, slang and casual phrasing.

This guide explains every part of a formal letter, how to choose the greeting and the sign-off, how to word your message, and how to write one in an exam. It ends with worked examples, a checklist, a glossary and ten practice questions.

1.What Is a Formal Letter, and When Do You Write One?

A formal letter is a letter written in a polite, serious style, with a fixed layout. It is the right choice when you need to make a request, report a problem, ask for information, apply for something, or put a message on record. People who receive many letters often read the first lines to decide what to do, so a clear structure helps your letter get a reply.

Many formal messages are now sent as emails. The ideas are the same, but an email drops the address block and is usually shorter. See Professional Email Etiquette. A friendly letter to a person you know follows different rules, which are covered in Informal Letter Writing.

2.The Layout of a Formal Letter

Most formal letters use the same order from top to bottom. The diagram shows it in nine parts.

A formal letter, part by part
Numbers appear one after another. Match each to the list below.
  1. Sender's address. Top right: your street and town. Do not write your name above it.
  2. Date. Day, month in full, year: 17 October 2026.
  3. Recipient's address. Left: the person's title and organisation, then the address.
  4. Salutation. "Dear ..." and a comma.
  5. Subject heading. A short line that says what the letter is about (optional but helpful).
  6. Opening sentence. State your purpose straight away.
  7. Body paragraphs. One main idea in each. Give the details and the request.
  8. Closing sentence. A polite ending, such as thanks and a request for a reply.
  9. Sign-off and name. Write "Yours faithfully" or "Yours sincerely", then your signature and printed name.

Diagram 1. The layout of a formal letter, with the nine main parts numbered.

The layout in the diagram is a common one. The sender's address is on the right, and everything else lines up on the left. Another version, called block format, puts every part on the left side. Both are acceptable. Choose one and use it all the way through. Address styles also differ between countries, so use the style that your country or your teacher expects.

3.Addresses and Date

Write the sender's address without your name above it, because your name appears at the end. Then write the date in full, for example "17 October 2026". Writing the month as a word avoids confusion, since the number 3/4/26 means different dates in different countries. Some countries put the month first ("October 17, 2026"), and either style is correct when used consistently.

Next comes the recipient's address: a title or name, the organisation, and then the address. When you know the person's job but not the name, use the job title, such as "The Principal" or "The Customer Services Manager". In modern layouts, you do not need to put a comma or full stop at the end of each address line.

4.The Greeting and the Sign-Off

The greeting (called the salutation) and the sign-off must match. In traditional British convention, the pair depends on whether you know the name.

Which pair should you use?
Always match the sign-off to the greeting.

Diagram 2. A named person gets "Yours sincerely". An unnamed person gets "Yours faithfully".

SituationGreetingSign-off
Name knownDear Mr Khan, or Dear Dr Patel,Yours sincerely,
Name unknownDear Sir or Madam,Yours faithfully,
Job title onlyDear Principal,Yours faithfully,
A team or departmentDear Admissions Team,Yours faithfully,
  • Follow the greeting with a comma, never an exclamation mark.
  • In British style, titles such as Mr, Ms, Mrs and Dr are written without a full stop.
  • If you do not know whether a woman prefers Ms, Mrs or Miss, "Ms" is a safe choice.
  • Capitalise only the first word of the sign-off: "Yours sincerely", not "Yours Sincerely".
  • Print your name under the sign-off. Leave a space for a handwritten signature if the letter is printed.

Two views exist on "Dear Sir or Madam". Exam courses and traditional guides still teach it. Many modern writers prefer to find a name or use a job title, because "Sir or Madam" can sound old-fashioned. If you can find the name, use it. "To Whom It May Concern" is more common in American English, and it is mainly used for general reference letters, not for ordinary requests. American English also tends to use "Sincerely" as the sign-off. Use the style that your syllabus or teacher expects.

5.The Subject Heading

A subject heading is a short line under the greeting that tells the reader what the letter is about, for example "Request to hold a charity bake sale". It is not required in every formal letter, but it helps a busy reader and is often used in letters to organisations. Keep it short and specific, and use a capital letter at the start only (or follow your teacher's style).

6.The Opening, the Body and the Closing

A formal letter does not build up slowly. State your purpose in the first sentence. Then develop it in two or three short paragraphs. A simple plan works for most letters:

  1. Opening: why you are writing.
  2. Details: the facts the reader needs: what, when, where and why.
  3. Action: what you would like the reader to do, and by when.
  4. Closing: a polite thanks and a request for a reply.
PurposeA suitable opening
RequestI am writing to request permission to...
EnquiryI am writing to enquire whether...
ComplaintI am writing to express my concern about...
ApplicationI am writing to apply for the position of...
Report a problemI am writing to report a problem with...

Each paragraph should cover one idea. Keep your tone measured, even in a complaint. A calm, clear letter is more likely to be taken seriously and answered. For more on complaints, see Complaint and Request Letters, and for the structure of paragraphs, see How to Write a Perfect Paragraph.

7.Language and Tone

Formal language is polite, precise and impersonal. That does not mean using long, difficult words. It means choosing the clear, respectful option. You can learn more about the difference in Formal vs. Informal English.

From informal to formal
The meaning is the same. The tone is not.
Informal
"I want to know when the hall is free."
Formal
"I would like to enquire when the hall is available."
Informal
"Can you send me the form?"
Formal
"Could you please send me the application form?"
Informal
"Tell me soon."
Formal
"I would be grateful for a reply at your earliest convenience."
Informal
"Sorry I'm writing late."
Formal
"I apologise for the delay in writing."
  • Avoid contractions. Write "I am", "do not" and "cannot", not "I'm", "don't" and "can't".
  • Avoid slang and chatty openings such as "Hope you are well". Go straight to the purpose.
  • Be polite and specific. Phrases such as "I would be grateful if..." and "Could you please..." soften a request. Give exact dates, names and numbers.
  • Stay calm. Do not use threats, insults or exaggeration. Say what happened and what you would like to be done.

8.Enclosures and Copies

If you send a document with your letter, such as a form or a copy of a certificate, mention it in the text ("I enclose a copy of my certificate") and write "Enc." at the bottom, after your name. If you send a copy of the letter to another person, write "cc" followed by their name. Never send original documents if a copy will do.

9.Formal Letters in Exams

An exam task usually gives you a situation, a reader and several points to cover. Spend a few minutes planning before you write.

  • Read the task twice. Note who you are writing to and why.
  • Cover every point in the task, each in a sensible paragraph.
  • Use the right level of formality for the reader, and keep it the same throughout.
  • Follow the instructions about names and addresses. Some tasks tell you which to use. If none are given, ask your teacher how to handle them.
  • Match the greeting and the sign-off. This is an easy mark to gain, and an easy one to lose.
  • Leave a minute to check. Look at spelling, punctuation and sentence endings.

10.Worked Examples

Example A: Turning a quick note into a formal letter

The note. "Hi, I want to do a bake sale for charity next Friday at break. Can I use the hall? Tell me soon. Sara"

1Add the layout. Sender's address, date and the recipient's address (The Principal and the school).
2Choose the pair. We do not know the Principal's name, so use "Dear Principal," and "Yours faithfully,".
3Add a subject heading. "Request to hold a charity bake sale".
4State the purpose first. "I am writing to ask for your permission to..."
5Give the details. What, when, where, who and why.
6Make the request and close. Ask for a reply, thank the reader, then sign off.

The formal letter.

15 Rose Avenue
Greenfield
17 October 2026

The Principal
Greenfield Secondary School
8 School Road
Greenfield

Dear Principal,

Request to hold a charity bake sale

I am writing to ask for your permission to hold a charity bake sale in the school hall during the morning break on Friday 6 November.

The money raised would be given to the local children's hospital. Members of Class 10B and the Charity Club have offered to prepare the food at home and to run the stalls. We would set up before the break and clear the hall afterwards.

I would be grateful if you could let me know whether the hall will be available, and whether any further permission is needed. I would be happy to discuss the plan with you at a time that suits you.

Thank you for considering this request. I look forward to hearing from you.

Yours faithfully,

Sara Malik
Class 10B

Try it yourself: write the opening sentence and the closing sentence of a letter to a librarian, asking for more time to return some books. Sample answer: Opening: I am writing to request an extension of the return date for the books that I currently have on loan. Closing: Thank you for your help. I look forward to hearing from you. (If you know the librarian's name, use "Dear Ms Hassan," and "Yours sincerely,".)

Example B: A complete letter from an adult

Here a tenant reports a repair problem to a landlord. Every part from Diagram 1 is present.

Flat 4B, 123 Cedar Lane
Greenfield
17 October 2026

Mr Daniel Alvarez
Alvarez Property Management
88 Oak Avenue
Greenfield

Dear Mr Alvarez,

Leaking pipe under the kitchen sink, Flat 4B

I am writing to report a leaking pipe under the kitchen sink at my rented flat, 123 Cedar Lane, Flat 4B.

The leak began on 10 October and has worsened over the past few days, causing water damage to the cabinet floor. I have placed a bucket underneath to catch the water, but the problem needs a plumber's attention as soon as possible.

Could you please arrange for a repair within the next few days? I am available most afternoons and can give access to the flat with a few hours' notice.

Thank you for your prompt attention to this matter.

Yours sincerely,

Maria Chen

The letter uses "Yours sincerely" because the landlord's name is known. The subject heading names the problem, the opening sentence states the purpose, and the body separates the facts from the request.

One long paragraphThe reader cannot find the request

Forgetting the date or the subjectThe reader cannot file or answer the letter
Writing your name above your addressYour name belongs after the sign-off

13.Common Myths About Formal Letters

MythFormal writing means long, difficult words.
FactFormal writing is clear and polite. Simple words in a formal tone work best.
MythYou must always write "Dear Sir or Madam".
FactIt is traditional, but a name or a job title is often better.
MythA formal letter must sound cold.
FactIt can be warm and courteous. It simply avoids slang and casual chat.
MythFormal letters must always be typed.
FactNeat handwriting is fine, especially in exams. Typing is common at work.

14.Key Terms

Formal letter: a polite, serious letter with a fixed layout.
Sender's address: your address, at the top of the letter.
Recipient's address: the address of the person or organisation you write to.
Salutation: the greeting, such as "Dear Mr Khan,".
Subject heading: a short line that says what the letter is about.
Body: the main paragraphs of the letter.
Sign-off (complimentary close): the polite ending, such as "Yours faithfully,".
Enclosure (Enc.): a document sent with the letter.
Block format: a layout where every part is aligned to the left.
Tone: how the writing sounds to the reader.

15.Why This Matters

A well-structured formal letter is taken seriously faster than a rambling one, because readers in official roles look for the purpose first. This skill also makes the rest of the Letter and Email Writing series easier, including Professional Email Etiquette, Job Applications and Cover Letters and Complaint and Request Letters. Before you send or hand in a letter, use the advice in Punctuation Rules to check it.

Key points to remember
  • Use the standard layout: sender's address, date, recipient's address, greeting, subject, body and sign-off.
  • Match the pair: "Yours sincerely" with a name, "Yours faithfully" without one.
  • State your purpose in the first sentence, and keep one idea in each paragraph.
  • Use a polite, calm and formal tone. Avoid contractions, slang and chatty openings.
  • In an exam, cover every point, keep a steady tone and check your work.
  • Mention enclosures in the text and write "Enc." at the bottom.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give learners a short, informal note and ask them to turn it into a formal letter using the nine-part layout. Then let them swap letters and check the greeting and sign-off pair, the opening sentence and the tone with the checklist.

Please note: all examples here are original, written for teaching. The addresses are made up. Layouts and sign-offs vary between countries and syllabuses, so follow the style your course expects.
