Tenses & Verb Forms: The Complete Guide

Every verb tells the reader not just what happened, but when. Here are all twelve English tenses, irregular verbs, voice, mood, and modals — explained clearly, with examples.

Tense is how English marks time. Get it right, and a reader knows exactly when something happened, is happening, or will happen — without a single extra word.

English has three basic time frames — past, present, and future — and four aspects within each: simple, continuous, perfect, and perfect continuous. Multiply these together and you get the twelve tenses that this guide covers in full, along with irregular verbs, active and passive voice, mood, and modal verbs.

1.How the Tense System Works

A verb tells the reader two things: when an action happens (the time frame) and how the speaker views it (the aspect). Strictly speaking, English changes the form of a verb for only two tenses, present and past; the future is built with will and other structures. Teachers still group verbs into 12 tenses (3 time frames × 4 aspects) because it is the clearest way to learn them.

  • Simple: facts, habits and complete actions seen as a whole.
  • Continuous (Progressive): an action in progress or temporary.
  • Perfect: links two times; something earlier matters at a later point.
  • Perfect Continuous: how long an activity has been going on up to a point.

To choose a tense, ask: When is it? then How do I see the action: whole, in progress, finished-with-a-result, or lasting?

2.Present Tenses

Present Simple

Use: habits, permanent facts, timetables, instructions. Form: (+) base verb, with -s/-es for he/she/it; (−) do/does not + base; (?) Do/Does + subject + base. Signal words: always, usually, often, every day, never. Example: "She walks to school." / "She doesn't walk." / "Does she walk?"

Present Continuous

Use: actions happening now, temporary situations, changing trends, arranged future plans. Form: am/is/are (not) + verb-ing. Signal words: now, at the moment, currently, these days, tonight. Example: "Prices are rising." / "We are meeting at 6."

Present Perfect

Use: a past action with a present result, life experience, unfinished periods (today, this week). Form: have/has (not) + past participle. Signal words: already, yet, just, ever, never, so far, recently, since, for. Example: "I have lost my keys." (I still can't find them.)

Present Perfect Continuous

Use: how long an activity has lasted up to now, or a recent activity with a visible result. Form: have/has (not) been + verb-ing. Signal words: for, since, all day, lately. Example: "Her eyes are red; she has been crying."

3.Past Tenses

Past Simple

Use: a finished action at a definite time, a sequence of events in a story. Form: (+) verb-ed or irregular form; (−) did not + base; (?) Did + subject + base. Signal words: yesterday, ago, last week, in 2019, when I was young. Example: "She walked home." / "Did she walk?" (After did the verb returns to base.)

For past habits, use used to or would: "We used to play cricket every evening."

Past Continuous

Use: an action in progress at a past moment, scene-setting, or a longer action interrupted by a shorter one. Form: was/were (not) + verb-ing. Signal words: while, as, at 8 p.m. yesterday. Example: "I was cooking when the power went out."

Past Perfect

Use: the earlier of two past events. Form: had (not) + past participle. Signal words: before, after, by the time, already, until then. Example: "When we arrived, the film had started."

Past Perfect Continuous

Use: how long something had been happening before a past moment, or the cause of a past situation. Form: had (not) been + verb-ing. Signal words: for, since, all morning. Example: "He was exhausted; he had been running."

4.Ways to Talk About the Future

will + base

Use: spontaneous decisions, offers, promises, opinions or predictions. Form: will (not) + base verb. Signal words: probably, I think, perhaps. Example: "I'll carry that for you."

be going to

Use: plans already made, or predictions based on present evidence. Form: am/is/are (not) going to + base. Signal words: tonight, next week. Example: "Look at those clouds! It is going to rain."

Present Continuous / Present Simple

Use: arranged plans (continuous); timetables and schedules (simple). Form: as present forms. Signal words: tomorrow, next Monday. Example: "We are flying at noon." / "The train leaves at 9."

Future Continuous

Use: an action in progress at a future time; polite enquiries. Form: will be + verb-ing. Signal words: this time tomorrow, at 8 a.m. Example: "Will you be using the car tonight?"

Future Perfect

Use: an action finished before a future time. Form: will have + past participle. Signal words: by, by the time, before. Example: "By June, she will have graduated."

Future Perfect Continuous

Use: duration up to a future point. Form: will have been + verb-ing. Signal words: by, for. Example: "By June, I will have been working here for ten years."

Time-clause rule: after when, as soon as, before, after, until, if, unless, use a present form for future meaning, not will. Correct: "I'll call you when I arrive." Incorrect: "when I will arrive."

6.Spelling Rules for Verb Endings

EndingRuleExamples

-ingdrop silent e; double a final consonant after a short stressed vowel; ie becomes ymaking, running, beginning, lying

7.Negatives and Questions: the Auxiliary Rule

The first auxiliary does the work. It takes not for negatives and moves in front of the subject for questions: "She has walked" → "She hasn't walked" / "Has she walked?" Simple present and past have no auxiliary, so English adds do/does/did. After do, does, did the main verb is always the base form: "Does she walk?" (not "walks"), "She didn't go" (not "went").

8.Stative Verbs: Why Some Verbs Resist the Continuous

Verbs of state, thought, feeling, possession and sense usually stay simple: know, understand, believe, like, love, hate, want, need, prefer, own, belong, seem. Incorrect: "I am knowing the answer." Correct: "I know the answer." Some verbs change meaning: "I think it's good" (opinion) but "I am thinking about moving" (process); "She has a car" (possession) but "She is having lunch" (activity).

9.Present Perfect vs Past Simple; For vs Since; Been vs Gone

  • Finished time named (yesterday, in 2010, ago, last year, when) → Past Simple: "I saw him yesterday."
  • No time named, or time not finished (today, this week, ever) → Present Perfect: "I have seen him today."
  • for + length of time (for two hours); since + starting point (since 9 a.m.). Both pair with present perfect: "I have lived here since 2020."
  • been = went and came back ("She has been to Paris"); gone = is still away ("She has gone to Paris").

Regular verbs add -ed. Irregular verbs look random but fall into patterns, which makes them easier to learn in groups.

Often confused: lie–lay–lain (rest) vs lay–laid–laid (put something down).

11.Active and Passive Voice

In the passive, the receiver of the action becomes the subject: be (in the right tense) + past participle. Use it when the doer is unknown, unimportant or obvious, or when the result matters more: "The samples were tested under controlled conditions." Only transitive verbs have a passive ("The accident happened", never "was happened"). Avoid it when it hides who is responsible.

12.Conditionals and Wishes

TypeIf-clauseMain clauseUse
Zeropresent simplepresent simplegeneral truths: If you heat ice, it melts.
Firstpresent simplewill + basereal, likely future: If it rains, we will stay in.
Secondpast simple (were for all subjects in formal English)would + baseunreal or unlikely present: If I were you, I would apologise.
Thirdpast perfectwould have + V3unreal past: If she had studied, she would have passed.
Mixedpast perfectwould + basepast cause, present result: If I had taken that job, I would be abroad now.

Never put will or would in the if-clause: "If I will go" is wrong. Wishes: wish + past simple for present regret ("I wish I knew"); wish + past perfect for past regret ("I wish I had listened"); wish + would for annoyance ("I wish he would stop").

13.Reported Speech: Backshift

When the reporting verb is in the past ("She said…"), the tense usually shifts one step back, and pronouns and time words change (today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, here → there).

Direct speechReported speech
present simplepast simple
present continuouspast continuous
present perfect / past simplepast perfect
will / can / may / mustwould / could / might / had to

No backshift is needed when the statement is still true or the reporting verb is present: "She said that the Earth orbits the Sun."

14.Mood: Indicative, Imperative, Subjunctive

  • Indicative: facts, opinions, questions. "She works hard."
  • Imperative: commands, requests, instructions. "Work harder."
  • Subjunctive: wishes, hypotheticals and demands. Were-subjunctive: "I wish she were here." Mandative: base verb after essential, suggest, insist, demand: "It is essential that he arrive on time."

15.Modal Verbs

Modals add meaning (ability, possibility, obligation, advice) to a base verb. They take no -s, -ing or -ed, form questions and negatives themselves ("Can she swim?"), and are followed by the base verb (ought takes to).

can / couldability, permission; could = past ability, polite requestShe could swim at five.

Modal perfectMeaningExample

16.Keeping Tenses Consistent in Writing

Choose a main time frame and change only when the time really changes. In a past-tense story, use the past perfect for earlier events and the present simple for lasting truths. Incorrect: "He opened the door and sees a stranger." Correct: "He opened the door and saw a stranger who had been waiting for an hour." Before you submit, ask: does every verb match the time I mean?

17.Common Tense Errors

  • She walks to school and then ate lunch. → She walked to school and then ate lunch. Avoid unnecessary tense shifts.
  • I have seen him yesterday. → I saw him yesterday. A finished-time word needs past simple.
  • She goed to the store. → She went to the store. Learn irregular forms.
  • I am knowing the answer. → I know the answer. Stative verbs stay simple.
  • He doesn't likes tea. → He doesn't like tea. The base form follows does/did.
  • Did she went home? → Did she go home? Past marked once, by did.
  • If I will go, I will call. → If I go, I will call. No will in the if-clause.
  • Call me when you will arrive. → Call me when you arrive. Use present in time clauses.
  • I live here since 2020. → I have lived here since 2020. Since/for need present perfect.
  • I am living here for five years. → I have been living here for five years. Duration up to now.
Real-World Example

Consider this line from a project update email: "By the time you read this, the team will have finished testing, and the results will be shared by Friday." The opening time clause, "By the time you read this," uses the present simple (read) with future meaning, exactly as the time-clause rule requires. The main clause uses the future perfect ("will have finished") for an action completed before that moment. The second clause uses the future simple passive ("will be shared") because the focus is on the results, not the sharer.

Why This Matters

Tense is what places every sentence you write in time. Once you know the parts of speech (Topic I), how to structure a sentence (Topic II), and how to punctuate it (Topic III), tense and verb form (Topic IV) is what lets you say precisely when something happened — a small grammatical choice that has a large effect on how clearly a reader understands you.
