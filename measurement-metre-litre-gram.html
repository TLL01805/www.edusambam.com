Measurement: Metre, Litre & Gram

Learn the metric units for length, mass and capacity, how prefixes work, how to convert in both directions, how area and volume factors differ, how to compare prices, and how accurate a measurement can be.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|20 min read|Last updated: November 2026|Mathematics

Every time you buy rice, fill a bottle, measure a room or read a medicine label you use measurement. The metric system is built so that these quantities connect through powers of ten, and once you understand why, conversions become a short reasoning step instead of a trick with the decimal point. This guide covers length, mass and capacity, the prefixes behind the units, converting in both directions, adding and comparing measurements, and the links between length, area, volume and capacity. It also covers how to read scales correctly, how accurate a measurement can be, and the history of the units, including the parts that are defined differently today.

1.What Measurement Means

Measuring means comparing a quantity with an agreed standard unit. Saying a table is 2 metres long means it is two times the length of the standard metre. Three quantities appear most often at school level:

QuantityIt tells usCommon metric units
LengthHow long, tall, wide or farkm, m, cm, mm
MassHow much matter an object containst, kg, g, mg
CapacityHow much a container can holdkL, L, mL

The unit must always be written. A distance of 5 km is very different from 5 m, and 5 g is very different from 5 kg.

Mass and weight. In science, mass is the amount of matter in an object (measured in kg), while weight is the pull of gravity on it, which is a force measured in newtons. In everyday speech and in many exam questions, “weighs 2.4 kg” is used to mean “has a mass of 2.4 kg”. This article uses mass.

2.Metric Prefixes

A prefix tells you how a unit relates to the base unit. The same prefixes work for metres, grams and litres.

The symbol m can mean “metre” or “milli”, so the position matters: in “mm” the first m is milli and the second is metre. A tonne (t) is a metric unit equal to 1000 kg.

Careful: in the SI system the base unit of mass is the kilogram, not the gram, even though we usually think of the gram as the basic unit. The litre is not an SI base unit either (the SI unit of volume is the cubic metre), but it is accepted for everyday use alongside SI.

3.Length: km, m, cm, mm

Figure 1. To go down the ladder to a smaller unit, multiply. To go up to a bigger unit, divide.
RelationshipMeaning
1 km = 1000 mone thousand metres in a kilometre
1 m = 100 cmone hundred centimetres in a metre
1 cm = 10 mmten millimetres in a centimetre
1 m = 1000 mm100 × 10 = 1000

Bigger unit to smaller unit: multiply

You need more small units to make the same length, so the number gets bigger.

Example 1 · Converting down

(a) 4.7 m to cm: 4.7 × 100 = 470 cm. (b) 6.25 km to m: 6.25 × 1000 = 6250 m. (c) 2.4 km to cm: 2.4 × 1000 = 2400 m, then 2400 × 100 = 240 000 cm (km to m, then m to cm).

Smaller unit to bigger unit: divide

Example 2 · Converting up

(a) 375 cm to m: 375 ÷ 100 = 3.75 m. (b) 2450 mm to m: 2450 ÷ 1000 = 2.45 m. (c) 2500 m to km: 2500 ÷ 1000 = 2.5 km.

“250 cm = 25 m, because I divided by 10.”

Is a metre bigger or smaller than a centimetre?

The fix: there are 100 cm in 1 m, so divide by 100, not 10: 250 ÷ 100 = 2.5 m. Do a size check as well: a metre is a bigger unit, so the number must get smaller, and 25 is far too large for a 250 cm object (about a tall door). Check the factor, then check the direction.

4.Mass: t, kg, g, mg

RelationshipMeaning
1 t = 1000 kga tonne
1 kg = 1000 gone thousand grams
1 g = 1000 mgone thousand milligrams
1 kg = 1 000 000 mg1000 × 1000
Example 3 · Mass conversions

(a) 3.6 kg to g: 3.6 × 1000 = 3600 g. (b) 2500 g to kg: 2500 ÷ 1000 = 2.5 kg. (c) 4.2 g to mg: 4.2 × 1000 = 4200 mg. (d) 850 mg to g: 850 ÷ 1000 = 0.85 g.

(e) 0.045 kg to mg. Milligrams are far smaller than kilograms, so the number must grow a lot. 1 kg = 1 000 000 mg, so 0.045 × 1 000 000 = 45 000 mg. (Check: 0.045 kg = 45 g, and 45 × 1000 = 45 000 mg.)

“0.8 kg = 80 g.”

How many grams are in a whole kilogram?

The fix: kg to g means × 1000, so 0.8 × 1000 = 800 g. Moving the decimal point by three places needs a place holder zero: 0.8 becomes 800, not 80. A quick check: 0.8 kg is nearly a kilogram, so about 800 g is sensible and 80 g is not.

5.Capacity: kL, L, mL

Capacity is how much a container can hold. A litre is a convenient unit for liquids, and 1 L = 1000 mL. A kilolitre is 1000 L.

Example 4 · Capacity

(a) 1.75 L to mL: 1.75 × 1000 = 1750 mL. (b) 650 mL to L: 650 ÷ 1000 = 0.65 L. (c) 0.375 L = 375 mL. (d) A recipe needs 1.25 L, and the jug is marked in mL: 1.25 × 1000 = 1250 mL.

How capacity links to length: the cube

Since 1964 the litre has been defined as exactly one cubic decimetre, which is a cube with edges of 10 cm. Its volume is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 cm³. So 1 L = 1000 cm³ and 1 mL = 1 cm³.

Figure 2. A cube with 10 cm edges holds exactly 1 litre, which is 1000 millilitres, so each cubic centimetre is one millilitre.

Water has a convenient property: 1 mL of water has a mass of about 1 g, so 1 L of water has a mass of about 1 kg (this is true for pure water and is a close approximation at ordinary temperatures). The metric system was designed this way.

6.Area and Volume Conversions

Be careful with squared and cubed units. When both lengths change units, the conversion factor is squared.

Figure 3. 1 m = 100 cm, so a square metre is 100 × 100 = 10 000 square centimetres, not 100.
ConversionWhy
1 m² = 10 000 cm²100 × 100
1 cm² = 100 mm²10 × 10
1 km² = 1 000 000 m²1000 × 1000
1 hectare = 10 000 m²a square 100 m by 100 m
1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³100 × 100 × 100
1 m³ = 1000 L1 000 000 cm³ ÷ 1000 cm³ per litre

The word hectare comes from “hecto-are”: an are is 100 m² and a hectare is 100 ares.

Example 5 · Area and volume

(a) 5.2 m² to cm²: 5.2 × 10 000 = 52 000 cm². (b) 3.5 m³ to litres: 3.5 × 1000 = 3500 L.

(c) A rectangular plot is 20 m by 15 m. Area = 300 m² = 300 ÷ 10 000 = 0.03 hectare.

(d) A tank 2 m long, 1.5 m wide and 1 m deep holds 2 × 1.5 × 1 = 3 m³ = 3000 L. Filled with water, it has a mass of about 3000 kg = 3 tonnes.

“1 m² = 100 cm², because 1 m = 100 cm.”

How many 1 cm by 1 cm squares fit in a 1 m by 1 m square?

The fix: an area has two lengths. A square metre is 100 cm by 100 cm, so it contains 100 × 100 = 10 000 small squares. For cubic units the factor is cubed: 1 m³ = 100 × 100 × 100 = 1 000 000 cm³. Square the length factor for area and cube it for volume.

7.Adding, Comparing and Using Measurements

You can only add, subtract or compare measurements in the same unit. Convert first.

Example 6 · Combining measurements

(a) A ribbon of 2 m and another of 75 cm: 75 cm = 0.75 m, so 2 + 0.75 = 2.75 m. A common error is to write 2 + 75 = 77.

(b) 1.8 kg of flour plus 650 g: 650 g = 0.65 kg, so 1.8 + 0.65 = 2.45 kg.

(c) Bottle A holds 750 mL and bottle B holds 1.2 L = 1200 mL. Difference = 1200 − 750 = 450 mL.

(d) Repeated amounts. A café uses 250 mL of milk per drink. For 18 drinks: 18 × 250 = 4500 mL = 4.5 L. A factory packs 24 bags of 350 g: 24 × 350 = 8400 g = 8.4 kg.

(e) Leftover. A jug holds 1.2 L. Seven glasses of 150 mL are poured. Used: 7 × 150 = 1050 mL, and 1.2 L = 1200 mL, so 150 mL is left.

(f) Medicine. A dose of 5 mL, three times a day for 7 days: 5 × 3 × 7 = 105 mL.

(g) Comparing size. A shelf is 1.8 m = 1800 mm long and a gap is 12 mm, so the shelf is 1800 ÷ 12 = 150 times the gap.

“2 m + 75 cm = 77.”

What is missing from the answer?

The fix: the answer has no unit, and the numbers were added as though both were in the same unit. 2 m is 200 cm, so 200 + 75 = 275 cm = 2.75 m. Always convert both to one unit first and then write the unit with the answer.

Value for money: unit price

To compare prices, convert to the same unit and find the price per unit. One pack has 750 g for Rs 270, and another has 1.2 kg for Rs 408. Price per kg: 270 ÷ 0.75 = Rs 360 for the first pack, and 408 ÷ 1.2 = Rs 340 for the second. The larger pack is cheaper per kilogram.

Estimating gives a useful check. A rough guide: a door is about 2 m high, a pencil about 18 cm, a mobile phone about 150 g, a cup about 250 mL, a bottle of drinking water 500 mL to 1.5 L, a bag of flour 1 kg. If an answer says a pencil is 18 m long, the unit or the factor is wrong.

9.Reading Scales and Accuracy

Figure 4. Read a ruler straight on. This bar is 7.4 cm long, to the nearest millimetre.

Start from the zero mark, read the whole centimetres, then count the small marks for millimetres. Look straight at the mark, not from the side, to avoid a reading error caused by the angle.

Figure 5. The liquid surface curves, and you read the bottom of the curve at eye level.

How accurate is a measurement?

Every measurement is rounded to the precision of the instrument. A length given as 12 cm to the nearest centimetre could be any value from 11.5 cm up to (but not including) 12.5 cm: 11.5 ≤ length < 12.5. A mass of 4.2 kg to one decimal place lies in 4.15 ≤ mass < 4.25. In general the error can be up to half of the smallest unit used.

10.Other Systems of Measurement

  • Imperial units. Some countries still use inches, feet, miles, pounds and gallons. They are defined in terms of metric units. For example, 1 inch = 2.54 cm exactly (so 1 foot = 30.48 cm), 1 mile ≈ 1.609 km, 1 pound ≈ 0.4536 kg, and 1 UK gallon ≈ 4.546 L (a US gallon is smaller, about 3.785 L).
  • Traditional units in South Asia. Units such as the tola (about 11.66 g), the seer and the maund are still used in some markets. Their sizes have differed by region and period, so check the definition when it matters.
  • Land units. In many parts of Punjab, land is sold in marla and kanal. The commonly used standard values are 1 marla = 272.25 square feet (about 25.3 m²), 1 kanal = 20 marla = 5445 square feet (about 506 m²), and 8 kanal = 1 acre. Local values can differ, so always confirm the definition in a property document.
Example 7 · Imperial to metric

6 feet = 6 × 30.48 = 182.88 cm. 5 miles ≈ 5 × 1.609 = 8.05 km. 2.5 pounds ≈ 2.5 × 0.4536 = 1.13 kg.

11.A Short History of Metric Units

The metric system was developed in France in the 1790s. The metre was first defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator along the meridian through Paris, and surveyors measured part of that distance to fix its length. The gram was first based on the mass of a cubic centimetre of water, and the kilogram became the base unit. Over the years the definitions were improved. Since 1983 the metre has been defined using the speed of light (the distance light travels in 1/299 792 458 of a second), and since 2019 the kilogram has been defined using a fixed value of a constant of nature, the Planck constant, instead of a metal cylinder kept in France. These changes did not alter the everyday size of the units, but they made them reproducible anywhere.

Did You Know?

A cube of water 10 cm on each side holds 1 litre and has a mass of about 1 kilogram. The units for length, capacity and mass are connected by design: this is the main reason the metric system is so easy to use.

12.Common Mistakes

  • Multiplying when you should divide (or the reverse). Check the direction with a size check.
  • Using 100 when the factor is 1000. Litres to millilitres, kilograms to grams and metres to millimetres all use 1000.
  • Using the length factor for area or volume. Square it for area and cube it for volume.
  • Adding different units directly. Convert first.
  • Forgetting the placeholder zero when the decimal point moves (0.8 kg is 800 g).
  • Leaving out the unit, or mixing mass with capacity (grams are not millilitres, except for water as an approximation).
  • Quoting more accuracy than the instrument gives.

13.Try It Yourself

  1. Convert 0.6 km to cm, and 7250 mL to L.
  2. A bag of 2.5 kg of sugar is shared equally into packets of 250 g. How many packets?
  3. Write 3.2 m² in cm².
Show answers

1. 0.6 km = 0.6 × 1000 = 600 m, and 600 × 100 = 60 000 cm. 7250 mL = 7250 ÷ 1000 = 7.25 L.

2. 2.5 kg = 2500 g, and 2500 ÷ 250 = 10 packets.

3. 3.2 × 10 000 = 32 000 cm².

14.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [3 marks] Convert (a) 0.045 kg to mg, (b) 3.6 km to cm, (c) 2750 mL to L.
  2. [3 marks] One ribbon is 2 m 75 cm long and another is 1 m 40 cm long. Find their total length in metres.
  3. [3 marks] A jug holds 1.2 L. Seven glasses of 150 mL each are filled from it. How much is left, in mL?
  4. [4 marks] A water tank is 2 m long, 1.5 m wide and 1 m deep. How many litres does it hold when full? Estimate the mass of the water in tonnes.
  5. [3 marks] A 750 g pack costs Rs 270 and a 1.2 kg pack costs Rs 408. Which is better value? Show the price per kg.
  6. [3 marks] A length is 12 cm, correct to the nearest cm. Write the lower and upper bounds of the true length.
  7. [4 marks] A rectangular plot is 20 m by 15 m. Find its area in m², and in hectares. A car uses 8 litres of fuel for each 100 km. How much fuel does it use for 350 km?
Show answers

1. (a) 0.045 × 1 000 000 = 45 000 mg. (b) 3.6 × 1000 = 3600 m, then × 100 = 360 000 cm. (c) 2750 ÷ 1000 = 2.75 L.

2. 2 m 75 cm = 2.75 m. 1 m 40 cm = 1.40 m. Total = 2.75 + 1.40 = 4.15 m (that is, 4 m 15 cm).

3. Used = 7 × 150 = 1050 mL. 1.2 L = 1200 mL. Left = 1200 − 1050 = 150 mL.

4. Volume = 2 × 1.5 × 1 = 3 m³. 1 m³ = 1000 L, so the tank holds 3000 L. Water has a mass of about 1 kg per litre, so the mass is about 3000 kg = 3 tonnes.

5. 750 g = 0.75 kg, so the first pack costs 270 ÷ 0.75 = Rs 360 per kg. The second costs 408 ÷ 1.2 = Rs 340 per kg. The 1.2 kg pack is better value.

6. Lower bound 11.5 cm; upper bound 12.5 cm (the true length satisfies 11.5 ≤ length < 12.5).

7. Area = 20 × 15 = 300 m² = 300 ÷ 10 000 = 0.03 hectare. Fuel: 350 ÷ 100 = 3.5, and 3.5 × 8 = 28 litres.

15.Key Points

  • Prefixes: kilo = 1000, centi = 0.01, milli = 0.001. 1 tonne = 1000 kg.
  • Bigger unit to smaller unit: multiply. Smaller unit to bigger unit: divide. Then check the size.
  • 1 L = 1000 mL = 1000 cm³, so 1 mL = 1 cm³. 1 m³ = 1000 L.
  • Area factors are squared (1 m² = 10 000 cm²) and volume factors are cubed.
  • Convert to a common unit before adding, subtracting or comparing.
  • Compare prices with the price per kg or per litre.
  • Read scales straight on, and remember a rounded measurement could be up to half a unit either way.

16.For Teachers and Parents

Measurement is best learned with real objects. Ask learners to estimate first and then measure: the width of a desk, the mass of a bag, the capacity of a cup. Fill a litre bottle with water and use a 10 cm cube or a balance to show that 1 L is 1000 cm³ and about 1 kg. The most common errors are the direction of conversion and using the wrong factor for area, so insist on a size check every time. Use unit prices when shopping or cooking to make the topic real.
