Common Pronunciation Mistakes

A closer, sound-by-sound look at the specific errors that most often make speech harder to understand — and exactly how to correct each one.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 14 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | English Language

Most pronunciation problems do not come from a lack of talent. They come from a small number of specific, learnable sounds and patterns, and many of these come from two things: English spelling, and the sounds of your first language.

English spelling is inconsistent. The same letters can stand for different sounds, and the same sound can be written in several ways. English also has more than 40 distinct sounds, depending on the accent, but only 26 letters to write them. On top of that, every learner brings the sound system of their own language, which can help or get in the way. This guide explains why pronunciation mistakes happen, covers the most common ones with how to fix each, and shows a practice method that works.

It builds on the overview in Spoken English and Pronunciation. It ends with worked examples, a checklist, a glossary and ten practice questions.

🔊 Tap a "Listen" button to hear words read by your device's built-in voice. Voices and accents differ between devices, so check difficult words in a dictionary that gives audio for the accent you want.

1.Why Pronunciation Mistakes Happen

There are three main causes, and usually more than one is at work.

  • Spelling. English spelling does not show pronunciation reliably. "Though", "through" and "thought" look alike but sound different.
  • First-language influence. If a sound does not exist in your first language, you may replace it with the closest sound you know. Your ear may also find it hard, at first, to notice a difference that your own language does not use.
  • Habit. Sounds are movements of the tongue, lips and jaw. Old movements are strong, and new ones need repeated practice.

Because of the second cause, there is no single list of mistakes that fits every learner. Research on Indian English, for example, describes patterns that come from the sounds of Indian languages: the English "th" sounds are often replaced by dental stops (so "think" sounds close to "tink"); "v" and "w" often merge into one sound; and the English "t" and "d" are often made with the tongue curled back (called retroflex). Hindi and Urdu share most of their sounds, so learners from either background may notice the same patterns. These are tendencies, not rules for every speaker.

An accent is not a mistake

Everyone who speaks English has an accent. Accents are normal and are not errors. The aim of pronunciation practice is to be easy to understand, not to sound like someone else. A "mistake" in this guide means a sound pattern that can make a listener hear the wrong word.

2.Sounds, Symbols and Minimal Pairs

A phoneme is a sound that can change the meaning of a word. "Bin" and "pin" are different words because /b/ and /p/ are different phonemes. Dictionaries show each sound with a symbol from the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), so that one symbol stands for one sound. The symbols go between slanted lines, as in /θ/ for the "th" in "think".

A minimal pair is two words that differ by only one sound, such as "ship" and "sheep". Minimal pairs are the best tool for practising a difficult contrast, because they show you when your pronunciation would change the meaning.

It also helps to know about voicing. Put your fingers lightly on your throat and say "sss", then "zzz". With "zzz" you feel a buzz. That buzzing sound is voiced. "Sss" has no buzz, so it is voiceless. Pairs like s and z, f and v, and t and d differ mainly in voicing, and voicing decides how the endings "-ed" and "-s" are pronounced.

4.Consonant Confusions

R vs. L
right ≠ light, red ≠ led
✗ Making the two sounds the same
✓ R: the tongue pulls back or bunches up and does not touch the roof of the mouth. L: the tongue tip touches the ridge just behind the top teeth.
B vs. V
berry ≠ very, boat ≠ vote
✗ Using both lips for both sounds
✓ B: both lips close, then open. V: the top teeth touch the bottom lip, and air keeps flowing.
V vs. W
vest ≠ west, vine ≠ wine
✗ Using one in-between sound for both
✓ V: top teeth on the bottom lip, with a buzz. W: round the lips, with no teeth touching.
S vs. SH
sip ≠ ship, sea ≠ she
✗ Using the same hissing sound for both
✓ S: the tongue is flat with a narrow gap. SH: the tongue pulls back a little and the lips round.
SH vs. CH
ship ≠ chip, wash ≠ watch
✗ Not stopping the air at the start of CH
✓ CH starts with a short stop, like t, then releases into sh.
TH
think ≠ tin, this ≠ dis
✗ Replacing th with t or d, or s or z
✓ Put the tongue tip lightly between the teeth and let air flow. Voiceless in "think", voiced in "this".
T and D
tin, day, time
✗ Curling the tongue back too far (retroflex)
✓ Touch the tongue tip lightly to the ridge just behind the top teeth, with a light tap.

The English "th" has two sounds: voiceless /θ/ in "think" and "three", and voiced /ð/ in "this" and "then". Put them in minimal pairs to hear the difference: "thin" and "tin", "three" and "tree", "then" and "den".

5.Final Consonants and Clusters

English uses final consonants to carry grammar: the plural in "cats", the past tense in "walked", the third-person "-s" in "she walks". If you drop them, the listener may lose the grammar or hear a different word. Many words also end in groups of consonants, called clusters, such as "asked", "texts" and "months". It is natural to simplify these, but practise saying each part, slowly at first.

6.Vowel Length and Quality

Many English words differ only in their vowel, and some pairs of vowels differ in both length and quality. English has more vowel sounds than the vowel letters a, e, i, o and u, so spelling does not help much.

Ship vs. Sheep
/ɪ/ (short) vs. /iː/ (long)
✗ Treating both vowels as the same
✓ "Sheep" has a longer, tenser vowel than "ship".
Full vs. Fool
/ʊ/ (short) vs. /uː/ (long)
✗ Merging both vowels
✓ "Fool" is longer and rounder than "full".
Cat vs. Cut vs. Cart
/æ/, /ʌ/, /ɑː/
✗ Using one vowel for all three
✓ Open the mouth wide for "cat", keep it relaxed for "cut", and make it long and low for "cart" (British).
Bad vs. Bed
/æ/ vs. /e/
✗ Making both sound like one vowel
✓ Drop the jaw more for "bad" than for "bed".

Accents differ here. For example, many British speakers say "bath" with the long vowel /ɑː/, while many American speakers use /æ/. Both are normal. Choose one accent as your model and stay consistent.

7.Word Endings: -ed and -s

The endings "-ed" (past tense) and "-s" or "-es" (plurals, third-person verbs and possessives) each have three pronunciations. The choice depends on the last sound of the word, not on the spelling.

10.Which Mistakes Matter Most?

Not all pronunciation differences affect understanding equally. The linguist Jennifer Jenkins studied conversations between non-native speakers of English and proposed a "Lingua Franca Core" of features that matter most for being understood. In her proposal, these matter most: almost all consonant sounds, the difference between short and long vowels (such as "sit" and "seat"), and the stress on the most important word in a group of words. She found that the two "th" sounds were not essential for being understood.

This is one research proposal, and it is meant for English used between people who speak different first languages. Later studies suggest that vowel quality also matters. For exams, interviews and conversations with native speakers, follow the standard that your teacher or syllabus expects. A useful way to decide what to practise first is to ask: "Which mistake could make my listener hear a different word?" Start there.

11.Sounds, Stress and Intonation Work Together

Correct sounds are only one layer. A word said with the wrong stress can be as hard to understand as a word with a wrong sound, and a flat voice can hide your meaning. Learn more in Word and Sentence Stress Patterns and Intonation in Conversation.

12.How to Fix a Pronunciation Mistake

Fixing one specific sound is faster than general practice, because it targets the exact problem. Use this method.

  1. Identify one sound that you often get wrong. Use this guide or feedback from a listener.
  2. Learn how it is made. Check the tongue, lip and jaw position in a dictionary or a video. Use a mirror.
  3. Find minimal pairs. Choose 5 to 10 pairs, and say them aloud, slowly, then at normal speed.
  4. Put the sound in sentences. Then use it in short, real conversations.
  5. Record yourself and compare with the dictionary audio. Listen for the difference.
  6. Repeat a little every day for one or two weeks, before you move to the next sound.

R and Lright / light, red / led, rock / lock

B and Vberry / very, boat / vote, bat / vat

Tongue twisters are good for movement practice. Try "She sells seashells by the seashore" (s and sh) and "Red lorry, yellow lorry" (r and l). Say them slowly and correctly first, and only then try to speed up.

13.Worked Examples

Example A: Diagnose and plan

A learner reads aloud: "I wery like to go on a ship. I tink this is a good idea. He ask me to come." You hear three patterns.

1Record and list the patterns. "wery" for "very" (v and w), "tink" for "think" (th), and "ask" for "asked" (a missing -ed).
2Decide which can change the meaning. "Wery" can sound like "wary", and the missing "-ed" changes the tense. "Tink" is less likely to cause confusion.
3Make a plan. Week 1: v and w. Week 2: the -ed endings. Week 3: th (if the learner and teacher want it).
4Practise with pairs. vest / west, vine / wine. Then walked, played, wanted.
5Check by recording again at the end of each week.

Try it yourself: say "She asked if he walked and played" with the correct endings. Sample answer: asked = "askt" (/t/), walked = "wawkt" (/t/), played = "playd" (/d/). None of these adds an extra syllable.

Example B: A ten-minute practice session

1Warm up (1 minute). Open and close your jaw, move your lips, and make a few buzzing "zzz" sounds.
2Pairs (4 minutes). Say each pair three times: slowly, then normally. For v and w: vest, west, vest, west.
3Sentences (3 minutes). "The vet sent a vest west." Say it slowly, and then at normal speed.
4Record and compare (2 minutes). Listen to your recording and to a dictionary recording of the same words.

Short, daily sessions work better than one long session each week.

16.Key Terms

Phoneme: a sound that can change the meaning of a word.
IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet): a set of symbols in which each symbol stands for one sound.
Minimal pair: two words that differ by only one sound, such as "ship" and "sheep".
Voiced and voiceless: voiced sounds make the throat buzz (z). Voiceless sounds do not (s).
Cluster: a group of consonants together, such as "sts" in "texts".
Retroflex: made with the tongue tip curled back.
Accent: a way of pronouncing a language.
Intelligibility: how easily a listener can understand the speaker.

17.Why This Matters

Individual sound mistakes are among the most likely causes of real misunderstanding in conversation, because a confused sound can change the word the listener hears. Listeners can often work around a grammar error, but they cannot easily guess which word you meant if you say another one. Pronunciation also affects confidence. Correcting a few specific sounds, together with the stress and intonation work in this series and the habits in Fluency Building Techniques, is the final layer of speaking clearly.

Key points to remember
  • Mistakes come from spelling, your first language, and habit. There is no single list for every learner.
  • Minimal pairs are the best tool for practising a difficult contrast.
  • Voicing decides how -ed and -s endings are pronounced, with an extra syllable after t, d or hissing sounds.
  • English spelling is irregular, so check new words in a dictionary.
  • An accent is not a mistake. Aim to be easy to understand.
  • Practise one sound at a time, record yourself, and repeat a little every day.
For teachers and parents

Activity: record a short passage read by each learner. Together, list two or three sound patterns that could change the meaning of a word. Choose one pattern, practise it with minimal pairs, and record the passage again a week later.

Please note: examples are original, written for teaching. Patterns from the learner's first language vary from person to person, so use feedback from the learner's own speech. Respellings are approximate and follow a common British pronunciation.
