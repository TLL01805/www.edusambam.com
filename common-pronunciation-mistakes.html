Common Pronunciation Mistakes

A closer, sound-by-sound look at the specific errors that most often make speech harder to understand — and exactly how to correct each one.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 13 min read | English Language

Most pronunciation problems are not about a lack of talent — they are a handful of specific, learnable sounds and patterns that English spelling hides rather than reveals.

Because English spelling is inconsistent — the same letters can represent different sounds, and the same sound can be spelled several different ways — pronunciation mistakes tend to repeat across learners of many different backgrounds. This article catalogues the most common ones in detail, building on the brief overview from Topic I, with specific correction techniques for each.

Before testing your understanding with ten practice questions, work through each category below.

1.Consonant Confusions

R vs. L
right ≠ light, rice ≠ lice
✗ Merging both into one sound
✓ R: tongue curls back, doesn't touch the roof. L: tongue tip touches just behind the front teeth
B vs. V
berry ≠ very
✗ Using lips only for both sounds
✓ B: both lips together. V: top teeth touch bottom lip
S vs. SH
sip ≠ ship
✗ Using the same hissing sound for both
✓ S: flat tongue, narrow air gap. SH: tongue pulled back slightly, lips rounded
Final Consonants
cat, cats, walked
✗ Dropping the final sound entirely
✓ English relies heavily on final sounds to mark plurals and past tense — they must be pronounced

2.Vowel Length and Quality

English distinguishes many words purely by vowel length and quality — differences that don't exist in the same way in many other languages.

Ship vs. Sheep
/ɪ/ (short) vs. /iː/ (long)
✗ Treating both vowels as identical
✓ "Sheep" holds the vowel noticeably longer than "ship"
Full vs. Fool
/ʊ/ (short) vs. /uː/ (long)
✗ Merging both vowel sounds
✓ "Fool" is longer and rounder than the short vowel in "full"

3.Word Endings Learners Often Skip

Certain word endings are frequently dropped or mispronounced, which can distort grammar as much as sound.

Example: The "-ed" Ending

The past tense "-ed" is pronounced three different ways depending on the sound before it: /t/ after voiceless sounds ("walked"), /d/ after voiced sounds ("played"), and /ɪd/ after "t" or "d" sounds ("wanted," "needed").

Ending Sound"-ed" PronunciationExample
Voiceless (k, p, s, f, sh)/t/walked, hoped
Voiced (b, g, v, m, n, l)/d/played, called
t or d/ɪd/ (extra syllable)wanted, needed

4.Spelling Traps

Some of the most common mistakes come directly from assuming English spelling reflects pronunciation reliably — it frequently does not.

Though / Through / Thought
✗ Assuming "-ough" always sounds the same
✓ Each has a completely different pronunciation despite similar spelling
Colonel
✗ Reading it letter by letter as spelled
✓ Pronounced "KER-nel" — an irregular historical spelling
Real-World Example

A learner who reads "though," "through," "cough," and "enough" aloud using one consistent rule for "-ough" will mispronounce at least three of them — because English absorbed spelling from multiple historical sources without ever standardising the sound. This is why listening and repeating specific words matters more than applying spelling rules in these cases.

5.How to Actually Fix These Patterns

Correcting a specific mispronunciation is faster than general practice, because it targets exactly the sound causing trouble.

A Simple Correction Method

1. Identify one specific sound you consistently confuse (using this list or feedback from a listener).
2. Find 5–10 common words containing that sound.
3. Practise minimal pairs — two words that differ by only that one sound — aloud, daily, for one to two weeks.
4. Record yourself saying both words and compare to a native recording.

6.Why This Matters

Individual sound mistakes are the most likely thing to cause a genuine misunderstanding in conversation — more so than a grammar error, which listeners can usually work around. Paired with the stress, intonation and fluency techniques covered earlier in this series, correcting these specific sounds is the final layer of building genuinely clear, confident spoken English.
