Empathy and Social Skills: How to Understand People and Build Better Relationships

How do we really understand other people and build good relationships? See what research says about empathy, listening, friendship, online messages, feedback and apologies, with practical tools.

Last updated: 12 October 2026. Written by the EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team. This article is for general education and does not replace advice from a qualified professional.

You have probably noticed that two people can be equally clever and still have very different lives with others. One is easy to talk to, has loyal friends and settles disagreements calmly. The other is often misunderstood and lonely. The difference is rarely luck. Empathy and social skills are learnable, and the research on them is fascinating, and sometimes surprising. For example, "put yourself in their shoes" turns out to be less reliable than simply asking. In this guide, which builds on our article on Goleman's five components of EQ, we look at what scientists have found about understanding people, listening, friendship, online messages, feedback, apologies and teamwork, and we give you practical scripts to try.

Key Takeaways

Good relationships are linked with longer, healthier lives. To understand someone, asking and listening works better than imagining. Empathy can be trained, and high-quality listening helps people feel less defensive. Popular body-language rules, such as "93% of communication is non-verbal", are myths. Text messages are misread far more often than people expect. Friendships take many hours of shared time. Feedback should be about the task, not the person. A good apology takes responsibility and offers to repair. Small acts of kindness are good for the giver too.

1.Why Relationships Matter

Humans are a social species, and the health evidence is striking. A large 2010 meta-analysis (Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton) combined 148 studies with 308,849 participants and found that people with stronger social relationships had about a 50% greater likelihood of survival over the follow-up period (an average of about seven and a half years) than people with weaker relationships. A second review in 2015 of 70 studies involving more than 3.4 million people found that social isolation was linked with about a 29% higher risk of death, loneliness with about 26%, and living alone with about 32%, after taking account of age and starting health.

Two cautions are important. First, these are observational studies. They show strong links, but cannot prove that relationships alone cause longer life, because health can also affect social life. Second, what matters is quality, not just numbers: one or two trusted relationships can be worth more than a large but shallow network. Even so, the finding is a strong reason to treat social skills as a serious part of health and learning, not just as "nice to have".

2.Empathy: Ask, Don't Assume

We often hear that the way to understand someone is to "put yourself in their shoes". This is called perspective taking, and it is very popular in schools and workplaces. But does it make us more accurate? In 2018, Tal Eyal, Mary Steffel and Nicholas Epley reported 25 experiments that tested this. Participants had to judge other people's thoughts, feelings and attitudes, for instance predicting emotions from faces and body postures, spotting lies, or guessing a spouse's preferences. Some were told to take the other person's perspective first.

The results were unexpected. Although most people believed that perspective taking would help, the researchers found no consistent evidence that it improved accuracy. If anything, accuracy decreased a little, while confidence sometimes went up. In a final experiment, people who got the other person's view directly, through conversation, became more accurate. The authors' summary: understanding another person's mind is enabled by getting perspective, not just taking perspective.

Imagining another person's view can make us feel more certain, but the research found that actually asking them gave better accuracy.
Worked Example

Your friend Zainab goes quiet at lunch. Assuming: "She must be angry with me because of yesterday." You stay away, and the gap grows. Asking: "You seem quiet today. How are you doing?" She answers that she is worried about her grandmother's health and had not slept. The question gave you real information that no amount of imagining could supply.

This does not mean that thinking about others is useless. The researchers note that imagining another view can reduce some egocentric thinking, and perspective taking may help in other ways, such as being kinder. The lesson is simply that when accuracy matters, ask, listen and check.

Useful asking phrases

  • "How are you finding this?"
  • "What was that like for you?"
  • "Is it okay if I check that I understood you? You feel... because...?"
  • "What would help most right now: advice, or just someone to listen?"

3.Can Empathy Be Learned?

Yes, according to the evidence. A 2016 meta-analysis (Teding van Berkhout and Malouff) combined 18 randomised controlled trials of empathy training with 1,018 participants in total. Training programmes had a medium effect on empathy overall (an effect size of 0.63, which fell to about 0.51 after adjusting for the likelihood that unsuccessful studies were less often published). The length of training and the time between training and testing (up to six months) were not clearly linked to the size of the effect.

These results should be read with care. The studies measured empathy in different ways, often with questionnaires or role-play tasks, and the longest follow-up was six months, so we know less about long-term change in real-life behaviour.

Empathy has limits and biases

As we explained in our article on EQ, researchers separate cognitive empathy, emotional empathy and compassion. A further caution is that empathy can be biased toward people who are close to us, similar to us or whose stories are vivid. A skilled communicator notices this tendency and deliberately extends care to others, and also avoids becoming overwhelmed by absorbing every person's pain.

4.Body Language, Tone and Culture

The "93%" myth

You may have heard that "only 7% of communication is words, 38% is tone and 55% is body language". This comes from two 1967 experiments by Albert Mehrabian and colleagues. They looked at how people judge a speaker's feelings or attitudes when the words, tone and facial expression do not match, such as a person saying a positive word in a negative tone. Mehrabian himself has stated that the percentages do not apply unless a person is talking about feelings or attitudes. So it is a myth that all communication is mostly non-verbal. What the research does suggest is that when words and tone disagree, people tend to trust the tone and face.

Can faces be read?

Many people believe that a smile, scowl or frown reliably shows a specific emotion. A major 2019 review by Lisa Feldman Barrett and colleagues examined the scientific evidence and concluded that the common view is not well supported: the same facial movement can express different emotions in different situations, and a given emotion can be expressed with many different movements. For example, a scowl often communicates something other than anger. The authors note that facial movements do carry useful social information, but they are context-dependent and variable. (Some researchers still defend more universal expressions, so this remains an active scientific debate.)

The practical message is to treat body language as a clue, not a verdict. Crossed arms may mean that someone is cold, not defensive. Avoiding eye contact may mean shyness, respect or concentration. Check clues against the context and, when it matters, ask.

Culture and personal space

Rules for distance, eye contact, touch, silence and directness differ between cultures and families. The anthropologist Edward Hall (1976) described a contrast between high-context cultures, where much of the meaning is carried by the situation and relationships, and low-context cultures, where meaning is expected to be stated in words. These are tendencies, not boxes for people. A global study of personal space (Sorokowska and colleagues, 2017) surveyed 8,943 participants in 42 countries and found that preferred distances to other people varied with age, gender and region, with some variation linked to local temperature.

5.Listening: The Core Social Skill

Listening is more than staying silent. Researchers Guy Itzchakov, Avraham Kluger and colleagues have studied high-quality listening, in which the listener is attentive, empathic and non-judgemental. In a series of laboratory experiments, speakers who experienced high-quality listening reported lower social anxiety, thought more deeply about their own views, became less defensive and showed less extreme and more nuanced attitudes, without the listener trying to persuade them. The effect was seen in later studies in organisations as well. In other words, being truly listened to helps people think more clearly.

Levels of listening. The highest level, sometimes called high-quality listening, is attentive, empathic and non-judgemental.

A separate study of 180 undergraduates in peer interviews (Weger, Castle and Emmett, 2010) compared listeners who paraphrased what the speaker said with those who gave simple acknowledgements or advice. Paraphrasing made the listeners seem more socially skilled, though it did not clearly change how understood the speakers felt in that short setting. Both findings suggest that careful listening is noticed, but that its deepest benefits probably build over many conversations.

Real-World Example

A teacher notices that a student has stopped speaking in class. Instead of a lecture, she asks quietly after the lesson: "I noticed you have been quiet lately. How are things?" She listens, reflects ("It sounds like you are worried about the exam and feel alone with it"), and asks, "What would help?" The student shares that he does not understand one topic and is embarrassed to ask. A small, attentive conversation changes his week.

If you feel shy, begin with low-pressure situations and small goals, such as saying hello to one new person a day. Skills improve with practice. If worry about social situations is so strong that you avoid school, work or friends, or it causes you great distress, talk to a doctor or counsellor. Effective help is available.

7.Making and Keeping Friends

People often feel that friendships "just happen". Research suggests that they need time. In two studies (Hall, 2018), one of about 350 adults who had recently moved to a new town and one of university first-year students, the author estimated that it takes about 50 hours of time together to move from acquaintance to casual friend, about 90 hours to become a friend, and over 200 hours to become a close friend. Hours spent together just working counted less than time spent in relaxed activities and conversation.

Estimated hours of time together needed for friendships to deepen (Hall, 2018; two studies of adults who had moved to a new town and of first-year students).

These numbers are rough estimates from self-reported surveys, but the message is useful: closeness is built by repeated time together, not by a single great conversation. Other findings from social psychology fit this picture:

  • Proximity and repeated contact: In a classic study of student housing (Festinger, Schachter and Back, 1950), people were most likely to become friends with those who lived close to them, simply because they met often.
  • Taking turns to share: In laboratory experiments, pairs who took turns sharing increasingly personal information felt closer afterwards than pairs who made small talk (Aron and colleagues, 1997). Sharing should be gradual, mutual and comfortable for both people.

Being a good friend

  • Show up and keep in touch, including small gestures such as a message to say "I was thinking of you".
  • Invite. Many people wait to be asked. A simple "Would you like to study together on Friday?" starts the hours counting.
  • Celebrate their successes as well as supporting them in hard times.
  • Keep confidences and be honest, kindly.
  • Repair quickly after misunderstandings.
  • Respect differences and give each other space.

8.Texts, Emails and Group Chats

Much of our communication now happens in writing. This causes a hidden problem. In five experiments (Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng, 2005), people tried to convey tone, such as sarcasm or seriousness, by email or by voice. Senders expected recipients to understand them about 78% of the time. In reality, listeners correctly understood the tone in voice messages about 73% of the time, but only about 56% of the time over email, little better than a coin toss. The researchers explained this as egocentrism: when you write a sentence, you "hear" it in the tone you intend, and forget that the reader cannot.

Tone is easy to misread in text. In Kruger and colleagues' 2005 experiments, people believed they would be understood about 78% of the time, but the true rate for email was close to chance. (Values are rounded.)

9.Giving and Receiving Feedback

Feedback is meant to help, but it does not always do so. A large meta-analysis by Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi (1996) combined 607 effect sizes from 23,663 observations. On average, feedback improved performance (an effect size of 0.41), but in more than one third of cases, it made performance worse. The authors found that feedback worked less well when it drew attention to the self (for example "you are bad at this") and worked better when it focused on the task and how to improve it.

A practical format for giving feedback

A popular tool taught in leadership training is Situation, Behaviour, Impact (a practitioner model, not a research-tested programme). It keeps feedback concrete and about actions, not character.

StepMeaningExample
SituationWhen and where"In yesterday's group presentation..."
BehaviourWhat you saw or heard, without judging"...you spoke very quickly and looked at your notes."
ImpactThe effect on you or the task"It was hard for the audience to follow the key points."
Next stepA helpful suggestion or question"Could we practise slowing down and pausing after each point?"

Receiving feedback well

  1. Breathe and listen to the whole message before reacting.
  2. Ask for an example if it is not clear: "Can you give me one example?"
  3. Separate the task from your worth. A comment on an essay is not a verdict on you.
  4. Thank the person, then decide what to act on.
  5. Follow up later to show what you changed.

10.Saying Sorry and Forgiving

Everyone hurts someone sometimes. A good apology can repair trust, and research tells us what makes one effective. In two experiments with 755 people (Lewicki, Polin and Lount, 2016), participants read apologies that contained one, three or all six of the following elements and rated how effective they were.

ElementExampleImportance in the study
Acknowledgement of responsibility"I was wrong, and it was my fault."Most important
Offer of repair"Let me replace it" or "How can I make this right?"Second most important
Expression of regret"I am truly sorry that I hurt you."Helpful
Explanation"I was stressed and rushed, but that is not an excuse."Helpful, if not used as an excuse
Declaration of repentance"I will not do it again."Helpful
Request for forgiveness"Can you forgive me?"Helpful, but never a demand

One limitation to note: participants rated written, hypothetical apologies, so real life may be more complicated. Still, it matches common sense: "I'm sorry you feel that way" is weak, whereas "I was wrong, and I will fix it" is strong.

Worked Example

Hira borrowed Sara's notes and forgot to return them before the test. Weak: "Sorry, but you should have reminded me." Stronger: "I'm really sorry. I forgot, and it was my responsibility to return them on time. I know it made your revision harder. I'll bring them first thing tomorrow, and I'll photocopy my own notes for you as well."

Forgiving is a personal choice. It means letting go of the wish for revenge, and it does not require you to accept harmful behaviour or to stay in an unsafe situation. You can forgive and still set boundaries.

11.Teamwork and Group Projects

Working with others is one of the main places where social skills show. Two well-studied ideas help.

  • Social loafing: a large meta-analysis (Karau and Williams, 1993) found that people often put in less effort when working in a group, especially when their individual contributions cannot be identified or evaluated. The fix is clear roles and visible individual tasks.
  • Psychological safety: Amy Edmondson (1999) defined it as a shared belief that a team is safe for taking interpersonal risks, such as asking questions, admitting mistakes or disagreeing. In her study of 51 work teams in a manufacturing company, psychologically safe teams were more likely to learn and improve. Teams do better when members feel able to speak up.
ProblemHelpful response
One person does everythingDivide the work into named parts, with deadlines and a shared checklist

12.Kindness: Good for Others and for You

Acts of kindness benefit receivers, and research suggests that they may benefit givers too. In a 2008 study in Science (Dunn, Aknin and Norton), people who spent money on others reported greater happiness than those who spent it on themselves. A 2013 follow-up (Aknin and colleagues) analysed survey data from 136 countries and found that giving to others was associated with greater happiness in poor and rich countries alike. Experiments in Canada, Uganda and India showed that recalling a time of spending on others raised happiness, and an experiment in Canada and South Africa found that buying items for charity gave a bigger lift in positive feelings than buying the same items for oneself.

Notice the limits: most of this research is about spending money, the global survey data is correlational, and the experiments were done in a few countries. Even so, many researchers see kindness as a reliable way to feel connected. Kindness does not need money:

  • Listen to someone without a phone in your hand.
  • Help a classmate with a topic you understand.
  • Write a short thank-you note to a teacher or a relative.
  • Include someone who is standing alone.
  • Offer to carry, to share, or to help at home before you are asked.

13.When Social Life Is Hard: Loneliness, Shyness and Bullying

Loneliness

Loneliness is the painful gap between the connection you want and the connection you have. It is common, including among teenagers and young adults, and it is not a personal failing. Small steps help: join a regular group around an interest, say yes to invitations, invite one person for a simple activity, volunteer, reach out to an old friend and be patient, remembering that friendships need hours. If loneliness is heavy and lasts, speak to a trusted person or a counsellor.

Bullying

If someone is bullying you, it is not your fault. Tell a trusted adult such as a parent, teacher or counsellor, keep a record of what happened (dates, messages), avoid retaliating, and stay near supportive friends. If you see bullying, you can help by supporting the target privately, refusing to join in, and reporting it to an adult. Schools and many organisations have policies and helplines for bullying and online harassment.

Important

If you or someone you know is in danger, or if sadness, anxiety or loneliness makes you think about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or a crisis helpline in your country, or speak to a trusted adult or doctor right away. You do not have to manage this alone.

A Closing Thought

Good relationships are not a gift that some people receive and others do not. They are built from small repeated actions: asking instead of assuming, listening with full attention, writing messages with care, giving honest and kind feedback, apologising properly, and showing up. None of these skills needs special talent, but all of them need practice. Choose one, try it today, and notice the difference it makes to the people around you, and to you.
