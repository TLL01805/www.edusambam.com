R7: Organising and Communicating Information

A focused Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 unit on organising selected information and communicating it clearly for a specific purpose and audience.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|Unit 15|English Language

Once useful information has been selected and developed, the next step is to organise it and communicate it effectively. A strong response has a clear purpose, a logical order and language that helps the reader follow the information.

1. Unit Purpose

R7: Organising and Communicating Information focuses on turning relevant information into a clear, well-ordered response. Good organisation helps the reader understand what matters, how ideas relate to one another and why each point has been included.

EDUSAMBAM principle

Choose → Group → Order → Connect → Communicate → Check.

2. Learning Outcomes

  • identify the purpose and intended audience of a response;
  • group related information together;
  • choose a logical order for ideas;
  • distinguish main points from supporting details;
  • use paragraphs or sections purposefully;
  • use linking language accurately;
  • maintain a clear focus throughout a response;
  • adapt the amount of explanation to the task;
  • communicate information clearly and precisely;
  • avoid unnecessary repetition;
  • check that the final response is complete and coherent;
  • revise organisation when ideas do not follow logically.

3. Why Organisation Matters

Even accurate information can be difficult to understand when it is presented in a confusing order. Organisation allows the reader to see the structure of the response.

Weak organisationEffective organisation
Ideas appear in random order.Ideas follow a clear and purposeful sequence.
Related points are separated.Related points are grouped together.
Examples appear before the point they support.Supporting details follow the relevant point.
The conclusion introduces a new major idea.The conclusion brings the response together.

4. Start with Purpose and Audience

Before organising information, identify what the response is trying to achieve and who will read it. A report, explanation, set of instructions and summary may contain similar information but require different organisation.

PurposeLikely organisation
ExplainPoint → explanation → supporting detail
ReportFindings → evidence → implications/recommendations
Give instructionsSteps in the order they should be completed
SummariseMain points arranged according to the source's central ideas

Always let the task determine the organisation rather than forcing every answer into the same structure.

5. Group Related Information

When several pieces of information belong to the same idea, place them together. Grouping prevents the reader from having to search through the response for connected details.

Example

Selected information: longer opening hours; evening study sessions; extra computers; quiet study areas; research workshops.

Possible groups:

Access: longer opening hours; evening study sessions.

Facilities: extra computers; quiet study areas.

Skills: research workshops.

Grouping is especially useful when a task asks you to explain several different ways in which something helps a group of people.

6. Choose a Logical Order

After grouping information, decide the most useful order. The correct order depends on the purpose of the response.

Ordering methodUseful when
ChronologicalEvents or procedures happen over time.
Step-by-stepThe reader needs to follow a process.
General to specificA main idea is followed by details and examples.
Cause to effectThe response explains why something happens and what follows.
Problem to solutionThe task asks for difficulties and ways of addressing them.
ImportanceThe task or audience benefits from prioritising the strongest points.

7. Main Point and Supporting Detail

A well-organised paragraph normally has a clear central idea. Supporting details should help explain, prove or illustrate that idea.

Paragraph model

Main point: The centre provides students with better opportunities for independent study.

Support: It offers computers, reference books and quiet study areas.

Development: These resources allow students to complete research and work without the distractions they may face elsewhere.

If a detail does not support the paragraph's main point, consider moving it to another paragraph or removing it.

8. Paragraphs as Units of Meaning

A paragraph should normally have a recognisable purpose. Starting a new paragraph can signal a change of idea, stage, time, viewpoint or topic.

  • Begin with a clear focus where appropriate.
  • Keep related information together.
  • Use supporting details that belong to the same idea.
  • Move to a new paragraph when the focus changes.
  • Avoid making every sentence a separate paragraph.

9. Linking Ideas Clearly

Connections between ideas should be easy for the reader to follow. Linking words and phrases can show addition, contrast, cause, result, sequence or example.

RelationshipExamples
Additionalso, in addition, furthermore
Sequencefirst, next, afterwards, finally
Causebecause, since, as
Resulttherefore, as a result, consequently
Contrasthowever, although, whereas
Examplefor example, for instance, such as

Do not use a connector merely because it sounds formal. The relationship must be accurate.

10. Signposting

Signposting helps readers understand where they are in a response. It is particularly useful in longer answers.

Useful signposts

Beginning: The main advantage is...

Adding: Another important factor is...

Changing focus: A different issue is...

Contrast: However, the evidence also shows...

Conclusion: Overall, the information suggests...

Use signposts naturally. Too many can make writing mechanical.

11. Communicate with Precision

Clear communication depends on accurate word choice. Avoid vague expressions when the source allows a more precise statement.

Less preciseMore precise
It helps people a lot.It gives students access to additional study resources.
There are many things there.The centre provides computers, reference books and quiet workspaces.
It makes things better.It reduces waiting time and makes the service more convenient.

Precision does not mean using complicated vocabulary. It means choosing words that communicate the intended meaning accurately.

12. Avoid Repetition

Repeating a point can make a response seem longer without making it more informative. Before repeating an idea, ask whether the new sentence adds a different detail or explanation.

Compare

Repetitive: The centre is useful. It is helpful for students. Students find it useful.

Improved: The centre is useful because it provides students with computers and quiet spaces for independent study.

13. Keep Every Part Focused on the Task

A response may contain accurate information that is still irrelevant to the question. Good organisation removes or reduces material that does not help answer the task.

Focus test

For each paragraph, ask: “If I removed this paragraph, would the answer lose information that the task requires?”

If the answer is no, reconsider whether the paragraph belongs in the response.

14. Communicating Information from Two Sources

When information comes from more than one source, organise it so the reader can understand how the sources relate. Depending on the task, this may mean combining similar points, contrasting different views or arranging information under common themes.

Source ASource BPossible organisation
Walking reduces short car journeys.Safe footpaths encourage walking.Group both under the theme of encouraging active travel.
One group supports longer opening hours.Another group prefers more weekend services.Present the different priorities as contrasting needs.

Do not merge two sources in a way that changes what either source actually says.

15. Worked Cambridge-Style Example

Source text

“The town introduced a community garden on a previously unused plot. Residents can grow vegetables there and attend weekend gardening sessions. The project has brought together people of different ages. Local organisers also use the garden to teach children about plants and food.”

Task: Explain the different benefits of the community garden.

Step 1 — Group: food growing; social interaction; education.

Step 2 — Order: practical benefit → community benefit → educational benefit.

Step 3 — Communicate: The garden provides a practical opportunity for residents to grow vegetables. It also brings people of different ages together, strengthening community interaction. In addition, children can learn about plants and food through activities held in the garden.

Why it works: The response groups related information, follows a logical order and communicates each benefit clearly without unnecessary repetition.

16. Common R7 Mistakes

  • Random ordering: points appear without a clear reason for their sequence.
  • Mixed themes: unrelated ideas are placed in the same paragraph.
  • Weak paragraph focus: the reader cannot identify the main point.
  • Repetition: the response repeats information instead of adding value.
  • Unclear links: connectors do not match the relationship between ideas.
  • Irrelevant detail: accurate information is included even though it does not answer the task.
  • Overcomplication: unnecessarily difficult vocabulary hides a simple idea.
  • Unsupported organisation: information from sources is combined in a way that changes their meaning.

17. Exam Strategy: GROUP → ORDER → SIGNAL → CHECK

Four-step R7 method

1. GROUP: Put related information together.

2. ORDER: Decide the sequence that best serves the task.

3. SIGNAL: Use paragraphs and accurate linking language to guide the reader.

4. CHECK: Confirm that every part is relevant, clear and complete.

This method builds directly on the previous skills: selected information is developed first, then organised into a response that the reader can follow easily.

18. Practice Task

Organise and communicate

“A school introduced a lunchtime reading area. It contains fiction and non-fiction books, comfortable seating and a small discussion space. Students can recommend books for the collection. Teachers sometimes use the area for short reading activities.”

Task: Explain how the reading area supports students and how you would organise your response.

Suggested plan:

Paragraph 1: Resources and comfortable space — books and seating support reading.

Paragraph 2: Student participation — recommendations give students a role in developing the collection.

Paragraph 3: Learning use — teachers can use the area for reading activities.

The key is to group related information and make the purpose of each paragraph clear.

19. Final R7 Checklist

  • Have I identified the purpose of the response?
  • Have I considered the intended audience?
  • Have I grouped related information?
  • Have I chosen a logical order?
  • Does each paragraph have a clear focus?
  • Have I separated main points from supporting details?
  • Are my linking words accurate?
  • Have I avoided unnecessary repetition?
  • Is every part relevant to the task?
  • Is my language precise and easy to follow?
  • Have I preserved the meaning of the source information?
Key takeaway

Good information becomes effective communication through organisation. Group related ideas, put them in a purposeful order, signal the relationships clearly and check that the final response stays focused on the task.
