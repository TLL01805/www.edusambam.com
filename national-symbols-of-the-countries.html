National Symbols of the Countries

Animals, birds, flowers, trees, emblems and mottos: what national symbols are, why countries choose them, and a reference table for 97 countries.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|20 min read|General Knowledge |Last updated: October 2026

Every country has a few objects and living things that stand for it: an animal, a bird, a flower, a tree, a motto, a song. Some are chosen by law; others simply grow into place over centuries. This article explains what national symbols are, why countries choose them, and how to read them. It also gives a reference table of national symbols for 97 countries, and a close comparison of Pakistan and India.

In this article

What national symbols are · the main types · why countries choose them · official or unofficial? · Pakistan and India compared · symbols of 97 countries · patterns in the table · symbols and conservation · four worked examples · key terms · common mistakes · practice questions and a quiz.

1.What Are National Symbols?

A national symbol is an object, living thing, design, song or phrase that represents a country and its people. A national animal, for example, tells us something that the country wants to say about itself: perhaps its wildlife, its history or the values it admires, such as courage or patience.

It helps to separate three groups:

  • Official symbols are chosen by a law, a government proclamation or a formal decision, so there is a clear record of them.
  • Traditional symbols are used for a very long time and are widely accepted, even though no law may have declared them.
  • Popular symbols are named by the public, a group of experts or a magazine, and the government may not have adopted them.

In this article's table, an asterisk (*) after a name marks a symbol that is widely recognised or traditionally associated with the country but may not have been formally declared. Where a country has no widely recognised symbol of a type, you will see a dash (—). A dash does not mean the country has nothing; it means we could not list a well-established entry.

2.The Main Types of National Symbols

Most countries have some of the following. The flag is a very big topic, so it has its own article: see Flags of the World. The other symbols are the focus here.

Figure 1. Common national symbols. The dashed circle around the flag shows that it is covered in a separate article.
  • Emblem or coat of arms: a design, often a shield with animals, plants or tools, used on official documents and buildings.
  • National anthem: a song played at official ceremonies and sports events.
  • Motto: a short phrase that sums up a country's ideals.
  • National animal and bird: creatures that represent the country's nature or character.
  • National flower and tree: plants that are native, beautiful, useful or meaningful in the country's history.
  • Others: some countries also name a national fruit, river, sport, dish or dress.

3.Why Countries Choose the Symbols They Do

When you read the table in section 7, you will notice that symbols are rarely random. The main reasons are:

  • Nature and geography. Many countries pick a species that lives mainly or only in their land. For example, the Philippine eagle lives only in the Philippines, and the kiwi is found only in New Zealand.
  • Strength and courage. Eagles, lions, tigers and bears appear again and again because people link them with power and bravery.
  • History and heraldry. Many European symbols come from medieval coats of arms. The lion on several European coats of arms, and the unicorn associated with Scotland, are examples.
  • Culture and daily life. Plants that are used for food, medicine, building or religion often become symbols. The mango in Pakistan, India and Bangladesh is both a favourite fruit and a familiar sight.
  • Beauty and meaning. Flowers such as the lotus, jasmine and the cherry blossom carry ideas such as purity, welcome and the passing of seasons in different cultures.

4.Official or Unofficial? How a Symbol Gets Its Status

A symbol becomes official in different ways in different countries. In some, a law is passed; in others, a ruler or government issues a proclamation; in others, a tradition simply lasts.

  • By law. In Canada, the beaver and the Canadian horse have official symbol status, given by acts of Parliament (the beaver in 1975 and the Canadian horse in 2002). In the United States, the bison was made the national mammal by law in 2016.
  • By long tradition. The bald eagle has appeared on the Great Seal of the United States since 1782, and it was formally named the national bird by law in late 2024.
  • By public vote, but not law. In 2016 the magazine Canadian Geographic named the gray jay "Canada's national bird" after a public poll and an expert panel. The government did not adopt it, so the gray jay is a popular choice, not an official symbol.
Worked example 1: Is this symbol official?

Question: A website says the gray jay is the national bird of Canada. Is that official?

Step 1. Ask: is there a law or government proclamation? For Canada's beaver and Canadian horse, yes. For the gray jay, no.

Step 2. Ask: who named it? A magazine and a panel of experts, not the government.

Step 3. Ask: does the government use it officially? Not as an official bird symbol.

Answer: It is a well-known popular choice, but it is not an official national symbol, so we mark it with an asterisk.

5.Pakistan and India Compared

These two neighbouring countries became independent in 1947 and share a lot of geography and culture, but their national symbols are different. The table below puts them side by side.

Interesting details. Pakistan's anthem is unusual because the music was composed first, by Ahmed G. Chagla, and the words were written afterwards by the poet Hafeez Jalandhari to fit it. India's emblem shows three lions, though the original sculpture has four standing back to back; the fourth is hidden from view. India has both an anthem and a national song with equal honour. A common mistake is to say hockey is India's official national sport: the Indian government has said that no sport has been officially declared the national sport.

Worked example 2: Compare two countries

Question: What do Pakistan and India share, and how do they differ?

Step 1. Read across each row of the table above. The rows where both columns match are the shared symbols.

Step 2. Only one row matches: the national fruit, the mango.

Step 3. Look at the differences. Pakistan chose a mountain animal (markhor) and a partridge; India chose a big cat (tiger) and the peacock.

Answer: They share the mango, but their animals, birds, flowers and trees differ, because each country picked symbols that reflect its own landscapes and traditions.

6.Reading Symbols: Four Short Stories

The dodo of Mauritius. The dodo was a flightless bird that lived only on the island of Mauritius. It became extinct in the 1600s, soon after humans arrived. It now appears on the coat of arms of Mauritius, which turns an extinct creature into a reminder of why protecting wildlife matters.

The unicorn of Scotland. The unicorn is a mythical animal, but it has been a symbol of Scotland since the Middle Ages. It shows that a national animal does not have to be real. Singapore's merlion, a lion-headed fish, is another mythical symbol.

The kangaroo and emu of Australia. Both animals appear on Australia's coat of arms and are found only in Australia, so they say "this is Australia" very clearly.

The cow of Nepal. The cow is Nepal's national animal. The cow is respected in Hinduism, the main religion of Nepal, so this choice reflects religion and culture, not wildlife alone.

8.Patterns in the Table

Looking across the table, some symbols appear again and again. The chart counts how many countries in this table include each symbol among their listed animals, birds or flowers. It does not give worldwide totals.

Figure 2. How often some symbols appear in the table in section 7 (counts for the listed countries only).

Eagles come first, which fits the idea that people link them with strength and freedom. Lions, orchids and lotuses or water lilies follow, and parrots, tigers and condors also appear several times. Orchids, parrots and condors are mostly found in the warmer parts of the Americas, where these plants and birds live. Notice that many entries are shown "on the coat of arms" rather than as a named national bird, because coats of arms were a major source of national symbols.

Clues: (a) African country. (b) The national animal is an antelope that leaps. (c) The national flower is a large protea.

9.Symbols and Wildlife Conservation

Some national symbols are in danger. The Philippine eagle is listed as critically endangered, and the Bengal tiger as endangered. The dodo is gone for ever. Being a national symbol can help: people take pride in the animal, and governments can protect it. The markhor, Pakistan's national animal, was moved to a better category on the IUCN Red List (the international list of threatened species) in the 2010s, and conservationists have linked this to protection programmes that involve local communities. A symbol is most useful when it makes people care about the real animal.

11.Common Mistakes and Myths

  • "Every country has an official national animal, bird and flower." No. Many countries have only some, and some have none that are formally declared.
  • "A national animal is found only in that country." Not always. The Bengal tiger is a symbol of both India and Bangladesh, and the brown bear appears in Finland and Russia.
  • "A national symbol cannot be mythical." It can: the unicorn of Scotland and Singapore's merlion are examples.
  • "Hockey is India's official national sport." The Indian government has said no sport is officially declared.
  • "A popular vote makes a symbol official." Not unless the government adopts it. The gray jay is the example.
  • "Symbols never change." They can change over time, so check current sources.
Worked example 4: Read a table row

Row: Guyana — Jaguar — Hoatzin — Giant water lily.

Step 1. Find the column headings: national animal, national bird, national flower or tree.

Step 2. Read across: the animal is the jaguar, the bird is the hoatzin, the flower is the giant water lily (Victoria amazonica).

Step 3. Think about meaning: all three live in or near the rainforest and rivers of the Amazon region, so the symbols point to Guyana's natural heritage.

Answer: Guyana's symbols emphasise its rainforest nature.

12.Summary

  • National symbols are objects, living things, designs, songs and phrases that represent a country.
  • They can be official (by law), traditional (by long use) or popular (named by the public or a group).
  • Countries choose symbols for reasons of nature, strength, history, culture and beauty.
  • Pakistan and India share the mango as national fruit, but their other symbols differ.
  • Some symbols are mythical, and some stand for animals that are threatened or extinct.
  • Always check important details against an official source, because symbols can change.

13.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. Name four types of national symbol.

Any four of: emblem or coat of arms, national anthem, motto, national animal, national bird, national flower, national tree, flag.

2. What is the difference between an official and a popular symbol?

An official symbol is chosen by law or a government decision. A popular symbol is named by the public or a group, and the government may not have adopted it.

3. Which two animals appear on Australia's coat of arms?

The kangaroo and the emu.

4. Why is the dodo on the coat of arms of Mauritius an important reminder?

The dodo became extinct soon after humans arrived, so it reminds people why protecting wildlife matters.

5. What do Pakistan and India share among their symbols?

The mango as national fruit.

6. What does the asterisk (*) in the table mean?

The symbol is widely recognised or traditional, but may not have been formally declared by law.

Discussion prompts: Why do countries need symbols? What would you choose for your country and why?
