Gravity and Space: Why Things Fall and Planets Orbit

Why apples fall and planets orbit: gravity, satellites, the Solar System, the Moon, the seasons, stars and galaxies, explained with diagrams, animations and worked examples.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 19 min read | Science | Last updated: October 2026

According to a famous story, Isaac Newton was prompted to think about gravity when he saw an apple fall from a tree. The story is probably embellished, but the question he asked was real and enormous: if gravity pulls an apple to the ground, could the same force also hold the Moon in its orbit? His answer, published in 1687, was yes, and it unified the physics of falling objects on Earth with the motion of the Moon, the planets and the tides. Gravity is the force that shapes the universe: it pulled the Sun together from a cloud of gas, keeps the planets in orbit, and holds galaxies together. This article explains how gravity works, how orbits and satellites work, and what we know about the Solar System, the stars and the universe.

What you will be able to do
  • Use Newton's law of gravitation, g = GM ÷ r², and the inverse-square law.
  • Explain orbits and satellites, and calculate orbital speed and period.
  • Describe the Solar System, the Moon's phases, eclipses, tides, day and night, and the seasons.
  • Describe how stars form and end, and give the scale of galaxies and the universe.

1.The Force of Gravity

Gravity is the force of attraction between any two objects that have mass. It is always attractive, it acts over any distance (becoming weaker as the distance grows), and it is by far the weakest of the fundamental forces. We notice it only when one of the masses is enormous, such as a planet. Newton's law of universal gravitation gives the force between two masses:

Formula

F = G × m₁ × m₂ ÷ r²

m₁ and m₂ are the masses (kg), r is the distance between their centres (m), and G is the gravitational constant, 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². Two things follow: the force is proportional to each mass, and it follows an inverse-square law: double the distance and the force becomes one quarter; triple the distance and it becomes one ninth.

Figure 1. Gravity weakens with the square of the distance. R is the radius of the planet. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)
Worked example 1: the pull of the Earth on the Moon

The Earth's mass is 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, the Moon's mass is 7.35 × 10²² kg, and the distance between their centres is 3.84 × 10⁸ m. Find the gravitational force.

Step 1. F = G m₁ m₂ ÷ r² = (6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ × 7.35 × 10²²) ÷ (3.84 × 10⁸)².

Step 2. Numerator ≈ 2.93 × 10³⁷; denominator ≈ 1.47 × 10¹⁷.

Step 3. F ≈ 2.0 × 10²⁰ N. By Newton's third law, the Moon pulls on the Earth with the same size of force.

2.Gravitational Field Strength and Weight

Around any mass there is a gravitational field. The gravitational field strength g at a point is the force on each kilogram placed there (N/kg), which is also the free-fall acceleration (m/s²). For a planet of mass M and radius R, at its surface:

Formulae

g = G M ÷ R²  (and at a distance r from the centre, g = G M ÷ r²)

Weight W = m g

Worked example 2: g on the Moon

The Moon has M = 7.35 × 10²² kg and R = 1.74 × 10⁶ m. Find g at its surface.

g = GM ÷ R² = (6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ × 7.35 × 10²²) ÷ (1.74 × 10⁶)² = 4.91 × 10¹² ÷ 3.03 × 10¹² ≈ 1.6 N/kg, about one-sixth of the value on Earth.

Even on Earth, g is not exactly the same everywhere: it is slightly smaller at the equator (about 9.78) than at the poles (about 9.83), because of the Earth's rotation and slightly flattened shape, and it decreases with altitude. The standard value used for reference is 9.80665 N/kg.

Worked example 3: g at the height of the space station

The International Space Station (ISS) orbits about 400 km above the surface. The Earth's radius is 6371 km and GM for the Earth is 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s². Find g at that height.

r = 6371 + 400 = 6771 km = 6.771 × 10⁶ m. g = GM ÷ r² = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ ÷ (6.771 × 10⁶)² = 8.7 N/kg.

That is about 89% of the surface value. Astronauts on the ISS feel "weightless" not because gravity has disappeared but because they and the station are in continuous free fall together around the Earth.

3.Orbits and Satellites

Newton imagined a cannon on a very high mountain. A ball fired slowly lands nearby. A ball fired faster lands farther away. Since the Earth's surface curves away, there is a speed at which the ball falls towards the Earth but keeps missing it; it is then in orbit. An orbit is a continuous free fall around a body. The only force is gravity, and it supplies the centripetal force that bends the path into a circle or ellipse.

For a circular orbit of radius r around a mass M, setting gravity equal to the centripetal requirement gives:

Formulae

v = √(G M ÷ r)  and  T = 2πr ÷ v

A satellite closer to the Earth must travel faster. The orbital speed does not depend on the satellite's own mass.

Worked example 4: the ISS orbit

Find the orbital speed and period of the ISS at r = 6.771 × 10⁶ m. (GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s²)

Step 1. v = √(GM ÷ r) = √(3.986 × 10¹⁴ ÷ 6.771 × 10⁶) = √(5.89 × 10⁷) ≈ 7670 m/s ≈ 7.7 km/s (over 27 000 km/h).

Step 2. T = 2πr ÷ v = 2π × 6.771 × 10⁶ ÷ 7670 ≈ 5550 s ≈ 92 minutes.

So the ISS circles the Earth about 15–16 times a day, and its crew see a sunrise roughly every 90 minutes.

  • Low Earth orbit (a few hundred to about 2000 km): the ISS, Earth-observation and many communication satellites.
  • Navigation satellites: GPS satellites orbit at about 20 200 km with a period of about 12 hours. Receiving signals from several of them lets a receiver compute its position.
  • Geostationary orbit: at about 35 786 km above the equator the period is 24 hours, matching the Earth's rotation, so the satellite stays above one point of the Earth. Used for TV broadcasting and weather satellites.

4.Kepler's Laws

Before Newton, the German astronomer Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) analysed careful observations of the planets and found three patterns, published between 1609 and 1619:

  1. Planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus (not in perfect circles).
  2. A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so a planet moves faster when it is nearer the Sun.
  3. The square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun: T² ∝ r³.
Worked example 5: Kepler's third law

Mars orbits at about 1.52 AU from the Sun. Using Earth's values (T = 1 year at 1 AU), find the length of the Martian year. (1 AU is the average Earth–Sun distance, about 1.50 × 10¹¹ m.)

T² = r³ → T = r^1.5 = 1.52^1.5 = 1.52 × √1.52 = 1.52 × 1.233 ≈ 1.87 years (about 685 Earth days; the measured value is 687 days).

5.The Solar System

The Solar System is the Sun and everything bound to it by gravity. The Sun contains about 99.86% of the total mass. It is a star: a huge ball of hot gas, mainly hydrogen and helium, in which nuclear fusion releases energy. About 4.6 billion years ago it formed, with the planets, from a collapsing cloud of gas and dust.

One astronomical unit (AU) is the average Earth–Sun distance: 149 597 870 700 m, about 150 million km. Light from the Sun takes about 8.3 minutes to reach us.

  • Rocky (terrestrial) planets are small, dense and close to the Sun. Venus has a thick carbon dioxide atmosphere and a surface hot enough to melt lead, so it is the hottest planet, hotter even than Mercury.
  • Gas giants (Jupiter, Saturn) are enormous and mostly hydrogen and helium. Ice giants (Uranus, Neptune) contain more "ices" such as water, ammonia and methane around a rocky core.
  • Dwarf planets such as Pluto, Ceres and Eris are round bodies that have not cleared their orbits of other objects. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union (IAU) adopted the definition that placed Pluto in this class.
  • Asteroids are rocky bodies, mostly in the belt between Mars and Jupiter. Comets are balls of ice and dust whose tails of gas and dust form when they approach the Sun. Beyond Neptune lie the Kuiper belt and, further out, the hypothesised Oort cloud.

6.The Moon: Phases, Eclipses and Tides

The Moon orbits the Earth once every 27.3 days, and takes about 29.5 days to go through its cycle of phases (the phases take longer because the Earth also moves round the Sun). Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun. What changes is how much of the lit half we can see from Earth. The Moon always shows nearly the same face to us because it rotates once for each orbit.

Figure 3. The Moon's orbit seen from above the Earth's north pole. The half of the Moon facing the Sun is always lit; the phase depends on how much of that lit half faces the Earth. The grey dot shows the Moon moving round its orbit. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram; sizes and distances not to scale.)
  • Solar eclipse: the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth (at new Moon) and its shadow falls on part of the Earth. Total eclipses are seen only in a narrow path where the Moon appears just large enough to cover the Sun. Never look at the Sun directly; use certified eclipse glasses.
  • Lunar eclipse: the Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon (at full Moon) and its shadow falls on the Moon, often turning it a coppery red. It is safe to watch with the naked eye.
  • Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5° to the Earth's orbit, so the three bodies seldom line up exactly.
  • Tides: the Moon's gravity pulls more strongly on the near side of the Earth than on its centre and far side, producing two tidal bulges in the oceans, so most coasts have two high tides a day. The Sun adds a smaller effect. At new and full Moon the Sun and Moon pull in line, giving larger spring tides; at the quarter Moons they pull at right angles, giving smaller neap tides.

7.Day, Night and the Seasons

The Earth spins on its axis once in about 24 hours (precisely, 23 hours 56 minutes relative to the stars), which gives day and night. It orbits the Sun once in about 365.25 days. The Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.4° to the plane of its orbit, and it points in nearly the same direction in space all year. This tilt, not the distance from the Sun, causes the seasons. (In fact the Earth is nearest to the Sun in early January, during the northern winter.)

Figure 4. The seasons are caused by the tilt of the Earth's axis. The hemisphere tilted towards the Sun receives more direct sunlight and longer days. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram; orbit and sizes not to scale.)

When a hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, the Sun climbs higher in the sky, sunlight strikes the ground more directly (so the same energy is spread over a smaller area), and the days are longer. Near the poles there are weeks of continuous daylight in summer and continuous darkness in winter.

8.Stars and Their Life Cycles

Stars form inside giant clouds of gas and dust called nebulae. Gravity pulls a clump together; as it shrinks it heats up until the core is hot and dense enough for hydrogen to fuse into helium. At that point the star begins its long main sequence life, in which the outward pressure of the hot gas balances the inward pull of gravity. Our Sun is about halfway through this stage, with roughly five billion years to go.

What happens next depends on the star's mass:

  • Stars like the Sun: when the hydrogen in the core is used up, the star swells into a red giant, then sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a hot, Earth-sized core called a white dwarf that slowly cools.
  • Much more massive stars: they burn fuel quickly, expand into red supergiants, and end in a violent explosion called a supernova. The core that remains collapses into a neutron star or, if massive enough, a black hole, an object whose gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape from inside its event horizon.
Figure 5. The life cycles of stars. The path a star follows depends mainly on its mass. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)

Stars are very far away. The nearest star after the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 4.2 light-years away: its light takes about 4.2 years to reach us, even though light covers 300 000 km every second. When we look at stars, we see them as they were in the past.

Worked example 6: distance in kilometres

One light-year is the distance light travels in one year. Find it in kilometres, using c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.

1 year ≈ 365.25 × 24 × 3600 = 3.156 × 10⁷ s. Distance = ct = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 3.156 × 10⁷ = 9.47 × 10¹⁵ m = 9.47 × 10¹² km (the accepted value is 9.46 × 10¹² km).

Proxima Centauri at 4.2 light-years is about 4.2 × 9.46 × 10¹² ≈ 4.0 × 10¹³ km away.

9.Galaxies and the Universe

Stars are grouped into huge systems called galaxies, held together by gravity. Our Sun is one of hundreds of billions of stars in the Milky Way, a spiral galaxy about 100 000 light-years across. The Sun orbits the Milky Way's centre once every 220–250 million years. There are billions of other galaxies; the nearest large one, Andromeda, is about 2.5 million light-years away.

In the 1920s Edwin Hubble showed that distant galaxies are moving away from us, and the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it recedes. The light from distant galaxies is stretched to longer wavelengths, an effect called redshift. This tells us that the universe is expanding. Running the expansion backwards leads to the Big Bang theory: the universe began in an extremely hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago. A major piece of evidence is the cosmic microwave background, faint microwave radiation filling the whole sky, discovered in 1965 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson. Astronomers also find that visible matter is only a small part of the universe: most of it appears to be dark matter and dark energy, whose nature is still unknown.

11.Common Mistakes to Avoid

Watch out
  • "There is no gravity in space." Gravity extends everywhere. Astronauts feel weightless because they are in free fall, not because gravity is absent.
  • "Heavier objects fall faster." Without air resistance all objects fall with the same acceleration g.
  • Using the height instead of the distance from the centre. In F = Gm₁m₂ ÷ r², r is measured to the centre of the planet.
  • Forgetting to square r. Doubling the distance reduces the force to a quarter, not a half.
  • "Seasons are caused by the Earth being closer to the Sun." They are caused by the tilt of the axis.
  • "The Moon makes its own light." It reflects sunlight, and the phases are not caused by the Earth's shadow.
  • Calling a light-year a unit of time. It is a unit of distance.
  • "The Big Bang was an explosion in space." It was the expansion of space itself, everywhere, from a hot, dense state.

12.Check Your Understanding

Try each question before opening the answer.

1. By what factor does the gravitational force change if the distance between two masses is tripled?

It becomes one ninth (1 ÷ 3²).

2. What is the weight on Mars of a 50 kg object? (g = 3.7 N/kg)

W = mg = 50 × 3.7 = 185 N. Its mass is still 50 kg.

3. Why do astronauts in orbit feel weightless?

They and their spacecraft are in free fall around the Earth, accelerating at the same rate, so nothing pushes on them to give the sensation of weight.

4. A satellite's orbital radius is increased. Does it need to move faster or slower to stay in a circular orbit?

Slower, because v = √(GM ÷ r) decreases as r increases.

5. Use Kepler's third law to estimate the orbital period of a planet at 4.0 AU from a Sun-like star.

T = 4.0^1.5 = 4.0 × 2.0 = 8.0 years.

6. At what phase of the Moon can a solar eclipse occur, and why?

At new Moon, when the Moon is between the Sun and the Earth.

7. Why does the Southern Hemisphere have summer when the Northern Hemisphere has winter?

When the north is tilted away from the Sun, the south is tilted towards it, so it receives more direct sunlight and longer days.

8. What will probably happen to a star much more massive than the Sun at the end of its life?

It will explode as a supernova and leave behind a neutron star or a black hole.

Summary

  • Gravity is an attractive force between all masses: F = Gm₁m₂ ÷ r², an inverse-square law. Field strength g = GM ÷ r².
  • Orbits are continuous free fall; v = √(GM ÷ r). Kepler: elliptical orbits, equal areas in equal times, T² ∝ r³.
  • The Solar System has eight planets, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets, all orbiting the Sun.
  • The Moon's phases, eclipses, tides, day and night, and the seasons all follow from the geometry of orbits and the Earth's tilt.
  • Stars form in nebulae and end as white dwarfs, neutron stars or black holes. The universe is expanding and about 13.8 billion years old.
For teachers and parents

Activities that need little equipment: use a torch and two balls to model the Moon's phases; stretch a rubber sheet and roll marbles to show orbits; place a globe near a lamp, tilted, to demonstrate seasons; build a scale model of the Solar System along a corridor or playground (for example, Neptune about 30 times as far from the Sun as the Earth); use a free stargazing app to find planets and the Moon, and observe the Moon's phase for a month. Never look directly at the Sun, and use certified solar viewers for eclipses. Numerical values are rounded; please check that they match the data booklet used by your examination board.
