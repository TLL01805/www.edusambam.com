Idioms & Phrases: The Complete Guide

"It's raining cats and dogs" has nothing to do with animals. Idioms are the part of English that dictionaries alone can never fully explain — here is how they work, and how to use them naturally.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 18 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Vocabulary

If you translated "break the ice" word for word into another language, it would make no sense. That is the heart of every idiom: the meaning lives in the whole expression, not in the separate words. Idioms are the part of English that vocabulary lists alone can never fully teach.

This guide explains what idioms are and how they behave, how they differ from proverbs, phrasal verbs, collocations and similes, and where they come from, with some honest warnings about "origin stories" that turn out to be guesses. It gives you a working reference list of idioms by theme with example sentences, rules for using idioms well, a method for learning them, and two worked examples, a checklist, common mistakes and ten practice questions.

It follows Synonyms & Antonyms and leads into Formal vs. Informal English, because deciding when to use an idiom is as important as knowing what it means.

1.What Is an Idiom?

An idiom is a fixed expression whose overall meaning cannot be worked out from the meanings of its separate words. Native speakers learn idioms as complete units, just as they learn single words.

IdiomLiteral words suggestActual meaning
break the icesmash frozen waterease tension at the start of a meeting
under the weatherbeneath the skyfeeling slightly unwell
a piece of cakea slice of dessertsomething very easy
kick the bucketstrike a pail with your footdie (informal, sometimes humorous)

The word for this feature is non-compositional: the meaning is not built up from the parts like a wall from bricks. That is why a learner who knows every word in "kick the bucket" can still be completely unable to understand it.

Idioms can also be used literally. A farmer who really kicks a bucket across the yard is not using an idiom. Context tells you which meaning is intended. This is one reason why idioms need sentences, not just definitions.

2.Idioms and Their Relatives

Several kinds of expression are often mistaken for idioms. Knowing the differences helps you use the right word when you talk about language, and helps you to understand dictionaries.

TypeWhat it isExample
IdiomFixed expression with a non-literal meaningspill the beans (reveal a secret)
ProverbA complete sentence of traditional advice or wisdomA stitch in time saves nine.
Phrasal verbA verb plus a small word (particle) that works as one verb. Some are literal; many are idiomatic.sit down (literal); give up (idiomatic: stop trying)
CollocationWords that naturally go together; each word keeps its normal meaningstrong coffee, heavy rain, make a decision
Simile (fixed)A comparison with "as…as" or "like" that has become a set expressionas cool as a cucumber; as quiet as a mouse
Binomial (pair)Two words joined by "and" or "or" in a fixed ordersalt and pepper; now or never
ClichéAn expression so overused that it has lost its forceat the end of the day
SlangVery informal words used within a group or periodchill (relax)

Notice that one phrase can belong to more than one group. "Give up" is a phrasal verb and also idiomatic. "Raining cats and dogs" is an idiom and may also be a cliché in some writing. The labels are for understanding, not for exams in themselves.

3.How Idioms Behave in Grammar

Idioms are fixed, but not frozen. Most allow the grammar around them to change, while the key words stay the same.

What can changeExample
Tense of the verbShe spilled the beans. He is spilling the beans.
Subject and pronounsI lost my temper. They lost their temper.
Added adverbs (sometimes)It really cost an arm and a leg.
What usually cannot changeWhy
Swapping a key word: "kick the pail"The fixed form carries the meaning
Changing a singular to a plural: "spill the bean"Listeners will not recognise it
Making many idioms passive: "The bucket was kicked by him"The idiomatic meaning is lost

Writers sometimes change an idiom on purpose for humour or style ("the elephant in the room has left the building"), but this works only because the reader knows the original.

5.Where Idioms Come From (and Which Stories to Doubt)

Many idioms come from ordinary life in earlier times. Typical sources include the sea and boats, farming and animals, cooking and food, games and sports (the ball is in your court comes from tennis; touch base and out of left field come from baseball), trades and tools, and old texts such as the Bible and Shakespeare. When the original activity fades from daily life, the expression survives.

But be careful. Colourful "origin stories" for idioms are very often guesses, repeated until they sound true. Language historians call these folk etymologies. For example, the popular claim that raining cats and dogs comes from animals washed off thatched roofs has no firm evidence, and scholars do not agree on where it came from.

Even a famous case needs care. The old explanation for break the ice is that ships had to break ice so other ships could pass. The real history is more interesting, as the diagram shows.

6.Regional Variation

Idioms differ between English-speaking regions, even when the meaning is the same. Dictionaries often label them as British (UK), American (US) or Australian.

MeaningBritishAmerican
a fuss about something smalla storm in a teacupa tempest in a teapot
to be the most extreme example (of bad behaviour)that takes the biscuitthat takes the cake
at the limit of patienceat the end of my tetherat the end of my rope
make contact brieflyget in touchtouch base (from baseball)

Neither region is "right". Choose the idioms familiar to your audience, and use a dictionary that marks UK or US use. See also English as an International Language, which explains why idioms are especially risky with international readers.

7.Using Idioms Well

  • Match the register. Many idioms are informal or conversational. They suit speech, emails to friends and stories, but are often out of place in formal essays, reports and exams. See Formal vs. Informal English.
  • Do not change the wording. "Kick the pail" does not mean the same as "kick the bucket".
  • Do not mix them. A mixed metaphor happens when two images collide: "We'll burn that bridge when we come to it" mixes burn your bridges and cross that bridge when we come to it. Correct it to one or the other.
  • Use idioms sparingly. One well-chosen idiom adds colour. Five in a paragraph feel cluttered and may hide your own thinking.
  • Avoid tired clichés in careful writing. If you have read the phrase a thousand times, readers have too.
  • Consider your audience. People who speak English as an additional language may not know an idiom, so explain it or choose a plain alternative.
  • Be sensitive. Some idioms refer to disability, race, religion or violence in ways that readers may find offensive. If you are unsure about a phrase, choose another.

8.How to Learn Idioms

You will not master hundreds of idioms in one week, and you do not need to. Choose idioms that you meet often, in reading, films, conversation or the news, and learn them properly.

An idiom notebook card
Four boxes make the idiom memorable.

Diagram 3. The third box, a sentence about your own life, is the one that makes the idiom stay.

  1. Notice an idiom in a real sentence.
  2. Work out the meaning from context, then check a good learner's dictionary, which lists idioms under the main word.
  3. Write the card above, with a sentence about your own life.
  4. Say it aloud two or three times, as one unit.
  5. Use it in speaking or informal writing within a few days, then review it a week later. See Fluency Building Techniques.

Aim for three or four new idioms a week, not thirty in one day.

9.Worked Examples

Example A: Decoding an idiom you do not know

You read: "I have sent my offer to the buyer. Now the ball is in his court." You have never seen the idiom.

1Read the context. You sent an offer. Something happens next, and it depends on the buyer.
2Think of the literal picture. A ball landing on someone's side of a court, as in tennis. The player on that side must hit it back.
3Make a guess. It is now the buyer's turn to act.
4Check a dictionary. Confirmed: it is someone's turn to take the next step.
5Test it. Make your own sentence: "I gave my answer; now the ball is in the teacher's court."

Notice that the dictionary check comes last, after you have used the context. This builds your skill for the next unknown idiom.

Example B: Making an idiom-heavy sentence formal

Draft: "At the end of the day, the school needs to bite the bullet and throw money at the library, because it's a no-brainer, and we can't just sit on the fence."

1Underline the idioms. at the end of the day, bite the bullet, throw money at, no-brainer, sit on the fence.
2Write the plain meaning of each. finally; accept a hard decision; spend a lot on; obvious choice; avoid deciding.
3Rewrite in formal English and add the reason.

Formal version: "The school should invest in improving the library, because the benefit to students is clear, and a decision should not be delayed."

Mixed metaphor repair. "We'll burn that bridge when we come to it" becomes "We'll cross that bridge when we come to it" (deal with it when it happens).

Try it yourself. Rewrite in plain, formal English: "It's raining cats and dogs, so the match is off, and the coach is under the weather."

Sample answer: "It is raining heavily, so the match has been cancelled, and the coach is unwell."

11.Common Mistakes

MistakeBetter habit
Translating an idiom word for wordFind the meaning, then match it to an English idiom or plain words
Changing a word ("kick the pail")Keep the fixed form
Mixing two idioms into oneChoose one image
Putting idioms in a formal exam essayUse plain, precise language
Using five idioms in one paragraphUse one only when it helps
Memorising long lists without sentencesLearn a few, with your own example
Saying the idiom without checking meaningCheck a dictionary first
Believing every origin storyLook for dated sources

12.Common Myths

MythThe more idioms you use, the better your English.
FactClear, accurate language matters more. Overusing idioms can make writing cluttered or unclear.
MythEvery idiom has a known, simple origin.
FactMany origins are uncertain, and popular stories are often folk etymologies.
MythIdioms are just slang.
FactIdioms range from informal to quite standard, and many are centuries old. Slang is a different category.
MythOnly native speakers can use idioms properly.
FactLearners can master the ones they need, by learning them in context.

13.Key Terms

Idiom: a fixed expression whose meaning cannot be worked out from its separate words.
Non-compositional: having a meaning that is not built from the meanings of its parts.
Proverb: a traditional saying that offers advice.
Phrasal verb: a verb with a particle that acts as one unit, such as give up.
Collocation: words that naturally go together.
Cliché: an overused expression.
Mixed metaphor: two images combined in one expression by mistake.
Folk etymology: a popular but incorrect story about a word's or phrase's origin.
Register: the level of formality of language.

14.Why This Matters

Idioms are where English stops being purely logical and becomes cultural. Understanding them lets you follow conversations, films, news and stories, and using a few well chosen ones lets you sound natural. The most important skill, though, is judgment: knowing which idiom, if any, suits this reader and this moment. Continue with Formal vs. Informal English.

Key points to remember
  • An idiom is a fixed expression whose meaning is not the sum of its words.
  • Idioms differ from proverbs, phrasal verbs, collocations and clichés.
  • Tense and pronouns can change, but the key words cannot.
  • Learn idioms in context, in themes, with a sentence of your own.
  • Be cautious with origin stories; many are guesses.
  • Match the register, avoid mixing metaphors, and use idioms sparingly.
  • Some idioms differ between UK and US English.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students one idiom each and ask them to draw its literal picture, write its meaning, and write a sentence. Display the pictures and have the class guess the idiom. Then ask each student to rewrite a short idiom-heavy paragraph for a formal reader.

Please note: origins of idioms are often uncertain, so avoid presenting stories as fact unless a dictionary or reliable source supports them. Examples are chosen for broad use but regional differences exist, and students should follow the usage of their own syllabus.
