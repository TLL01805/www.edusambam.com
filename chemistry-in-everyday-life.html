Chemistry in Everyday Life: The Science Around You

From the toast on your plate to the soap at your sink, chemical reactions are happening around you all day. Learn to see them.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 16 min read | Updated 21 October 2026

You do not have to enter a laboratory to see chemistry. It is in the bread that rises, the soap that cleans, the match that lights, the antacid that settles an upset stomach and the iron gate that slowly turns brown. This article connects the chemistry you learn in class with the things you meet every day, and shows how knowing a little chemistry also keeps you safe.

1.Chemistry in the Kitchen

Cooking is chemistry you can eat. Heat, water and acid change food in different ways.

  • Baking powder and baking soda. Baking soda is sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO₃. When it meets an acid (such as vinegar, lemon juice or the acid in baking powder) it releases carbon dioxide gas, which makes bubbles that lift cakes and pancakes.
  • Yeast and fermentation. In bread dough, yeast breaks down sugars to give carbon dioxide (which makes the dough rise) and ethanol (which mostly evaporates during baking).
  • Browning. The golden crust of bread and the brown flavour of roasted meat come mainly from the Maillard reaction, a reaction between amino acids (from proteins) and sugars at high temperature. Pure sugar heated on its own caramelises, which is a different set of reactions. Boiled food does not brown because water cannot get hot enough.
  • Eggs. Heat changes the shape of egg proteins (denaturation), so the clear liquid becomes a white solid. This change cannot be reversed.
  • Preserving food. Cold slows chemical reactions and microbe growth; salt and sugar draw water out of microbes; pickling uses acid; canning uses heat to destroy microbes and then seals out air.
Worked example 1: baking soda and vinegar

Question: Write a balanced equation for baking soda reacting with vinegar (ethanoic acid), and name the gas produced.

  1. Reactants: NaHCO₃ and CH₃COOH.
  2. Products of an acid reacting with a hydrogencarbonate: a salt (sodium ethanoate), water and carbon dioxide.
  3. Write: NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂.
  4. Check: Na 1 = 1; C 1 + 2 = 3 on the left and 2 + 1 = 3 on the right; H 1 + 4 = 5 on the left and 3 + 2 = 5 on the right; O 3 + 2 = 5 on the left and 2 + 1 + 2 = 5 on the right. It is already balanced.

Answer: carbon dioxide is released, which is why the mixture fizzes.

2.Soaps, Detergents and Cleaning

Water alone cannot remove grease, because grease and water do not mix. Soap solves this problem through the structure of its molecules. A soap molecule has a hydrophilic (water-loving) charged head and a hydrophobic (water-hating, oil-loving) hydrocarbon tail.

Figure 1. How soap works. The tails dissolve in the grease and the heads face the water, forming a ball called a micelle. The micelle can then be rinsed away. The ball turns slowly in this animation.

Soap is made by heating fats or oils with a strong alkali such as sodium hydroxide, a reaction called saponification, which gives soap and glycerol. Detergents are similar cleaning agents made from other chemicals, often from petroleum.

In hard water, which contains calcium and magnesium ions, soap forms an insoluble grey scum instead of lathering:

Ca²⁺ + 2C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻ → Ca(C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂ (scum)

Detergents do not form scum so easily in hard water, which is one reason they are widely used for laundry. Hard water can be softened by boiling (for temporary hardness), by adding washing soda, or by ion exchange.

3.Acids, Bases and the pH Scale at Home

The pH scale runs from 0 to 14. Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is alkaline. Many household items sit along it.

Figure 2. The pH scale with approximate positions of household substances. Strongly acidic and strongly alkaline products, such as bleach and ammonia cleaners, can be hazardous.

Your stomach contains hydrochloric acid, which helps digestion. Too much acid can cause indigestion or heartburn. Antacids are mild bases that neutralise some of this acid. For example, magnesium hydroxide reacts like this:

Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O

Toothpaste is mildly alkaline so it can neutralise the acids that bacteria produce from sugar, which would otherwise attack tooth enamel. Soil pH matters for farmers: most crops grow best in soil that is close to neutral, and farmers may add lime (calcium hydroxide or calcium oxide) to neutralise acidic soil.

Safety note

Never mix cleaning products. Bleach mixed with an acid (such as some toilet cleaners or vinegar) releases poisonous chlorine gas, and bleach mixed with ammonia cleaners releases toxic fumes. Use one product at a time, in a ventilated room, keep chemicals in their original labelled containers, and keep them away from children. Do not taste or deliberately sniff chemicals.

4.Medicines and Your Body

Medicines are chemicals that change how the body works. Aspirin and paracetamol relieve pain and fever; antibiotics target bacteria (they do not work against viruses); vaccines train the immune system. Chemists design drugs to fit and act on specific molecules in the body, then test them extensively for safety and effectiveness before they are approved.

Even a helpful medicine is harmful in the wrong dose, so always follow the label or the instructions of a doctor or pharmacist, never share prescription medicine, and keep all medicines locked away from children. This article is general education and not medical advice.

5.Materials: Plastics, Glass, Metals and Alloys

MaterialMade fromWhy it is useful
Plastics (polymers)Chemicals from crude oil, joined into long chainsLight, waterproof, cheap, can be moulded; but many persist in the environment
GlassSand (silicon dioxide), sodium carbonate and limestone, melted togetherTransparent, hard, unreactive to most chemicals
Ceramics and cementClay, limestone and other minerals heated to high temperaturesHard, heat-resistant, strong in compression
SteelIron with a small amount of carbon, an alloyMuch stronger than pure iron
AluminiumExtracted from bauxite by electrolysisLight, resists corrosion by forming a protective oxide layer

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements. The different-sized atoms make it harder for layers of atoms to slide over each other, so alloys are generally harder and stronger than pure metals. Brass (copper and zinc) and stainless steel (iron with chromium and nickel) are alloys.

6.Fuels, Fire and Safety

A fire needs three things, called the fire triangle: fuel, oxygen and heat. Take away any one and the fire stops. Water cools a fire and removes heat; a fire blanket or lid removes oxygen; turning off the gas supply removes fuel.

Figure 3. The fire triangle. The flame pulses to remind us that burning is a continuing chemical reaction needing all three sides.

Fuels such as natural gas, petrol, charcoal and wood release energy when they burn. Complete burning of a hydrocarbon gives carbon dioxide and water; with too little air, poisonous carbon monoxide can form. Fireworks get their colours from metal compounds heated in the flame: sodium gives yellow-orange, copper blue-green, strontium and lithium red, and potassium lilac.

Safety note

Never use water on a cooking-oil fire (it can make burning oil spatter violently) or on an electrical fire. Turn off the heat or power if it is safe, smother an oil fire with a lid or fire blanket, and call the emergency services. Never use charcoal burners, gas heaters or generators in a closed room because of carbon monoxide.

7.Farming and Fertilisers

Plants need nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) for growth. These are the three main nutrients in NPK fertilisers. Nitrogen fertilisers usually come from ammonia, which is made industrially by the Haber process from nitrogen in the air and hydrogen: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. The three numbers on a bag show the nutrient content as percentages by mass (phosphorus and potassium are often expressed in oxide form on labels).

Worked example 2: reading a fertiliser label

Question: A 50 kg bag is labelled 20-10-10. How much nitrogen does it contain?

  1. The first number is the nitrogen percentage: 20%.
  2. Mass of nitrogen = 20 ÷ 100 × 50 kg = 10 kg.

Answer: 10 kg of nitrogen.

Too much fertiliser can wash into rivers and lakes, where it causes algae to grow rapidly and then use up the oxygen in the water, harming fish. This is called eutrophication. Careful use and the right timing reduce this pollution.

8.Clean Water

Water for drinking is treated in several chemical and physical steps: screening and settling remove large particles; coagulation chemicals make fine particles clump together; sand filtration removes them; chlorination (or other disinfection) kills disease-causing microbes. At home, boiling water is a reliable way to make questionable water safer to drink. Seawater can be turned into freshwater by distillation or by reverse osmosis, though both need a great deal of energy.

9.Rust and Corrosion

Iron objects rust when they are exposed to both oxygen and water, forming reddish-brown hydrated iron(III) oxide. Salt speeds up rusting. Painting, oiling, galvanising (coating with zinc) and using sacrificial metals all slow it down. The same reaction costs economies a great deal of money each year in replacing bridges, pipes and vehicles.

10.Chemistry and the Environment

  • Greenhouse effect. Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that traps heat, which is the main cause of present-day global warming. Methane is another important greenhouse gas.
  • Acid rain. Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels dissolve in rain to form acids that damage trees, buildings and lakes.
  • Ozone layer. CFC chemicals once used in refrigerators and aerosols damaged the ozone layer; international agreement (the Montreal Protocol) phased them out and the layer is recovering.
  • Waste. Reducing, reusing and recycling save materials and energy. Recycling aluminium needs far less energy than extracting it from ore.

11.Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Thinking “chemical” means “harmful”. Water, salt and sugar are all chemicals. What matters is the substance and the dose.
  • Mixing cleaners to make them stronger. This can release toxic gas.
  • Believing all acids are dangerous. Citric acid in fruit and ethanoic acid in vinegar are safe in food; the concentration matters.
  • Using water on every fire. Oil and electrical fires need different methods.
  • Confusing soap and detergent. Soap is made from fats and alkali; detergents are made from other chemicals and work better in hard water.

12.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. Why does a cake rise when baking powder is added?

Answer: The baking powder releases carbon dioxide gas bubbles when it reacts with an acid and is heated.

2. Explain how a soap molecule removes grease.

Answer: Its oil-loving tail dissolves in the grease and its water-loving head faces the water, so the grease is surrounded and rinsed away in a micelle.

3. Why is scum formed in hard water?

Answer: Calcium and magnesium ions react with soap to give an insoluble compound.

4. What pH is a neutral solution, and is vinegar above or below it?

Answer: pH 7; vinegar is below 7 (acidic).

5. What type of reaction occurs when an antacid relieves heartburn?

Answer: Neutralisation: a base reacts with stomach acid.

6. Name the three sides of the fire triangle.

Answer: Fuel, oxygen and heat.

7. Why should bleach never be mixed with other cleaners?

Answer: It can give off poisonous gases such as chlorine when mixed with acids or toxic fumes with ammonia.

13.Summary

  • Cooking uses chemical reactions: gas release in baking, fermentation in bread and the Maillard reaction in browning.
  • Soap molecules have a water-loving head and a grease-loving tail, and form micelles.
  • pH measures acidity: below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 alkaline; antacids neutralise stomach acid.
  • Never mix household cleaners; read labels; store chemicals safely.
  • Alloys are stronger than pure metals; polymers and glass come from chemical processes.
  • The fire triangle explains fire safety, and fertilisers supply N, P and K to crops.
  • Chemistry is also behind environmental problems and their solutions.
For teachers and parents

Suggested use: ask students to bring a household product label and identify one ingredient and its job, or to test the pH of safe household liquids using red-cabbage indicator, with teacher supervision. Parents can reinforce the safety messages about mixing cleaners, storing medicines and fire safety. Content is general education, and local safety rules and syllabus requirements should always be followed.
