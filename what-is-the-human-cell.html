What Is the Human Cell? A Simple Guide

A guided tour of the human cell: membrane, nucleus, organelles, protein production, energy, transport, division and specialised cell types.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|Biology|In-depth learning article|Last updated: October 2026

Your body is built from tens of trillions of cells, and every one of them is a tiny, busy, self-regulating unit. This guide takes you inside a human cell: its boundary, its control centre, its factories, its power stations and its transport system. You will also learn how cells make proteins, move materials, copy themselves and specialise for different jobs.

Written for students up to O-Level (grades 8 to 10), and useful for parents and teachers. This is general education, not medical advice.

1.The Human Cell in Context

A cell is the smallest unit of an organism that can carry out the processes of life: it takes in materials, releases energy, makes new substances, responds to signals and can reproduce. The adult human body contains tens of trillions of cells, and they come in hundreds of different types.

Cells are grouped into tissues (similar cells doing one job), tissues form organs (such as the heart), and organs work together in systems (such as the circulatory system). Understanding the cell is therefore the key to understanding every other topic in biology, from digestion to genetics.

Most human cells are between about 10 and 100 micrometres (µm) across. One micrometre is one-thousandth of a millimetre, so you need a microscope to see them. A red blood cell is only about 7 to 8 µm wide, while a mature egg cell is about 0.1 mm, just visible to the naked eye as a speck.

2.Why Are Cells So Small? The Surface-Area Idea

Materials such as oxygen and glucose enter a cell through its surface, but they are used throughout its volume. As a cell gets bigger, its volume grows much faster than its surface area, so the surface can no longer supply the inside quickly enough. Small cells therefore exchange materials efficiently.

Worked example: surface area to volume ratio

Compare two cube-shaped 'cells'. Cell A has sides of 1 unit. Cell B has sides of 2 units.

  1. Surface area of a cube = 6 × side². A: 6 × 1² = 6. B: 6 × 2² = 24.
  2. Volume of a cube = side³. A: 1³ = 1. B: 2³ = 8.
  3. Ratio (surface area ÷ volume): A = 6 ÷ 1 = 6. B = 24 ÷ 8 = 3.

Doubling the side made the ratio half as large. The bigger cell has less surface for each unit of volume, so it would struggle to get enough oxygen. That is one reason cells stay small, and why cells that need a lot of exchange (such as intestine lining cells) have folded surfaces to increase area.

3.The Plasma Membrane: A Selective Boundary

The plasma membrane (also called the cell surface membrane) is a thin double layer of phospholipid molecules with proteins embedded in it. Each phospholipid has a water-loving (hydrophilic) head and two water-hating (hydrophobic) tails. In water the tails face inwards, away from water, and the heads face outwards, forming the bilayer.

The membrane is partially permeable (selectively permeable): small uncharged molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass through easily, while ions and large molecules need help from protein channels or carriers. Other membrane proteins are receptors that detect signals such as hormones, and some help cells recognise and stick to each other. Animal cell membranes also contain cholesterol, which helps keep the membrane flexible but stable.

Worked example: predicting water movement

A red blood cell is placed in a salt solution that is much more concentrated than the liquid inside the cell. What happens?

  1. Compare the two sides: the outside is more concentrated, so it has less free water.
  2. Water moves by osmosis toward the more concentrated side, which is the outside.
  3. The cell loses water, shrinks and its edge becomes crinkled.

Reverse the situation (pure water outside) and water enters, so the cell swells and may burst. This is why medical fluids given into the blood are made to match the body's own salt level (about 0.9% salt solution is the usual match for blood).

4.Cytoplasm and the Cytoskeleton

The cytoplasm is everything inside the membrane except the nucleus. Its watery part, the cytosol, contains dissolved salts, sugars, amino acids and enzymes; many chemical reactions, including the first stage of respiration, happen here. Floating in it are the organelles.

Running through the cytoplasm is the cytoskeleton, a network of protein fibres. It holds the cell's shape, anchors organelles, acts like a set of tracks for moving vesicles, and helps pull chromosomes apart during cell division. It is also what lets some cells, such as white blood cells, crawl.

5.The Nucleus: The Control Centre

The nucleus is surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope, which has small holes (nuclear pores) that let messenger molecules pass. Inside, DNA is wound around proteins to form chromatin, which condenses into visible chromosomes when the cell divides. Human body cells contain 46 chromosomes (23 pairs); egg and sperm cells contain 23.

A gene is a section of DNA that carries instructions for making a particular protein or functional RNA. Almost every cell in your body has the same set of genes, but each cell type switches on only the genes it needs. This is why a muscle cell and a nerve cell look and behave so differently. The dense spot inside the nucleus, the nucleolus, makes parts of ribosomes.

6.Making a Protein: Ribosomes, ER and Golgi

Proteins do most of the work in a cell: enzymes, channels, receptors, hormones and structural fibres are all proteins. The pathway below is one of the most important in biology.

Figure 3. Original EDUSAMBAM diagram of the protein-making and export pathway. The moving red dot is animated and stops if your device is set to reduce motion.
  1. Copying. In the nucleus, the gene is copied into a molecule of messenger RNA (mRNA). This is called transcription.
  2. Reading. The mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore and attaches to a ribosome. The ribosome reads the code and joins amino acids in the right order. This is called translation.
  3. Folding and processing. Proteins that will be exported are made on ribosomes attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum (rough ER), a folded membrane network. They enter the ER, fold and are checked.
  4. Sorting. Vesicles carry the protein to the Golgi apparatus, a stack of flattened sacs that modifies, sorts and packages it.
  5. Delivery. A vesicle carries the finished protein to the plasma membrane and fuses with it, releasing the protein outside the cell. This is exocytosis.

The smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, stores calcium ions in muscle cells and, in liver cells, helps break down some drugs and poisons.

Worked example: insulin

Insulin is a protein hormone made by beta cells in the pancreas. Follow one insulin molecule:

  1. The insulin gene in the nucleus is copied to mRNA.
  2. A ribosome on the rough ER builds the protein chain.
  3. The Golgi apparatus processes and packs it into vesicles.
  4. When blood glucose rises, vesicles fuse with the membrane and release insulin into the blood.

Pancreatic beta cells therefore have a lot of rough ER and Golgi, which is a good clue when you identify a cell under a microscope.

7.Mitochondria: Energy Conversion

Mitochondria (singular: mitochondrion) are the main sites of aerobic respiration, the process that uses oxygen to release energy from glucose:

glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy (transferred to ATP)

The first stage, glycolysis, takes place in the cytosol. Most of the ATP is made in mitochondria. Each mitochondrion has a smooth outer membrane and a folded inner membrane (the folds are called cristae), which increases the area available for ATP production. Mitochondria also contain a small amount of their own DNA and can divide independently.

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the cell's immediate energy currency: when it is broken down, the energy released powers muscle contraction, active transport, building molecules and other work. Cells that use a lot of energy, such as heart muscle cells, contain very large numbers of mitochondria. Mature red blood cells contain none, so they cannot use oxygen for their own energy and rely on glucose breakdown without oxygen.

8.Lysosomes, Peroxisomes and Recycling

Lysosomes are small sacs filled with acidic digestive enzymes. They break down worn-out organelles, food particles and bacteria taken in by white blood cells. Peroxisomes are another type of small sac; they contain enzymes that break down some fats and neutralise hydrogen peroxide, a harmful by-product of some reactions. Breaking things down inside enclosed sacs keeps the digestive enzymes from damaging the rest of the cell.

9.Cell Signalling

Cells constantly communicate. A signalling molecule, such as a hormone or a neurotransmitter, binds to a matching receptor protein, like a key fitting a lock. The binding triggers a chain of changes inside the cell, which can change enzyme activity, open channels or switch genes on or off. Because each step can activate many molecules in the next, a tiny signal can cause a large response.

Cells in contact can also pass small molecules directly through protein tunnels called gap junctions, which is important in heart muscle where cells must contract together.

10.The Cell Cycle and Mitosis

Cells that divide pass through a repeating series of stages called the cell cycle. In interphase (G1, S and G2) the cell grows and copies its DNA; then mitosis shares the copies equally between two new nuclei, and the cytoplasm divides (cytokinesis). The result is two genetically identical daughter cells. Mitosis is used for growth and repair, for example healing a cut.

Figure 4. The cell cycle. The red marker moves around the wheel to show that the cycle repeats.

At checkpoints, control proteins check that the DNA is undamaged and fully copied before allowing the cell to continue. Cancer happens when changes in the controlling genes allow cells to divide when they should not. Not all cells keep dividing: many nerve cells and most muscle cells divide very rarely in adults.

Do not confuse
Mitosis makes identical body cells (46 chromosomes each in humans). Meiosis is a different division that makes egg and sperm cells with 23 chromosomes; it is covered in the reproduction article.

Specialised cells have features (adaptations) that suit their function. To answer exam questions, always link a feature to its job.

Real-world connection

Blood tests count cells because their numbers and shapes carry information about health. For example, red blood cells that are too few carry less oxygen. Doctors, not websites, interpret such results; the point here is that cell structure links directly to body function.

12.Seeing Cells: Magnification Calculations

Cells are studied with microscopes. Magnification tells you how many times bigger an image is than the real object:

magnification = image size ÷ actual size (use the same units for both)

Worked example: real size from a drawing

A student draws a cheek cell. The drawing is 40 mm wide, and the magnification is ×800. What is the real width of the cell in micrometres?

  1. Rearrange: actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
  2. Convert units: 40 mm = 40 000 µm.
  3. Calculate: 40 000 ÷ 800 = 50 µm.

Check: 50 µm is within the usual 10 to 100 µm range for human cells, so the answer is reasonable. Always check that your answer is sensible.

13.From Cells to Tissues, Organs and Systems

Cells are part of a hierarchy: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. For example, cardiac muscle cells form cardiac muscle tissue; this tissue, with connective tissue, blood vessels and nerves, forms the heart; the heart, blood and vessels make up the circulatory system. The whole is able to do things (such as pumping blood around the body) that no single cell can do alone.

15.Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Saying mitochondria 'make' energy. Energy cannot be made; mitochondria transfer energy from glucose into ATP.
  • Thinking the nucleus makes proteins. Ribosomes make proteins; the nucleus holds the instructions.
  • Describing osmosis as water moving 'to where there is more water'. It moves toward the more concentrated solution (less free water).
  • Mixing up plant and animal cells. Human cells have no cell wall, no chloroplasts and no large permanent vacuole.
  • Believing different cells have different DNA. They have the same genes, but use different ones.
  • Writing 'the cell wall' when the question asks about the membrane that controls what enters and leaves.

16.Summary

  • Human cells are tiny so that materials can reach all parts quickly; a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio helps exchange.
  • The plasma membrane is a partially permeable phospholipid bilayer with proteins; substances cross by diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion, active transport and vesicles.
  • The nucleus stores DNA. Ribosomes build proteins; rough ER and Golgi fold, process and package them.
  • Mitochondria transfer energy from glucose to ATP using oxygen. Lysosomes digest and recycle.
  • Cells divide by mitosis to give identical cells for growth and repair, controlled by checkpoints.
  • Specialised cells have adaptations that match their jobs, and they build tissues, organs and systems.

17.Check Your Understanding

1. A cube-shaped cell has sides of 3 units. Calculate its surface area to volume ratio.
Answer: Surface area = 6 × 3² = 54. Volume = 3³ = 27. Ratio = 54 ÷ 27 = 2 (smaller than for the cells in the worked example, so exchange is less efficient).
2. Explain why a red blood cell placed in pure water may burst.
Answer: The water outside is more dilute than the cell contents. Water enters by osmosis. An animal cell has no cell wall to resist, so the cell swells until the membrane breaks.
3. Put these in order for a protein that is secreted: Golgi apparatus, nucleus, rough ER, plasma membrane.
Answer: Nucleus (gene copied to mRNA) → rough ER (protein made and folded) → Golgi apparatus (processed and packaged) → plasma membrane (released by exocytosis).
4. Why do muscle cells have many mitochondria?
Answer: Muscle contraction uses a lot of energy. Mitochondria transfer energy from glucose to ATP by aerobic respiration, so more mitochondria means more ATP can be supplied.
5. A drawing of a cell is 60 mm wide. Magnification is ×1200. What is the actual width?
Answer: Actual = 60 mm ÷ 1200 = 0.05 mm = 50 µm.
6. Two cells in your body have the same DNA but look very different. Explain.
Answer: Each cell type switches on a different set of genes, so it makes different proteins and develops a different structure and function.

18.For Teachers and Parents

Notes for adults
Suggested use. Read sections 1 to 3 first, then the protein pathway, and finish with the quiz. Ask the learner to explain the surface-area-to-volume example in their own words. Hands-on idea. Make a cube model with modelling clay and compare the ratios, or look at cheek cells with a simple microscope and a drop of dilute stain. Scope.
