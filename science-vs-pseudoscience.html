Science vs Pseudoscience: How to Tell the Difference

Astrology and astronomy both study the sky. Only one of them changes its claims when the evidence says it should. Learning to tell the two apart is one of the most useful skills science can teach.

Astrology and astronomy both look at the sky. Homeopathy and pharmacology both talk about treating illness. In each pair, one field follows the scientific method and the other borrows the language of science while avoiding the parts that could prove it wrong. Learning to spot that difference protects you from false claims, wasted money and sometimes real harm. This article gives you the tools to do it fairly.

In this article

What science is · what pseudoscience is · testability · warning signs · thinking traps · fair tests · peer review · the evidence ladder · case studies · three worked examples · key terms · common mistakes · practice questions and a quiz.

1.What Science Is, and What Pseudoscience Is

Science is not just a list of facts. It is a way of finding out about the natural world: asking questions, making testable explanations, collecting evidence, and changing our minds when the evidence demands it. If you have read our article on the Scientific Revolution, you have seen how this habit slowly became normal.

Pseudoscience (the word means "false science") refers to claims, beliefs or practices that are presented as scientific but do not follow the scientific method. They are not genuinely testable, they are not open to being proven wrong, and they are not required to change when evidence contradicts them.

It helps to separate three different things that are often mixed up:

  • Good science that is later corrected. Scientists make honest mistakes, and the method is designed to find them. This is science working normally.
  • Bad science. Careless methods, tiny samples or, in rare cases, dishonesty. It breaks the rules of science, but it can be exposed by the same rules.
  • Pseudoscience. Claims that dress up as science but avoid real testing, so they never get corrected.

The line between them is not always sharp. Philosophers of science still debate exactly how to draw it, and no single test settles every case. What we can do is look at several signs together.

2.The Key Idea: Testability

A scientific claim must be testable: there must be some possible observation that could show it is wrong. The philosopher Karl Popper (20th century) made this famous under the name falsifiability. "Falsifiable" does not mean "false". It means that the claim risks being shown false by evidence. A claim that can never be wrong, whatever happens, tells us nothing about the world.

Worked example 1: Which claim is testable?

Claim A: "On 12 March, the Moon will be in exactly this part of the sky at 9 pm."

Claim B: "Good fortune is coming your way, though it may take a different shape than you expect."

Step 1. Ask: what result would show the claim is wrong?

Step 2. For A, we can look up and see whether the Moon is there. If it is not, A is wrong. A is testable.

Step 3. For B, almost anything that happens can be called "good fortune in a different shape". No result could prove it wrong. B is not testable.

Answer: A is a scientific-style claim; B is the kind of vague statement found in many horoscopes.

This is why astronomy is a science and astrology is not. Astronomers can predict the positions of planets years ahead using physics, and a telescope can check them. Astrology says that planetary positions shape personality and events, but its statements are usually too loose to fail a test. When astrology has been tested in careful ways, it has not held up. In a well-known 1985 experiment published in the journal Nature, practising astrologers tried to match birth charts to personality profiles, and they did no better than chance.

3.Warning Signs of Pseudoscience

No single warning sign proves a claim is pseudoscientific. But when several appear together, it is a strong signal to look closer before accepting it.

Warning SignWhat It Looks Like
Untestable claimsNo experiment could ever show the claim is wrong
Relies on anecdotes"It worked for my cousin" replaces controlled evidence
Ignores contrary evidenceFailed results are dismissed rather than investigated
Cherry-pickingOnly the results that fit are shown; the rest are hidden
Explaining away failure"It only works if you believe in it" or "the test disturbed the energy"
No peer reviewClaims go straight to the public, skipping expert scrutiny
Secret or ancient knowledge"They don't want you to know this" replaces open evidence
Cure-all promisesOne product or method is said to fix a huge range of problems
Jargon without meaningScientific-sounding words ("energy", "quantum", "toxins") used without clear definitions

4.Why Smart People Believe Unproven Claims

Falling for a false claim does not mean someone is foolish. Human minds have habits that make weak evidence feel strong. Knowing them helps you catch yourself.

  • Confirmation bias. We notice and remember events that agree with what we already believe, and forget the misses.
  • Anecdotes. A personal story is vivid, but one story cannot tell us whether a method works for most people.
  • The Barnum (or Forer) effect. People tend to accept vague, general descriptions as personal. In 1948 the psychologist Bertram Forer gave his students the same "personal" description, and they rated it highly accurate for themselves.
  • The placebo effect. A person who expects to feel better sometimes does feel better, even with an inactive treatment. That is why fair tests need a comparison group.
  • Correlation is not causation. Two things happening together does not mean one causes the other.
Worked example 2: Correlation or cause?

Observation: Across many towns, months with higher ice cream sales also have more drowning accidents.

Step 1. Ask whether ice cream could cause drowning. There is no sensible mechanism for that.

Step 2. Look for a third factor that affects both. Hot weather makes people buy more ice cream, and it also makes more people swim.

Step 3. Conclusion: the link is explained by the hidden factor (temperature), not by ice cream.

Lesson: Before accepting "A causes B", look for a third factor, and ask whether a fair test has been done.

5.How Science Tests a Claim Fairly

To test whether a treatment, fertiliser or teaching method really works, scientists use a controlled experiment. The key features are:

  • A treatment group receives the thing being tested, and a control group does not (or receives an inactive version called a placebo).
  • People are put into groups by random assignment, so the groups are alike at the start.
  • In a blind test, participants do not know which group they are in. In a double-blind test, the researchers measuring the results do not know either, so their expectations cannot sway the measurements.
  • A large enough sample is used, because small groups can produce results by luck.
  • The experiment is repeated by others.
Figure 1. A controlled trial. Watch the volunteers being split at random into two groups that are then compared.
Worked example 3: Design a fair test

Claim: "Super Grow spray makes bean plants grow taller."

Step 1: Make groups. Plant 20 identical bean seeds in the same soil. Randomly choose 10 for the spray (treatment) and 10 for plain water (control).

Step 2: Keep everything else equal. Same pot size, same light, same watering schedule. Only the spray differs.

Step 3: Reduce bias. Label the pots with codes so whoever measures the plants does not know which are which.

Step 4: Measure. After 3 weeks, measure the height of every plant and compare the average heights of the two groups.

Step 5: Repeat. If the result is real, other people repeating the test should find a similar difference.

6.Peer Review and Replication: Science's Quality Control

Before most scientific findings are accepted, they pass through peer review, a process where other experts in the same field check the study's methods and evidence before it is published.

1
Study Submitted
A researcher submits findings to a scientific journal
→
2
Expert Review
Other scientists in the field examine the methods and evidence
→
3
Revise or Reject
Weak studies are sent back for changes or turned down
→
4
Published
Approved studies are shared publicly for the wider scientific community
→
5
Replication
Other labs try to reproduce the result independently
A finding only becomes widely trusted once independent labs replicate it. A single study is a starting point, not a final answer.

Peer review is not perfect. Reviewers can miss mistakes, and a few published studies have later been withdrawn (this is called a retraction). Passing peer review does not make a claim true; it means the work has had expert scrutiny. What makes science trustworthy is that the whole system keeps checking: errors are found, reported and corrected in the open.

7.The Evidence Ladder

Not all evidence is equally strong. As a rough guide, the more fairly a claim has been tested, and the more often independent teams have confirmed it, the more we can trust it.

Figure 2. The evidence ladder. Evidence higher up is generally more trustworthy, though quality always matters within each level.

8.Case Studies

Phrenology. In the early 1800s, Franz Joseph Gall and his followers claimed that the bumps on a person's skull revealed their talents and character. It was popular for decades. As brain science developed, researchers found that the skull's shape does not map onto traits in this way, and the idea was abandoned. It shows how a confident, scientific-sounding idea can still be wrong.

Lysenkoism. In the Soviet Union from the 1930s, the agricultural claims of Trofim Lysenko were backed by the government while mainstream genetics was rejected. Many scientists who disagreed lost their jobs or were punished. The episode is widely cited as an example of ideology overriding evidence, with damaging results for farming and for science.

A useful counter-example: continental drift. In 1912 Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents slowly move. Many scientists rejected it at first because he could not explain a mechanism. Decades later, new evidence from the ocean floor led to the theory of plate tectonics, and the basic idea was accepted. This shows that being unusual is not the same as being pseudoscience. Strange ideas become accepted when the evidence arrives, and pseudoscience is marked by avoiding that test.

Real-World Example

Many homeopathic remedies are diluted so many times that, by calculation, not even one molecule of the original substance is likely to remain in the final product. When the evidence from controlled trials has been reviewed, major health reviews have concluded that there is no good-quality evidence that homeopathy works better than a placebo for any health condition. Even so, some products are still sold with scientific-sounding language. This follows the pattern in this article: familiar vocabulary, but an unwillingness to accept what repeated testing shows. This is general education, not medical advice; if you are unwell, speak to a qualified doctor.

9.A Claim-Checking Toolkit

When you meet a claim in an advert, a video or a message, run it through these five questions.

Figure 3. A quick claim checker. Each "No" is a red flag, not proof; several red flags together mean be careful.
Worked example 4: Checking an advert

Advert: "The Brain-Boost Bracelet balances your body's energy and raises exam marks. Thousands of happy customers!"

Step 1: Testable? "Balances your energy" is not defined, so it cannot be measured. Red flag.

Step 2: Specific predictions? "Raises exam marks" could be tested, but no numbers are given. Partly vague.

Step 3: Fair tests? The only evidence is "happy customers", which are anecdotes without a control group. Red flag.

Step 4: Independent confirmation? No studies are named. Red flag.

Step 5: Open to change? No mention of what result would make the sellers admit it does not work. Red flag.

Conclusion: Several red flags together, so treat the claim as unproven until a fair test supports it.

12.Common Mistakes and Myths

  • "If an idea is unusual, it must be pseudoscience." No. Continental drift was unusual once. What matters is whether the idea can be tested and responds to evidence.
  • "Peer-reviewed means true." Not automatically. It means experts have checked the work; later studies can still correct it.
  • "One study proves it." A single study is a starting point. Look for replication.
  • "Science claims to know everything." It does not. Good science states what is uncertain.
  • "Traditional knowledge is always pseudoscience." Not so. Some traditional remedies have been tested and led to real medicines, such as the pain reliever aspirin, which has roots in willow bark, and artemisinin, a malaria drug developed from a plant used in Chinese traditional medicine. The right approach is to test claims, not to accept or dismiss them by their origin.
  • "Pseudoscience is harmless fun." Sometimes it is. But relying on unproven treatments instead of proper medical care, or paying for useless products, can cause real harm.

13.Summary

  • Science is a method: testable claims, fair tests, review and willingness to change.
  • Pseudoscience imitates the look of science but avoids real testing and does not change when evidence changes.
  • Testability (falsifiability) is the central idea; several warning signs together are a strong signal.
  • Our own thinking habits (confirmation bias, anecdotes, the Barnum effect, placebo effects) can mislead us.
  • Fair tests use control groups, random assignment, blinding, enough samples and repetition.
  • Peer review and replication help science correct itself, though neither is perfect.
  • The best question to ask of any claim: what evidence would show this is wrong, and has anyone checked?

14.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. Explain "falsifiable" in your own words.

A claim is falsifiable if there is some possible observation or result that could show it is wrong. It does not mean the claim is false.

2. Why is a control group needed in a trial?

It gives a fair comparison. Without it, we cannot tell whether improvement came from the treatment or from other things such as the placebo effect or natural change.

3. What does "double-blind" mean?

Neither the participants nor the researchers measuring the results know who is in the treatment group and who is in the control group until the end.

4. A shop says "Thousands of happy customers!" Is this strong scientific evidence? Why?

No. Testimonials are anecdotes with no control group, and unhappy customers may not be shown.

5. Shoe sales and sunburn cases both rise in summer. Does buying shoes cause sunburn?

No. A third factor, sunny weather, affects both. This is correlation without causation.

6. Why is "unusual" not the same as "pseudoscientific"?

Some unusual ideas, such as continental drift, were later accepted when evidence arrived. Pseudoscience is marked by avoiding real tests, not by being new or strange.

For Teachers and Parents

This topic works best as an activity. Try the Barnum test: write one general paragraph (for example, "You are usually friendly but sometimes need time alone, and you want others to respect your effort"), give every student the same text as "a personal profile", and ask them to rate how well it fits before revealing it was identical for everyone. A second activity is the bean-plant fair test in Worked Example 3. Discussion prompts: What evidence would change your mind about a belief you hold? Who benefits when a product claim goes unchecked? This article is general education for students up to age 18. It is not medical advice, and it aims to teach how to weigh evidence rather than to mock any group of people.
