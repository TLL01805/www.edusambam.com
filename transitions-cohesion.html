Transitions & Cohesion

Even a well-organised essay can feel disjointed without the connective tissue that links one idea smoothly to the next. Here is how to build it.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 17 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Academic Writing

A collection of good paragraphs is not yet a good essay. What turns separate points into one argument is the connective tissue between them: the small words, repeated terms and bridging sentences that tell the reader how each idea relates to the last.

This guide closes the Academic & Essay Writing series by explaining transitions and cohesion in depth. It covers the difference between cohesion and coherence, the main relationships that transitions show, how to punctuate them correctly (a common source of errors), the other cohesion tools that do not use transition words at all, how to link paragraphs, and how to revise a draft that feels disjointed. It includes two worked examples, a checklist, common mistakes and ten practice questions.

It builds on How to Write a Perfect Paragraph, Essay Structure, Thesis Statements and Evidence, Citations & Plagiarism.

1.What Are Transitions?

Transitions are words, phrases or sentences that show the relationship between two ideas, so that the reader moves from one to the next without a jolt. The Purdue Online Writing Lab describes them as bridges between parts of a paper: cues that help the reader interpret the ideas you develop, carrying a thought from one sentence to another, from one idea to another, or from one paragraph to another.

Think of a reader who silently asks, after every sentence, "How does this connect to what I just read?" If your writing answers that question before it is asked, the reader trusts you. If it does not, the reader has to do your thinking for you, and may give up.

2.Cohesion and Coherence: Two Layers

These two words are close in meaning and different textbooks use them in slightly different ways, so check your own course book. In this guide we use them as many writing teachers do:

Two layers that hold writing together
Strong writing needs both.

Diagram 1. Connecting words cannot rescue an illogical plan, and a good plan can still read badly without connecting words.

TermQuestion it answersTypical tools
CohesionAre the sentences clearly linked on the page?transitions, pronouns, repeated and related words, parallel structure
CoherenceDo the ideas make sense together as a whole?thesis, topic sentences, logical order, relevant evidence, planning

An essay can have a lot of linking words and still be incoherent, for example if the paragraphs are in a confusing order. The reverse is also possible: a well planned essay with no connecting words feels like a list. The fix for incoherence is mostly planning (see Essay Structure); the fix for low cohesion is mostly the techniques in this guide.

3.What Relationship Are You Showing?

The most important rule about transitions is this: choose by meaning, not by decoration. Decide how the new idea relates to the old one, then choose a word for that relationship. Simple words are often better than grand ones.

RelationshipSimpleMore formal
Addalso, and, too, anotherin addition, moreover, furthermore
Contrastbut, yet, stillhowever, nevertheless, on the other hand, in contrast
Concession (admit a point)although, even thoughadmittedly, while it is true that

The wrong word misleads. If you write "however" before a sentence that actually agrees with the last one, the reader expects a contrast that never comes. If you write "therefore" when one idea does not really cause the other, you have made a logical claim you cannot support. Always test: does this word say what I mean?

4.Punctuating Transitions (Where Many Errors Happen)

Transition words are not all the same part of speech, and the punctuation depends on the type. This causes the common error called a comma splice: joining two complete sentences with only a comma.

Same idea, three punctuation patterns
The word type decides the punctuation.

Diagram 2. Never join two full sentences with "however" and only a comma.

  • At the start of a sentence, put a comma after a transition word or phrase: However, the results were different. For example, the second group improved.
  • In the middle of a sentence, a transition that interrupts is set off by commas: The second group, however, improved.
  • Between two sentences, use a full stop or a semicolon, never only a comma, when the word is an adverb such as however or therefore. The SJSU Writing Center summarises the pattern as: independent clause, semicolon, transition word, comma, independent clause.
  • After a semicolon, do not add "and" or "but". A semicolon already joins the clauses.
  • Do not double up. Although it was late, but we kept working is wrong because although and but both try to do the same job.

For more on these rules, see Punctuation Rules and Common Errors in English.

5.Cohesion Without Transition Words

Transition words are only one cohesion tool, and professional writers often use them less than students expect. Much of the smoothness in good writing comes from the following techniques.

Reference: pronouns and "this + noun"

Words such as it, they, these, those and this point back to something already named, which links the sentences. The danger is vague reference. If a sentence starts with "This shows that…" and the reader cannot tell what "this" is, the link breaks. Fix it by adding a noun: This finding shows…, This delay shows….

Repetition and related words

Repeating a key word keeps the topic in view. Using close synonyms or related words (students, learners, pupils) prevents boredom. Do not change the word so often that the reader wonders if you have changed the idea.

Old to new information

A powerful and simple principle, recommended by many writing centres: start a sentence with something the reader already knows, and end it with something new. The new idea at the end becomes the starting point of the next sentence. This creates a chain.

A chain from old to new
Each sentence picks up what the last one ended with.

Diagram 3. The red words show how each sentence links back to the one before it. The example is invented.

Parallel structure

Using the same grammatical pattern for related ideas signals that they belong together: The first plan saves money; the second saves time; the third saves both.

Consistent viewpoint

Keep the same tense, person and level of formality unless there is a reason to change. Sudden shifts jar the reader and break the link between sentences.

6.Linking Paragraphs

The connection between paragraphs is the one most often missing. Several devices help:

  • A look-back at the start. Open the new paragraph with a phrase that points to the previous idea and then introduces the new one.
  • A look-forward at the end. Close a paragraph with a sentence that prepares for what comes next.
  • Topic sentences that echo the thesis. Each topic sentence should show how its paragraph supports your main argument, using a key word from the thesis.
  • A roadmap in the introduction. Tell the reader what the main parts will be, so each transition fulfils a promise.
  • Headings and signposts in reports and longer pieces, such as Causes and Solutions.
WeakStrong
"…is the first reason. Next, cost is also an issue."
Abrupt and mechanical; the second idea simply appears.		"…is the first reason to expand the library. Cost, however, is a separate concern, and it deserves equal attention."
Names the previous point and signals the contrast.
"Now I will talk about homework."
Announces a topic but does not link it.		"If reading builds skill in school, homework tests whether students can keep that skill alone."
Links the two topics by a logical relationship.

Notice that the strong versions are not longer for the sake of it. They carry meaning: they tell the reader how the paragraphs are connected.

7.Transitions in Different Kinds of Writing

WritingMost useful transitions

Storytime and place links (later that night, back at the station)

Speech or presentationspoken signposts ("Let's move to the second point", "To sum up")

In speech, listeners cannot see paragraph breaks, so signposts matter even more. See Fluency Building Techniques for chunks that help.

8.Too Many, Too Few, or the Wrong Kind

  • Overuse. A transition at the start of every sentence feels mechanical. If the link is already obvious, let the sentences speak.
  • Underuse. Facts placed side by side with no links force the reader to guess the relationship.
  • Empty transitions. "Moving on…" and "Another thing is…" announce a change but say nothing about how the ideas relate.
  • Heavy formal words. Moreover and furthermore are fine now and then, but in a piece full of them they sound stiff. Also and and often work better.
  • Repeating the same one. Five "however" in a page become a tic. Vary the structure.

9.How to Revise for Cohesion

  1. Do a reverse outline (see How to Write a Perfect Paragraph). Is the order logical? If not, fix coherence first.
  2. Read only the first and last sentences of each paragraph. Do they connect?
  3. Highlight every transition in one colour. Are they bunched together, or missing for pages?
  4. Test each transition by removing it. If the logic is still clear, the word may be unnecessary. If the sentence becomes confusing, keep it.
  5. Check each "this" and "it". Can the reader tell what it refers to?
  6. Read the draft aloud and mark where you stumble or lose the thread.

For a full editing plan, see Editing Your Own Work.

10.Worked Examples

Example A: From choppy to connected

Draft (an invented school story): "The school introduced a reading hour. Students borrowed more books. Test scores did not change. The library was crowded. The school bought more books."

1Find the relationships. Reading hour led to more borrowing (cause). Test scores did not change (contrast). Crowding and buying books (result of borrowing).
2Reorder if needed. Put the cause and its direct results together, then the contrast.
3Join with old-to-new links and transitions. Use when, as a result and however.
4Check the punctuation and the pronouns.

Revised: "When the school introduced a daily reading hour, students began to borrow far more books, and the library soon became crowded. As a result, the school bought extra copies of popular titles. Test scores, however, did not change in the first term, which suggests that the benefits of reading may take longer to appear."

What changed. Five short sentences became three. Each one starts from an idea already mentioned: the school, the school again, test scores as a new topic marked by however. The last clause explains why the contrast matters instead of leaving it for the reader to guess.

Example B: Repairing five punctuation errors

#ErrorCorrection and reason
1It rained all day, however the match went ahead.It rained all day; however, the match went ahead. (Or: It rained all day, but the match went ahead.) A comma alone cannot join two sentences with however.
2Although it was late, but we kept working.Although it was late, we kept working. (Remove but: it duplicates although.)
3Therefore the plan failed.Therefore, the plan failed. (Comma after an introductory transition.)
4She was tired; and she kept working.She was tired, and she kept working. (Not a semicolon before and.)
5We however did not mind.We, however, did not mind. (Commas around an interrupting transition.)

Try it yourself. Correct this sentence in two different ways: "The bus was late, therefore we missed the start."

Sample answer: (1) "The bus was late; therefore, we missed the start." (2) "The bus was late, so we missed the start." Both are correct. The error was joining two sentences with only a comma and "therefore".

13.Common Myths

MythThe more transition words, the better the writing.
FactToo many sound mechanical. Good cohesion also comes from pronouns, repetition and sentence order.
MythYou must never start a sentence with "And" or "But".
FactCareful writers do it for effect, and usage guides accept it, though it is less common in very formal writing.
MythA transition word can fix a disorganised essay.
FactA transition shows a relationship; it cannot create one that is not there.
MythLong formal words are always better transitions.
FactSimple words such as but, so and also are often clearer.

14.Key Terms

Transition: a word, phrase or sentence that links ideas.
Cohesion: the linking features that tie sentences together on the page.
Coherence: the logical sense and order of a whole piece of writing.
Conjunctive adverb: an adverb such as however or therefore that links independent clauses.
Coordinating conjunction: a word such as and, but, or, so that joins clauses of equal rank.
Subordinating conjunction: a word such as although, because that begins a dependent clause.
Comma splice: two complete sentences joined by only a comma.
Pronoun reference: the noun that a pronoun points back to.
Signpost: a phrase or heading that tells the reader where the writing is going.

15.Why This Matters

Structure, thesis, essay type and evidence can all be strong, and the essay can still feel like a series of separate blocks if the links are weak. Transitions and cohesion are what the reader experiences as flow. They also reveal how clearly you understand your own argument, because to link two ideas honestly you must know how they relate. With this guide, the Academic & Essay Writing series is complete: put its parts together using Essay Structure, Thesis Statements, Types of Essays and Evidence, Citations & Plagiarism.

Key points to remember
  • Transitions show the relationship between ideas; choose them by meaning.
  • Cohesion is the surface linking; coherence is the logic of the whole.
  • Punctuate by word type: but takes a comma; however takes a semicolon or full stop; although needs no joining punctuation.
  • Use pronouns, repetition and the old-to-new pattern for natural flow.
  • Link paragraphs with look-back and look-forward sentences.
  • Avoid overuse, vague "this", and empty announcements.
  • Revise by testing each transition and reading aloud.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students a paragraph with all transitions removed and ask them to supply the best ones, then compare choices and explain why. Follow with a "remove and test" exercise on a finished essay.

Please note: textbooks differ in how they use the terms cohesion and coherence, so students should follow the definitions used in their own course. The examples are invented, and the punctuation advice follows the standard conventions taught in British and American writing guides.
