Chemical Reactions and Equations: The Language of Change

Chemistry describes change, and equations are its shorthand. Learn to read them, balance them and predict how fast and how energetically a reaction happens.

Rust forming on a gate, a candle burning, bread rising and a battery powering a phone are all chemical reactions. Chemists record them in a compact written language: the chemical equation. This article teaches you to read that language, write it correctly, balance it, and understand how fast and how energetically reactions happen.

1.Physical and Chemical Changes

In a physical change, such as melting ice or dissolving sugar, no new substance forms and the change can usually be reversed easily. In a chemical change (a chemical reaction), one or more new substances form with different properties, and the change is usually hard to reverse. During a reaction the reactants (starting materials) are changed into products.

Signs that a chemical reaction may have happened include: a gas is given off (bubbles); a colour change; a solid forms from a solution (a precipitate); a temperature change; light or sound is produced. These signs are clues, not proof: boiling also makes bubbles, and dissolving can change temperature, so check whether a new substance is formed.

2.Conservation of Mass

The law of conservation of mass says that matter is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. The atoms are rearranged: bonds in the reactants are broken and new bonds form in the products. So the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. This was established through careful weighing experiments by the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier in the 1700s.

Figure 1. Hydrogen burning in oxygen to make water. The atoms are the same before and after; they are just regrouped. The molecules float to show that bonds are being rearranged.

If a reaction seems to lose mass, a gas has probably escaped (for example, carbon dioxide from marble chips and acid in an open flask). If it seems to gain mass, a gas from the air has probably joined in (for example, magnesium burning in oxygen to form heavier magnesium oxide).

3.Word Equations and Symbol Equations

A word equation names the substances: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide. A symbol equation uses formulae and must be balanced, with the same number of each type of atom on both sides:

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

The numbers in front of formulae are coefficients; they show how many units of each substance take part. Never change the small numbers inside a formula (subscripts) to balance an equation, because that would change the substance. For example, H₂O is water but H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a different compound.

State symbols show the physical state: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas and (aq) aqueous, meaning dissolved in water. Example:

Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

4.How to Balance an Equation

  1. Write the correct formula for every reactant and product.
  2. Count the atoms of each element on both sides.
  3. Add coefficients in front of formulae to make the counts equal. Start with the element that appears in the fewest places; leave hydrogen and oxygen for last.
  4. Recount every element to check.
  5. Add state symbols if required.
Worked example 1: iron and oxygen

Question: Balance Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃.

  1. Left: 1 Fe, 2 O. Right: 2 Fe, 3 O. Oxygen is awkward (2 on the left and 3 on the right), so find the lowest common multiple: 6.
  2. Put 3 in front of O₂ (6 O) and 2 in front of Fe₂O₃ (6 O): Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃.
  3. Now the right has 4 Fe, so put 4 in front of Fe on the left.
4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃

Check: Fe 4 = 4; O 6 = 6.

Worked example 2: burning ethanol

Question: Balance C₂H₅OH + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.

  1. Carbon: 2 on the left, so 2CO₂ on the right.
  2. Hydrogen: 6 on the left (5 + 1), so 3H₂O on the right.
  3. Oxygen on the right: 2 × 2 + 3 = 7. On the left, ethanol already has 1 oxygen, so O₂ must supply 6: 3O₂.
C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Check: C 2 = 2; H 6 = 6; O 1 + 6 = 7 and 4 + 3 = 7.

Worked example 3: aluminium and acid

Question: Balance Al + HCl → AlCl₃ + H₂.

  1. Chlorine: AlCl₃ has 3 Cl, so 3HCl on the left would give 3, but H₂ needs an even number of H. Use 6HCl and 2AlCl₃.
  2. Aluminium: 2AlCl₃ needs 2Al on the left.
  3. Hydrogen: 6 on the left, so 3H₂ on the right.
2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂

Check: Al 2 = 2; H 6 = 6; Cl 6 = 6.

5.Ionic Equations

When ionic compounds react in solution, some ions do not change; they are called spectator ions. An ionic equation shows only the particles that actually react, so it is a cleaner description of what happens. To write one: split every soluble ionic compound (aq) into ions, cancel the spectator ions, and write what is left.

Worked example 4: precipitation of silver chloride

Question: Write the ionic equation for AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq).

  1. Full ionic form: Ag⁺ + NO₃⁻ + Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s) + Na⁺ + NO₃⁻.
  2. Cancel the spectators, Na⁺ and NO₃⁻, which appear on both sides.
Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

This ionic equation is the same for any soluble silver salt mixed with any soluble chloride.

The ionic equation for any acid–alkali neutralisation is H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l), as you saw in the Acids, Bases and Salts article.

6.Types of Chemical Reaction

TypeWhat happensExample
Combination (synthesis)Two or more substances join to form one product2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
DecompositionOne compound breaks into two or more simpler substancesCaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (heat)
DisplacementA more reactive element replaces a less reactive one in a compoundZn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
CombustionA substance burns in oxygen, giving out energyCH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
NeutralisationAn acid reacts with a base to form a salt and waterNaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O
PrecipitationTwo solutions react to form an insoluble solidAg⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl
RedoxElectrons are transferred: one species is oxidised and one reducedZn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu

Many reactions fit more than one description. For example, zinc displacing copper is both a displacement and a redox reaction.

7.Energy Changes in Reactions

Every reaction involves energy changes, because breaking bonds needs energy and making bonds releases it.

  • An exothermic reaction gives out energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings rises. Examples: burning fuels, neutralisation, rusting, respiration.
  • An endothermic reaction takes in energy from the surroundings, so the temperature falls. Examples: thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate, photosynthesis, and dissolving ammonium nitrate in water, which is used in instant cold packs.
Figure 2. Energy profile diagrams. In an exothermic reaction the products have less energy than the reactants, so energy is released. In an endothermic reaction the products have more energy. The gold line pulses to mark the energy difference.

The energy difference is the enthalpy change of the reaction. The peak on the curve shows the activation energy, the minimum energy that colliding particles need in order to react. It is like the push needed to start a ball rolling over a hill. Even exothermic reactions need that initial energy, which is why a match must be struck to start burning.

Worked example 5: bond energies

Question: In a reaction, bond breaking absorbs 1000 kJ and bond making releases 1350 kJ. Is it exothermic or endothermic, and what is the energy change?

  1. Energy released (1350 kJ) is greater than energy absorbed (1000 kJ).
  2. Net energy change = 1350 − 1000 = 350 kJ released.
  3. More energy is released than taken in, so the reaction is exothermic.

8.Rates of Reaction

The rate of reaction is how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed. According to collision theory, particles must collide with enough energy (at least the activation energy) and the right orientation for a reaction to happen. Anything that increases the number of successful collisions per second speeds the reaction up.

FactorEffect on rateExplanation
Higher temperatureFasterParticles move faster and collide more often, and more of the collisions have enough energy
Higher concentration (or gas pressure)FasterMore particles in the same volume, so collisions are more frequent
Larger surface area (smaller pieces)FasterMore particles at the surface are exposed to collisions
CatalystFasterProvides an alternative route with lower activation energy; not used up
Figure 3. Gas volume against time for the same amount of reactant under different conditions. A steeper curve means a faster reaction. All curves end at the same height because the same amount of product forms.

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. For example, manganese(IV) oxide catalyses the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Enzymes are biological catalysts. Many industrial processes rely on catalysts, which saves energy and money.

Worked example 6: reading the graph

Question: Marble chips react with acid. Powdered marble gives a steeper curve than lumps but the same final volume of gas. Why?

Answer: Powder has a larger surface area, so more collisions happen per second and the reaction is faster. The mass of marble is the same, so the same amount of carbon dioxide forms in the end.

9.Reversible Reactions

Some reactions can go in both directions. They are shown with a double arrow, ⇌. For example, blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate loses water when heated to give white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate, and the white solid turns blue again when water is added: CuSO₄·5H₂O ⇌ CuSO₄ + 5H₂O. The reverse reaction is used as a simple test for water. In a closed system, a reversible reaction can reach dynamic equilibrium, where the forward and reverse reactions continue at the same rate so the amounts stay constant. The Haber process for ammonia, N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, is an important industrial example.

Safety note

Hydrogen mixed with air or oxygen is explosive, and heating chemicals can cause splashes. Wear eye protection in all experiments with reactions, tie back long hair and follow your teacher’s instructions.

10.Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Changing subscripts to balance. Only change coefficients.
  • Forgetting diatomic elements. Hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen and the halogens are H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.
  • Leaving out state symbols or using them wrongly. (aq) means dissolved in water, not just “liquid”.
  • Calling a catalyst a reactant. It is not used up and does not appear in the overall equation.
  • Saying exothermic means “hot”. It means energy is released to the surroundings.
  • Thinking a catalyst changes the amount of product. It only changes the speed.

11.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. Balance: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.

Answer: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

2. Balance: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.

Answer: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.

3. Write the ionic equation for the reaction of barium chloride with sodium sulfate solutions.

Answer: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s).

4. Is the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate exothermic or endothermic?

Answer: Endothermic: it needs a continuous supply of heat.

5. Why does a higher temperature increase the rate of reaction?

Answer: Particles move faster, so collisions are more frequent and a greater fraction have enough energy to react.

6. What does a catalyst do to the activation energy?

Answer: It lowers it by providing an alternative reaction pathway.

7. Magnesium burns in air and the product weighs more than the magnesium. Why?

Answer: The magnesium combined with oxygen from the air, so the product MgO has extra mass.

12.Summary

  • A chemical reaction forms new substances; reactants become products.
  • Mass is conserved because atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed.
  • Balance equations using coefficients only; use state symbols (s), (l), (g), (aq).
  • Ionic equations show only the reacting ions; spectator ions are left out.
  • Reaction types include combination, decomposition, displacement, combustion, neutralisation, precipitation and redox.
  • Exothermic reactions release energy; endothermic reactions absorb energy.
  • Rate depends on temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts, which all change collision frequency or energy.
For teachers and parents

Suggested use: have students weigh a sealed flask before and after a reaction (such as dilute acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate in a closed system) to show conservation of mass, then repeat open to explain the apparent loss. A temperature-probe experiment with dissolving salts shows exothermic and endothermic changes. Parents can reinforce balancing by using modelling clay or coloured beads for atoms. Please follow your own syllabus for required reaction types and notation.
