Study Skills and Exam Technique: A Complete Guide

How you study matters as much as how long you study. Learn how memory works, how to plan and revise actively, and how to use your time and knowledge well in the exam room.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 20 min read | Career & Study Skills

Many students believe that success in exams depends mainly on intelligence or on the number of hours spent studying. Research on learning points to a different answer: how you study matters at least as much as how long you study. Two students can spend the same three hours with the same book, and one can remember far more, simply because of the methods used. The good news is that study skills can be learned by anyone. This guide explains how memory works, how to plan your time, how to take notes and revise effectively, and how to use your time and knowledge well in the exam room.

Key Takeaways

Learn actively, not passively: test yourself, explain ideas in your own words and review at spaced intervals. Plan your time with a realistic timetable and clear goals. Practise with past papers under timed conditions. In the exam, read the instructions, divide your time by the marks, and check your work. Look after your sleep, food and stress, because your brain needs them.

1.How Learning and Memory Work

To study well, it helps to understand what your brain does when you learn. Memory involves three basic steps. Encoding is taking in new information and connecting it to what you already know. Storage is keeping that information over time. Retrieval is bringing it back when you need it, for example in an exam. A weakness at any one step causes forgetting, so good study methods help with all three.

In the 1880s, a German psychologist, Hermann Ebbinghaus, studied how quickly people forget newly learned material. His work showed what many students experience: without review, much of what we learn is forgotten surprisingly fast, and each review makes the memory last longer. This idea is called the forgetting curve, and it is the reason why spreading your study over several days works better than doing everything in one night.

Active learning and passive learning

Passive study means taking in information without working with it, for example rereading or highlighting. Active study means doing something with the information, such as answering questions or explaining it. Research in learning science has consistently found that active methods produce stronger, longer-lasting memory.

Passive (weaker on its own)Active (stronger)
Rereading the textbookClosing the book and writing what you remember
Highlighting every sentenceTurning headings into questions and answering them
Copying notes word for wordSummarising in your own words
Watching a video without pausingPausing to predict and explain what comes next

2.Setting Goals and Planning Your Study

Studying without a plan often leads to delay and worry. A clear goal turns a large, unclear task ("study biology") into a small, achievable one ("learn and test the stages of digestion for 30 minutes"). A helpful method is to set SMART goals. SMART is a memory aid for five qualities of a good goal:

LetterQualityExample
SSpecificComplete exercises 1 to 10 in the chapter on fractions
MMeasurableScore at least 8 out of 10 on a self-test
AAchievableTwo 30-minute sessions on a school day
RRelevantTopics that appear in the coming exam
TTime-boundFinish by Friday evening

Write your goals down. Writing makes them concrete, and ticking them off gives you a feeling of progress that helps you to continue.

3.Managing Your Time

Time management is not about filling every minute. It is about deciding what is most important and giving it focused attention. Follow these steps to build a realistic weekly timetable:

  1. Write down your fixed commitments: classes, work, family duties, meals and sleep.
  2. Find your free blocks: look for periods of at least 30 to 60 minutes.
  3. List your tasks and decide which are urgent (deadline soon) and which are important (they matter most for your goals).
  4. Place the difficult subjects in the times when you feel most alert.
  5. Leave gaps for rest, travel and unexpected events. A plan with no free time usually fails.
  6. Review every week and adjust it honestly.

Work in focused blocks

Many students find it helpful to study in short, focused periods with a break between them. A well-known version, called the Pomodoro technique, uses about 25 minutes of work followed by a break of about 5 minutes, with a longer break after several rounds. The exact times are less important than the principle: concentrate fully for a short period, then rest. Adjust the lengths to suit you.

Multitasking, such as studying while checking messages, reduces the quality of learning, because your attention switches back and forth and each switch costs time and focus. Put your phone in another room, or use a "do not disturb" setting, during study blocks.

4.Taking Effective Notes

Good notes are not a copy of everything that was said. They are a short, organised record that helps you to remember and review. Three common methods are shown below.

MethodHow it worksBest for
Cornell methodDivide the page into a main notes area, a narrow cue column and a summary box at the bottomLectures and textbook chapters
Outline methodUse headings and indented sub-pointsOrganised, structured topics
Mind mapPlace the main idea in the centre and branch out with related ideasSeeing connections between ideas

Whichever method you choose, follow these habits. Listen for the main ideas and write them in your own words. Use abbreviations and symbols that you will understand later. Leave space on the page. Review your notes within 24 hours, fill in gaps while the lesson is still fresh, and write a short summary in your own words.

Example: the Cornell page

Cue column: "What is photosynthesis? What are the inputs and outputs?" Notes area: "Process in which plants use light to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water; oxygen is released." Summary box: "Photosynthesis turns light energy into chemical energy in the form of glucose." Later, you cover the notes area and use the cues to test yourself.

5.Reading Actively with SQ3R

Reading a textbook from beginning to end without a plan can be slow and forgettable. SQ3R is a method that turns reading into active study. The letters stand for five steps:

StepWhat you do
SurveySkim the headings, diagrams and summary to see the shape of the chapter
QuestionTurn each heading into a question that you want to answer
ReadRead one section at a time, looking for the answer to your question
ReciteClose the book and answer the question aloud or in writing
ReviewAt the end, go back over the whole chapter and test yourself again

The "Recite" step is the most valuable. If you cannot answer from memory, you have found exactly what to study again.

6.Memory Techniques That Work

Retrieval practice

Retrieval practice means bringing information out of memory, instead of only putting it in. Examples include flashcards, closed-book summaries, practice questions and explaining a topic to someone without looking at your notes. Each effort to remember strengthens the memory, even when you make mistakes. Check the correct answer afterwards, and learn from the errors.

Spaced repetition

Spaced repetition means reviewing material several times, with gaps that gradually get longer. Instead of studying a topic for three hours on one day, study it for 40 minutes on three or four different days. A simple paper version is the Leitner box: keep flashcards in boxes, review cards in the first box every day, and move a card to the next box (reviewed less often) each time you answer it correctly. When you answer wrongly, the card goes back to the first box.

Interleaving

Interleaving means mixing different topics or types of problems in one session, instead of practising only one kind at a time. For example, mix addition, fractions and percentages in the same practice set. It feels harder, but it helps you to choose the right method, which is exactly what exams demand.

Elaboration and the "explain it simply" test

To check whether you truly understand something, try to explain it in simple words, without notes, as if teaching a younger student. Where you struggle or use vague words, you have found a gap. Go back, fix it and try again. This method is sometimes named after a famous physicist, Richard Feynman.

Chunking and memory aids

The mind holds a small amount of information at one time, so it helps to group items into meaningful chunks. A telephone number, for example, is easier to remember in groups. Memory aids, called mnemonics, can help with lists and order:

TypeHow it worksExample
AcronymMake a word from first lettersHOMES for the five Great Lakes: Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, Superior
AcrosticMake a sentence from first letters"My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming" for the order of the planets
Method of lociPlace items in imagined locations along a familiar routePicture each item in a room of your home
Story or imageLink items in a vivid, unusual storyImagine a giant red apple writing a letter

Use mnemonics for facts that you cannot reason out. For ideas that you can understand, work on understanding first, because understanding is more durable than memorised words.

7.Studying Different Subjects

Subject typeEffective approach
MathematicsStudy worked examples, then solve many problems yourself; keep an error notebook
SciencesDraw diagrams and concept maps; learn key terms with flashcards; practise calculation questions
LanguagesShort, daily practice of vocabulary, listening and speaking; use new words in your own sentences
History and social studiesBuild timelines; link causes and effects; practise writing short, evidence-based answers
Essay subjectsMake essay plans for past questions, and practise writing introductions and conclusions

8.Planning Your Revision

Revision means reviewing and practising what you have already learned, so that you can recall it in the exam. Start early and work backwards from the exam date.

  1. List every topic in the syllabus (the official list of what the exam covers).
  2. Rate each topic with a traffic light: red (weak), amber (some knowledge) and green (confident).
  3. Spend most time on red and amber topics, but return to green topics briefly so that you do not forget them.
  4. Do past papers as early as you can, then mark them carefully and study your mistakes.
  5. Keep a final week for light review, not new topics.
WeekFocus
Week 1List topics, rate them, make flashcards for key facts
Week 2Work through red topics with active methods
Week 3Work through amber topics; do the first timed past paper
Week 4Do more past papers, fix mistakes, light review of all topics

Past papers are especially useful because they show you the style of the questions, the vocabulary and the time pressure. Do some under real exam conditions: a quiet place, a timer and no notes. Afterwards, compare your answers with the mark scheme, and write down each mistake in an error log with the reason (a memory problem, a misunderstanding, a careless slip or a time problem). Patterns in your error log tell you exactly what to practise. You can also test yourself with the quizzes in the EDUSAMBAM Assessment Arena.

9.Looking After Your Brain and Body

Learning happens in a living brain, and it works best when you look after your body. Health services generally give the same basic advice to students:

  • Sleep: sleep helps the brain to store what you studied. Regular, sufficient sleep is usually more helpful than staying up all night before an exam.
  • Food and water: eat regular meals and drink enough water. A hungry or dehydrated body concentrates poorly.
  • Movement: a short walk or some exercise can refresh your attention and reduce stress.
  • Breaks: short breaks during study are part of learning, not a waste of time.
  • Support: talk to a teacher, family member or friend when you feel overwhelmed.

If worry or stress feels too heavy, or lasts for a long time, speak to a trusted adult, a teacher or a doctor. Asking for help is a sign of good judgement.

10.Exam Technique: Before the Exam

Exam technique is the skill of showing what you know within the time and rules of the exam. It can improve your marks even when your knowledge stays the same.

  • Check the date, time, place and what you must bring, such as pens, a calculator or identity documents.
  • Prepare your things the evening before, and plan to arrive early.
  • Do a short, light review the day before, then rest. Last-minute cramming usually increases worry more than it increases marks.
  • Eat a normal meal and sleep as well as you can.

11.Exam Technique: During the Exam

Read first, then plan your time

Start by reading all of the instructions carefully. Note how many questions you must answer, whether there are choices, and how many marks each question carries. Then divide your time by the marks. If an exam has 100 marks and lasts 120 minutes, you have about 1.2 minutes per mark. A question worth 5 marks therefore deserves about 6 minutes, and a question worth 20 marks about 24 minutes. Spending 20 minutes on a 2-mark question is a common and costly mistake.

Understand the command words

A command word is the word in a question that tells you what to do. Many students lose marks by writing a good answer to the wrong command word.

Command wordWhat it asks you to do
State / Name / ListGive a short answer; no explanation needed
DefineGive the exact meaning of a term
DescribeSay what something is like, or what happens, in detail
ExplainGive reasons: say why or how something happens
CompareShow similarities and differences
CalculateWork out an answer using numbers, and show your working
AnalyseBreak something into parts and examine how they relate
Evaluate / DiscussWeigh strengths and weaknesses and reach a reasoned judgement
JustifyGive reasons and evidence to support a decision or answer

Answering different question types

  • Multiple choice: read the question first, then all the options. Remove clearly wrong answers, then choose the best one from those that remain. Change an answer only if you find a clear reason.
  • Short answers: use the marks as a guide. A 3-mark question usually needs three clear points.
  • Calculations: write each step, include units and check that the answer is sensible. Marks are often given for correct method, even when the final answer is wrong.
  • Essays: spend a few minutes on a short plan, then write an introduction, clear paragraphs with evidence, and a conclusion that answers the question.

When you are stuck

Do not stay frozen on one hard question. Mark it, move on, and return at the end. Write something, even if you are unsure, because a partly correct answer can earn marks while a blank earns none. In the final minutes, check names, numbers, units and that you have answered every question.

12.Managing Exam Anxiety

Some nervousness before an exam is normal and can even help you to focus. When it becomes too strong, these steps can help:

  • Prepare well. Good preparation is the strongest protection against anxiety.
  • Breathe slowly. Breathe in gently through your nose for about four counts and out slowly for about six, and repeat several times.
  • Use realistic self-talk. Replace "I will fail" with "I have prepared, and I will do my best on each question".
  • Focus on the next question, not on the whole exam or on the result.
  • Do not compare your preparation with other students' claims.

If anxiety is severe, or if it affects your sleep, eating or daily life, speak to a teacher, a school counsellor or a doctor. Support is available, and you do not have to manage alone.

13.After the Exam

When the exam is over, rest, and avoid long discussions about every answer, because you cannot change them now. When your results or marked papers come back, treat them as information. Read the feedback, update your error log and decide what to improve next time. Every exam teaches you something about your methods.

14.Study Groups and Digital Tools

Studying with others can help when it is organised. A good group has a small number of members, a clear topic for each meeting and a rule that everyone prepares first. Take turns to explain topics and ask each other questions. Teaching a concept to a classmate is itself a strong form of active learning. A group fails when it becomes a social visit, so keep to the plan.

Digital tools can also help. Use flashcard apps with spaced repetition, a calendar or reminder app for deadlines, and a timer for focused blocks. Free educational websites offer explanations and practice questions. Choose a few tools and use them consistently, instead of searching for the perfect app, and remember that the tool is only useful when you use it to study actively.

15.Common Study Mistakes and Their Fixes

MistakeFix
Cramming the night beforeSpread your study over days, with spaced review
Only rereading and highlightingAdd self-testing, flashcards and summaries from memory
Studying only favourite topicsRate your topics and give more time to the weak ones
Studying with a phone nearbyPut it in another room during focused blocks
Avoiding past papersDo them early, under timed conditions, and mark them
Ignoring sleep and mealsProtect regular sleep and eat properly
Spending too long on one exam questionDivide time by marks and move on
Not reading the command wordUnderline it and answer exactly what it asks

16.Your Study Skills Checklist

  • I have SMART goals and a realistic weekly timetable.
  • I study in focused blocks, with my phone out of reach.
  • I take notes in my own words and review them within 24 hours.
  • I test myself from memory, and I space my reviews over several days.
  • I rated every topic and I spend most time on the weak ones.
  • I do timed past papers and keep an error log.
  • I sleep, eat and move enough to keep my brain working well.
  • In the exam I read the instructions, divide time by marks, and underline command words.

A Closing Thought

Good study is not about suffering for long hours. It is about using methods that fit how your memory works: setting clear goals, learning actively, testing yourself, spacing your review, practising under exam conditions and looking after your health. These habits take a few weeks to build, but they reward you for years, in school, in university and at work. Begin with just one change this week, such as closing the book and writing what you remember, and add another change each week.
