Making Inferences & Drawing Conclusions

The best readers don't just absorb what's written — they read between the lines. Here is how to do it reliably, and how to defend your reasoning with evidence.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 20 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Reading Comprehension

A writer can never say everything. Some of the meaning is always left for the reader to work out. Inference is the skill of working it out correctly, using evidence from the text instead of guesswork.

This guide explains what an inference is, how it differs from an assumption, a guess and a prediction, the formula and five-step routine for drawing a sound one, the main types of inference, and how single inferences add up to a conclusion. It also shows how to avoid the common traps, how inference works in news, emails and exams, and how to defend your answer with evidence. It includes two worked examples, a checklist, common myths and ten practice questions. Spelling follows British English.

It builds on Main Idea & Supporting Details and connects with Context Clues, Text Structure & Organization and Author's Purpose, Tone & Point of View.

1.What Is an Inference?

An inference is a logical conclusion drawn from clues in the text combined with your own reasoning. It is not stated directly. Understanding what a text says is called literal understanding. Understanding what it suggests is inferential understanding. Good readers do both at once, often without noticing.

Example

Text: "Maria grabbed her umbrella and glanced anxiously at the darkening sky before leaving the house."

Inference: Maria expects rain. The text never says "it will rain", but the umbrella, the dark sky and her anxious glance all point to it.

We make inferences all day. If a friend walks in dripping wet, you infer it is raining before anyone says a word. Reading is the same skill, applied to words.

2.Inference, Assumption, Guess and Prediction

These four are often mixed up, but only some are reliable reading skills.

TermBased onHow reliable?
InferenceClues in the text plus logical reasoningReliable and defensible
AssumptionYour own beliefs, taken for grantedOften unreliable; can carry stereotypes
GuessNo real evidenceUnreliable
PredictionClues, looking forwardAn inference about what happens next
The difference in action

Text: "The waiting room was silent except for the tapping of Daniel's foot on the tile floor."

Inference (evidence): Daniel is nervous or impatient. Foot-tapping and the silence both suggest tension.
Assumption: Daniel is waiting for a job interview. Nothing in the text says why he is there.
Guess: Daniel is late for a bus. There is no clue at all.

3.The Inference Formula

A sound inference always has two ingredients: clues from the text and what you already know about how the world works. Add them together and you get the inference.

Clues + knowledge = inference
The formula, with the umbrella example.

Diagram 1. Take away either part and the inference falls apart: clues alone are only facts, knowledge alone is only a guess.

Notice that background knowledge differs between readers. A reader who has never seen a gas lamp, a fishing boat or a monsoon may miss clues that another reader sees at once. That is why wide reading, and looking up unfamiliar words and objects, makes you a better inferrer.

4.Why Inference Matters

No text states every implication, and skilled writers trust their readers to connect the dots. Inference is how you understand motive, tone, humour, cause and consequence.

Research on young readers supports its importance. In a study published in 1999, the psychologists Kate Cain and Jane Oakhill compared skilled and less skilled comprehenders. Their results suggested that the ability to make inferences is not just a side effect of reading well. Good inference skills appear to be a plausible cause of good comprehension. In a later study (2001), the link between comprehension skill and inference-making stayed strong even when background knowledge was controlled, so it was not simply that some children knew more. In other words, drawing inferences is a skill worth practising on purpose.

5.Types of Inference

TypeQuestion it answersExample clue and inference
CharacterHow does or did the person feel? Why?"He kept checking his phone" → he is waiting anxiously for news.
Cause and effectWhy did this happen?"The class cheered after the bell" → they were relieved the lesson ended.
Setting and timeWhere? When?"Gas lamps flickered along the cobbled street" → a city before electric lighting.
PredictionWhat will happen next?"The ice creaked under his boots" → it may crack.
Word meaningWhat does this word mean here?See Context Clues.
Writer's attitudeHow does the writer feel?See Author's Purpose, Tone & Point of View.

Researchers also separate coherence inferences, which connect ideas so the text makes sense (for example, working out who "she" is), from elaborative inferences, which add extra colour and detail but are not essential. Exams usually test the first kind.

6.Where the Clues Hide

Think of an iceberg. The writer states only the small tip. The rest of the meaning is below the surface, and your job is to read the clues that point down.

The inference iceberg
What the text says, and what it suggests.

Diagram 2. The first two inferences are well supported. The third is only possible, so it is marked as such.

Where to look for clues:

  • Actions and body language: what characters do, and how.
  • Dialogue: what is said, what is avoided and how it is said.
  • Word choice: "stormed" and "strolled" show different moods.
  • Description and setting: weather, objects and places carry meaning.
  • What is left out: a speech that never mentions the obvious question may tell you something.
  • Punctuation and style: an ellipsis, a dash or a one-word sentence can show hesitation or anger.

7.A Five-Step Routine

  1. Find what is stated. Separate the facts the text gives from anything you might add yourself.
  2. Notice the details. Look at actions, word choice, setting and small details that seem deliberate.
  3. Add what you know. Bring in background knowledge about how people, places or events usually work.
  4. Write the inference as a full sentence. Say it plainly: "Daniel is nervous."
  5. Test it. Could another reading fit the same clues? Does anything in the passage contradict it? If you cannot point to the clue, drop it.

A sentence frame that keeps you honest: "I infer that ___ because the text says ___, and I know that ___." If you cannot complete the second blank with something from the text, you may be assuming.

8.How Strong Is Your Inference?

Inferences come in strengths. The more clues agree, the stronger the inference. A single clue may only make something possible.

The evidence ladder
From weakest to strongest.

Diagram 3. Use "probably", "may" and "suggests" when the evidence is thin.

9.Drawing Conclusions

A conclusion is a broader judgement formed from all the evidence in a passage, often after several smaller inferences. An inference is usually local (one sentence or detail). A conclusion pulls them together.

Two kinds of reasoning lie behind conclusions:

  • Deductive: from a general rule to a certain result. "All the museum's exhibits are labelled in English. This is an exhibit from the museum. So it is labelled in English." If the rule and the facts are true, the conclusion must be true.
  • Inductive: from specific observations to a probable general result. "The last five buses were late. Probably the buses on this route are often late." The conclusion is likely, not certain.

Most inferences in reading are inductive, which is why you should speak of what is likely and always remain open to evidence that changes your mind.

A trap in data and arguments: two things happening together does not mean one causes the other. Ice-cream sales and sunburn both rise in summer, but ice cream does not cause sunburn. Hot weather causes both. Always ask whether a hidden third factor could explain the link.

10.Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat it looks like
AssumptionUsing a stereotype or personal belief as if the text said it.
Over-inferringGoing beyond the clues: from "his stomach growled" to "he had an argument at breakfast".
Under-inferringStaying so literal that obvious meaning is missed.
Ignoring contradictionsUsing one clue and overlooking another that points the other way.
Using outside factsChoosing an answer that is true in real life, but not supported by the passage.
Repeating the textGiving a stated fact when the question asks what is suggested.

11.Inference Beyond Stories

Inference matters in every kind of reading.

  • Emails and letters. "Thank you for your proposal. We have decided to go ahead with another supplier, but we would welcome the chance to work together in future." The reader infers a polite rejection, even though "no" never appears.
  • News and adverts. What is left out may matter. An advert for a "healthy" snack that never mentions its sugar content invites an inference the facts may not support.
  • Charts and data. A line that rises tells you something, but the conclusion you draw must match what was actually measured.
  • Messages. A one-word reply ("Fine.") may suggest annoyance, or it may only mean the person is busy. Text without tone is easy to misread, so keep your inference tentative.
  • Poetry. Poets rely on suggestion more than anyone. See Poetry.

12.Inference Questions in Exams

Look out for question stems such as suggests, implies, most likely, can be inferred, probably, what does the writer want us to feel. A few habits help.

  • Go back to the text. Find the lines that support each answer before you choose.
  • Beware extreme words. Options with always, never, all, only are rarely supported.
  • Reject the true but unsupported. An option can be true in the real world and still not be an inference from this passage.
  • Quote briefly. When asked "How do you know?", give a short quotation or close reference, then explain what it shows.
  • Use hedging words. "Suggests" and "probably" are fine when evidence is limited.
Supporting an inference

Inference: The narrator misses her hometown.
Best evidence: "She kept the faded photograph of Main Street in her wallet, though she told herself she never looked at it." (It shows a longing she tries to hide.)
Weaker evidence: "She now lived in a large city." (True, but it does not point to a feeling.)

13.Worked Examples

Example A: Building inferences, then a conclusion

The passage is invented for practice:

"A handwritten sign filled the front window: 'Closing down. Everything must go.' Mr Patel packed the last jars into a box. For a long moment he ran his thumb over the faded gold letters of his name above the door, and then he switched off the lights."

1Stated: the shop is closing down. Mr Patel is packing the last jars.
2Inference 1: Mr Patel feels sad or attached. Clue: "for a long moment" and the thumb on the letters of his name. Knowledge: people linger over things that matter to them.
3Inference 2: the shop has been there a long time. Clue: "faded gold letters". Knowledge: paint fades with age.
4Over-inference to avoid: "The shop failed because of a rival supermarket." The text never says why it is closing.
5Conclusion: closing the shop is a hard and emotional moment for Mr Patel, who has built something lasting.

Try it yourself. What can you infer? "The street was strangely quiet. Every curtain was drawn, and the usual crowd outside the café had gone."

Sample answer: something unusual has happened, or people are staying indoors, perhaps because of bad weather or an event. The clues (quiet, drawn curtains, missing crowd) are consistent but not specific, so the inference should be tentative.

Example B: Sorting inferences, assumptions and guesses

Passage (invented): "Leila's reply was a single word: 'Fine.' There was no smiley face, and she did not answer any of his messages for the rest of the evening."

1"Leila may be upset or annoyed." Clues: one-word reply, no smiley face, silence afterwards. Several clues agree, so this is a likely inference. Use "may" because a busy evening could also explain it.
2"Leila is angry with her brother." The text mentions no brother and no reason. This is an assumption.
3"Leila is eating dinner." No clue supports it. This is a guess.
4Write the answer: "Leila probably feels annoyed, because her reply was one cold word and she then stayed silent."

Try it yourself. Write one inference and its two supporting clues for: "He checked his watch for the fifth time and tapped the empty seat beside him."

Sample answer: he is waiting impatiently for someone who is late. Clues: checking his watch repeatedly, and the empty seat beside him.

14.Practising Inference Every Day

Inference gets better with regular, light practice. You do not need a textbook.

  • Cover the ending. Stop a story or a news article halfway and write what you think will happen, and which clues told you. Then read on and check.
  • Caption a photo. Look at a photograph and write three things you can infer about the people, the place and the moment. Point to the detail behind each.
  • Headline first. Read a headline and write what you infer the article will say. Then compare it with the first paragraph.
  • What is missing? After reading a short advert, list what it does not tell you, and what you might infer from the gaps.
  • Talk it through. Explain an inference to a friend using the frame "I think ___ because ___." If they offer a different reading, test both against the text.

Over time you will notice that the stronger inferences are the ones with the most clues behind them, and you will start to hedge automatically with words such as "probably" when the evidence is thin.

16.Common Myths

MythAn inference is just a good guess.
FactA guess has no evidence. An inference is built from clues and reasoning.
MythThere is always exactly one correct inference.
FactOften several are possible. The best one is the one most strongly supported by the text.
MythIf it is true in real life, it is a valid answer.
FactIn a comprehension question it must be supported by the passage.
MythInference is only for fiction.
FactIt is just as important in news, emails, adverts, data and instructions.

17.Key Terms

Inference: a conclusion drawn from clues plus reasoning.
Literal meaning: what the text says directly.
Implied meaning: what the text suggests without saying.
Assumption: a belief taken for granted without checking the text.
Prediction: an inference about what will happen next.
Conclusion: a broader judgement built from several inferences.
Deductive reasoning: from a general rule to a certain result.
Inductive reasoning: from observations to a probable general result.
Over-inference: going beyond what the clues support.
Background knowledge: what you already know that helps you read.

18.Why This Matters

Writers rely on readers to read between the lines. Inference is how you understand motive, tone and meaning that sit below the surface of the words, in a novel, a news report or a polite email. It also builds a healthy habit of mind: asking "what is my evidence?" before you decide what something means. Continue with Main Idea & Supporting Details and Author's Purpose, Tone & Point of View to complete your reading toolkit.

Key points to remember
  • An inference is a conclusion from clues in the text plus your reasoning.
  • Formula: clues from the text + what you know = inference.
  • Inferences are not assumptions or guesses; they need evidence you can point to.
  • Clues agree more, inference gets stronger; one clue means "possible".
  • A conclusion pulls several inferences together.
  • Avoid over-inferring, under-inferring and ignoring contradictions.
  • In exams, go back to the text and choose the answer the passage supports.
For teachers and parents

Activity: show a photograph or read a short passage with no explanation. Ask each pupil to write one inference using the frame "I infer ___ because ___, and I know ___." Then collect inferences on the board and sort them as strong, possible, assumption or guess, discussing why. Repeat with a text message or email.

Please note: books label some skills differently ("inference", "implication", "deduction"). Follow the terms your syllabus uses. The passages in this guide are original. The description of the research follows the published abstracts of the studies listed below.
