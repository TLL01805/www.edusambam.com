Making Inferences & Drawing Conclusions

The best readers don't just absorb what's written — they read between the lines. Here is how to do it reliably, and how to defend your reasoning with evidence.

A writer can never state everything outright — some of the meaning is always left for the reader to work out. Inference is the skill of filling that gap correctly, using evidence rather than guesswork.

This guide, the second in our Reading Comprehension series, covers what an inference actually is, how it differs from an assumption or a guess, the specific evidence-based process for drawing one, and how inference connects to the main idea skills covered in Topic I.

1.What Is an Inference?

An inference is a logical conclusion drawn from evidence in the text, combined with reasoning — not information stated directly. Making an inference means understanding what the text implies, not just what it says.

Example

Text: "Maria grabbed her umbrella and glanced anxiously at the darkening sky before leaving the house." Inference: Maria expects it to rain soon. The text never says "it will rain," but the umbrella and the darkening sky make that conclusion the logical one.

2.Inference vs. Assumption vs. Guess

These three are often confused, but only one of them is a reliable reading skill.

TermBased OnReliability
InferenceTextual evidence + logical reasoningReliable, defensible
AssumptionPersonal belief, taken for granted without checking the textOften unreliable, can introduce bias
GuessNo real evidence at allUnreliable
Example: The Difference in Action

Text: "The waiting room was silent except for the tapping of Daniel's foot against the tile floor." Inference (evidence-based): Daniel is nervous or anxious — foot-tapping and silence both point to tension. Assumption (not evidence-based): Daniel is waiting for a job interview — nothing in the text supports this specific detail. Guess: Daniel is late for a meeting — pure speculation with no textual support at all.

3.What Is a Conclusion?

A conclusion is closely related to an inference, but broader in scope: it is a reasoned judgment formed after considering all the evidence in a passage as a whole, often at the end of a reading process, rather than in response to a single detail.

Inference vs. Conclusion

An inference is often small and local — drawn from one sentence or detail. A conclusion is usually the sum of several inferences, formed after reading an entire passage. Reading several paragraphs about declining bee populations, pesticide use, and food supply risk, a reader might draw the broader conclusion that agricultural policy needs to change — built from multiple smaller inferences along the way.

4.Types of Inferences

  • Inferences about character or people: deducing emotion, motive, or personality from actions and dialogue rather than direct description.
  • Inferences about cause and effect: figuring out why something happened, even when the text doesn't explicitly connect the two events.
  • Inferences about setting or time: determining where or when a scene takes place from contextual clues rather than a direct statement.
  • Inferences about outcome: predicting what is likely to happen next, based on the pattern of events so far.
Example: Inferring Setting

"The horse-drawn carriage rattled over the cobblestones as gas lamps flickered to life along the street." Without any date being stated, details like the carriage, cobblestones, and gas lamps allow a reader to infer this scene takes place in a city sometime before the widespread use of electric lighting and automobiles.

5.The Inference Formula

A reliable inference always combines two ingredients, and can be expressed as a simple formula worth memorising:

Textual Evidence + Background Knowledge = Inference

Reading "Ben's stomach growled loudly as he stared at the bakery window," the textual evidence is the growling stomach and the staring; the background knowledge is that a growling stomach signals hunger. Combined, the inference is that Ben is hungry.

6.A Step-by-Step Process for Making Inferences

  1. Identify what is directly stated. Separate the facts explicitly given from anything you might be adding yourself.
  2. Notice the details that stand out. Word choice, actions, and small details are often placed deliberately to signal something beyond the literal statement.
  3. Apply relevant background knowledge. Combine what you already know about how people, places, or situations generally work.
  4. Form the inference as a complete statement. Not just a feeling, but a specific, defensible claim.
  5. Test it against the text. Could another, equally valid inference be drawn from the same evidence? If several conflicting inferences fit equally well, the evidence may be too thin to support a firm conclusion.

7.Supporting an Inference with Evidence

On reading comprehension tests, it is rarely enough to state an inference — you must be able to point to the specific textual evidence that supports it. This is often tested directly with "which detail from the passage best supports this inference?" style questions.

Example

Inference: The narrator regrets leaving her hometown. Best supporting evidence: "She kept the faded photograph of Main Street tucked in her wallet, though she told herself she never looked at it." Weaker evidence: "She now lived in a large city." (True, but doesn't specifically support regret — it only establishes a fact, not an emotion.)

8.Common Mistakes When Making Inferences

  • Confusing inference with assumption: treating personal belief or stereotype as if it were supported by the text.
  • Over-inferring: drawing a conclusion so specific that the text doesn't actually support it — inferring "Ben skipped breakfast because of a family argument" from a passage that only mentions his growling stomach.
  • Under-inferring: staying so literal that obvious implied meaning is missed entirely, and only directly stated facts are accepted.
  • Ignoring contradicting evidence: forming an inference from one detail while ignoring another detail in the same passage that complicates or contradicts it.

9.Inference in Nonfiction and Everyday Reading

Inference is not just a fiction-reading skill — it is essential in news articles, workplace emails, and instructions, where writers frequently imply more than they state directly, often for reasons of tone, tact, or brevity.

Example: Workplace Email

"Thank you for your proposal. We've decided to move forward with another vendor for this project, but we'd welcome the opportunity to work together on future opportunities." A careful reader infers this is a polite rejection, not an invitation to reapply immediately — the phrase "future opportunities" signals closure on the current project, even though rejection is never stated outright.

Real-World Example

Consider this short passage: "The store shelves near the entrance stood mostly empty, save for a few dented cans pushed to the back. A hand-written sign taped to the door read: 'Cash only until further notice.' The manager avoided eye contact with the line of waiting customers." No sentence directly states what is happening, yet a careful reader can draw several linked inferences: the empty shelves and dented cans suggest a supply shortage; "cash only" suggests a payment system failure, possibly linked to a broader crisis; and the manager's avoided eye contact suggests stress or embarrassment about the situation. Combined, these inferences support a broader conclusion: the store — and possibly the wider area — is experiencing some kind of disruption or emergency, even though the word "crisis" never appears in the text.

Why This Matters

Writers rely on readers to make inferences constantly — no text states every implication outright, and skilled writing often trusts its audience to connect the dots. Building on the main idea skills from Topic I, inference and conclusion-drawing is what allows a reader to understand tone, motive, and meaning that live beneath the surface of the words themselves — a skill just as essential for a novel as for a news report or a professional email.
