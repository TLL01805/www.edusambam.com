Fraction Fix-It: Strategies for Confident Fraction Sense

Fractions are not broken, but almost every student’s first answer to a fraction problem is. Spot the mistake, fix it, and understand why the fix works.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|14 min read|Last updated: October 2026|Mathematics

Every fraction mistake follows a pattern. Learn the six patterns behind the mistakes, and fixing fractions stops being guesswork. It becomes a checklist you can run on any answer before you write it down.

Most students get fractions wrong in the same handful of ways: adding denominators together, forgetting to simplify, or comparing fractions by the wrong number. This guide flips the usual approach. Instead of only teaching the right method, it shows you the broken answer first, the one almost every student writes, and then fixes it step by step. When you have seen exactly where a method goes wrong, the correct method is much easier to remember.

Did You Know?

The horizontal fraction bar that we use today is usually credited to Arab mathematicians of the Middle Ages, such as al-Ḥaṣṣār in the 12th century. Before that, fractions were often written out in words. Imagine writing “three-sevenths” every time instead of 3/7!

1.What a Fraction Actually Means

A fraction is a part of a whole, split into equal pieces. The bottom number (the denominator) says how many equal pieces the whole is cut into. The top number (the numerator) says how many of those pieces you have. So 3/4 means: cut the whole into 4 equal pieces, and take 3 of them.

Example 1

A pizza is cut into 8 equal slices and 5 slices are eaten. Then 5/8 of the pizza is gone and 3/8 is left. The two parts add up to the whole: 5/8 + 3/8 = 8/8 = 1.

The word “equal” matters. If a chocolate bar is broken into one big piece and two small ones, you cannot call each piece 1/3. Fractions only work when the pieces are the same size.

2.Fix-It: Adding Fractions

1/4 + 1/4 = 2/8

This is the single most common fraction mistake: adding the numerators and the denominators.

The fix: when the denominators already match, only add the numerators. The denominator stays the same, because the size of each piece has not changed. 1/4 + 1/4 = 2/4, which simplifies to 1/2.

Think of pizza slices: two quarter-slices put together are still quarter-sized slices. There are just two of them now, not eight tiny pieces. The pieces do not shrink because you are adding.

The bottom bar glows to show the answer: two quarters, which is the same as one half.

3.Equivalent Fractions: Same Value, Different Look

Multiplying (or dividing) both the numerator and the denominator by the same number never changes the value of a fraction. It only changes how many pieces the whole is cut into.

Example 2

1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8 = 50/100. Each fraction is the exact same amount, just sliced differently. Multiply top and bottom by 2: 1/2 → 2/4. Multiply by 4: 1/2 → 4/8. Multiply by 50: 1/2 → 50/100.

You can test whether two fractions are equivalent by simplifying both. For example, are 6/10 and 9/15 equivalent? 6/10 simplifies to 3/5 (divide by 2) and 9/15 simplifies to 3/5 (divide by 3). Both give 3/5, so yes.

4.Common Denominators: Making Fractions Comparable

You can only add, subtract or directly compare fractions once they describe pieces of the same size. Finding a common denominator means changing each fraction into an equivalent one that shares a denominator. The neatest choice is the lowest common multiple (LCM) of the denominators, because it keeps the numbers small.

1/3 + 1/6 = 2/9

The fix: convert 1/3 into sixths first: 1/3 = 2/6. Now both fractions share a denominator: 2/6 + 1/6 = 3/6, which simplifies to 1/2.

Example 3: Different Denominators

Work out 2/3 + 3/4.

Step 1: The LCM of 3 and 4 is 12.

Step 2: Convert: 2/3 = 8/12 (multiply top and bottom by 4) and 3/4 = 9/12 (multiply top and bottom by 3).

Step 3: Add the numerators: 8/12 + 9/12 = 17/12.

Step 4: Write as a mixed number: 17/12 = 1 5/12.

Answer: 1 5/12

Once both fractions are in twelfths, the pieces are the same size, so the shaded cells can be counted together.
Example 4: Subtracting

Work out 5/6 − 1/4.

The LCM of 6 and 4 is 12. 5/6 = 10/12 and 1/4 = 3/12. Subtract the numerators: 10/12 − 3/12 = 7/12.

Answer: 7/12 (already in lowest terms, because 7 is prime and does not divide 12).

5.Simplifying Fractions: Finding Lowest Terms

A fraction is in lowest terms when the numerator and denominator share no common factor other than 1. Simplifying means dividing both by their greatest common factor (GCF).

6/8 left as the final answer

The fix: 6 and 8 share a common factor of 2. Divide both: 6 ÷ 2 = 3 and 8 ÷ 2 = 4. The simplified answer is 3/4. Always simplify unless the question says otherwise.

Example 5: Finding the GCF

Simplify 36/60.

List the factors. Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. Factors of 60: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60. The greatest common factor is 12.

Divide both by 12: 36 ÷ 12 = 3 and 60 ÷ 12 = 5.

Answer: 3/5

If you do not spot the GCF, divide by any common factor you can see and repeat. For example, 36/60 → 18/30 → 9/15 → 3/5. You reach the same answer in more steps.

6.Comparing Fractions: Which Is Bigger?

Comparing fractions with different denominators is a trap if you do it by eye. Always convert to a common denominator, or use a benchmark such as 1/2.

“1/3 is bigger than 2/7 because 3 is bigger than 2”: comparing the wrong numbers

The fix: convert to a common denominator (21): 1/3 = 7/21 and 2/7 = 6/21. Now compare the numerators directly: 7/21 > 6/21, so 1/3 is bigger. The conclusion is right, but the first reason was wrong. With a different pair, such as 1/3 and 2/5, the same bad reasoning would give the wrong answer.

Example 6: Comparing and Ordering

(a) Which is bigger, 5/8 or 7/12? The LCM of 8 and 12 is 24. 5/8 = 15/24 and 7/12 = 14/24. So 5/8 is bigger.

Quick check by cross-multiplying: 5 × 12 = 60 and 7 × 8 = 56. Since 60 > 56, the first fraction is bigger. ✓

(b) Put 1/2, 3/5, 2/3 and 7/10 in order from smallest to largest. The LCM of 2, 5, 3 and 10 is 30. The fractions become 15/30, 18/30, 20/30 and 21/30.

Answer: 1/2 < 3/5 < 2/3 < 7/10

7.Mixed Numbers and Improper Fractions

An improper fraction (like 9/4) has a numerator bigger than its denominator, so it is worth more than one whole. A mixed number (like 2¼) shows the same amount as whole numbers plus a leftover fraction. Both describe the identical quantity.

Example 7

9/4 means 9 quarters. Four quarters make one whole, so 9 quarters make 2 wholes with 1 quarter left over: 9/4 = 2¼.

The red dot sits one quarter after 2. Every tick between whole numbers is one quarter.
2¼ = 3/4 (adding 2 + 1 on top and keeping 4 underneath)

The fix: change the whole number into quarters first. 2 wholes = 2 × 4 = 8 quarters. Add the 1 extra quarter: 8 + 1 = 9 quarters. So 2¼ = 9/4. The shortcut is (whole × denominator + numerator) ÷ denominator.

Example 8: Converting Both Ways

Mixed to improper: 3 2/5 = (3 × 5 + 2)/5 = 17/5.

Improper to mixed: 23/6. Divide 23 by 6: 6 goes into 23 three times (18), with remainder 5. So 23/6 = 3 5/6.

Example 9: Adding Mixed Numbers

Work out 2 1/3 + 1 3/4.

Method 1: add the wholes (2 + 1 = 3), then the fractions: 1/3 + 3/4 = 4/12 + 9/12 = 13/12 = 1 1/12. Total: 3 + 1 1/12 = 4 1/12.

Method 2: convert to improper fractions: 7/3 + 7/4 = 28/12 + 21/12 = 49/12 = 4 1/12. ✓ Both methods agree.

8.Multiplying and Dividing Fractions

Multiplying and dividing do not need a common denominator. This is where many students waste time, or make mistakes by copying the rule for addition.

  • To multiply: multiply the numerators, multiply the denominators. Simplify (cancelling first makes the numbers smaller).
  • To divide: keep the first fraction, flip the second fraction upside down (its reciprocal), then multiply.
1/2 ÷ 1/4 = 1/8 (multiplying straight across instead of flipping)

The fix: to divide, multiply by the reciprocal of the second fraction. 1/2 ÷ 1/4 = 1/2 × 4/1 = 4/2 = 2. It makes sense: how many quarters fit into one half? Two of them. The answer 1/8 is smaller than 1/2, which cannot be right when you are dividing by a number smaller than 1.

Example 10: Multiplying

Work out 2/3 × 3/8. Multiply across: (2 × 3)/(3 × 8) = 6/24 = 1/4. Or cancel first: the 3 on top cancels with the 3 on the bottom, and 2/8 simplifies to 1/4, so the answer is 1/4.

“Of” means multiply: 3/4 of 20 = 3/4 × 20 = 60/4 = 15.

Example 11: Dividing

Work out 3/4 ÷ 3/8. Flip the second fraction and multiply: 3/4 × 8/3 = 24/12 = 2.

Check: 3/8 fits into 3/4 exactly twice, because 3/4 = 6/8 and 6/8 is two lots of 3/8. ✓

9.Fractions in Real Life

Real-World Example: Doubling a Recipe

A recipe needs 3/4 cup of flour, but you are doubling it for a bigger batch. 3/4 + 3/4 = 6/4, which simplifies to 1½ cups. It is the same fixing process used above, now measuring real flour into a real bowl.

Example 12: A Monthly Budget

A family earns Rs 12,000 in a month (a small number, to keep the arithmetic easy). They spend 1/4 on rent and 1/3 on food. What fraction is left, and how much money is that?

Spent: 1/4 + 1/3 = 3/12 + 4/12 = 7/12.

Left: 1 − 7/12 = 12/12 − 7/12 = 5/12.

In rupees: 5/12 × 12,000 = Rs 5,000.

Check: rent = 1/4 × 12,000 = Rs 3,000 and food = 1/3 × 12,000 = Rs 4,000. Left = 12,000 − 3,000 − 4,000 = Rs 5,000. ✓

Example 13: Cutting Ribbon

A tailor has a 5-metre roll of ribbon and cuts pieces that are each 5/8 of a metre long. How many pieces can be cut?

Number of pieces = 5 ÷ 5/8 = 5 × 8/5 = 8.

Answer: 8 pieces. Check: 8 × 5/8 = 5 metres. ✓

Did You Know?

Ancient Egyptians mostly used “unit fractions”, which are fractions with a numerator of 1, like 1/3 or 1/7. To write 2/5, they had to add two different unit fractions (1/3 + 1/15) instead.

10.The Six Fraction Patterns to Watch For

  1. Adding the denominators. 1/4 + 1/4 is not 2/8. Keep the denominator.
  2. Skipping the common denominator. 1/3 + 1/6 is not 2/9. Convert first.
  3. Not simplifying. Always check whether the top and bottom share a factor.
  4. Comparing by the wrong number. A bigger denominator means smaller pieces. Convert to a common denominator to compare.
  5. Mixed-number slips. 2¼ is 9/4, not 3/4. Turn the whole number into pieces first.
  6. Using the wrong rule for × and ÷. Multiplying needs no common denominator, and dividing means flip the second fraction and multiply.

Run through this list each time you finish a fraction question. One quick check, “does my answer make sense?”, catches most mistakes. For example, if you add two positive fractions, the answer must be bigger than each of them. If you divide by a fraction less than 1, the answer must get bigger.

11.Try It Yourself

  1. Work out 3/5 + 1/10 and give the answer in lowest terms.
  2. Simplify 24/36.
  3. Which is bigger, 4/9 or 3/7?
Show answers

1. 3/5 = 6/10, so 6/10 + 1/10 = 7/10. It is already in lowest terms.

2. The GCF of 24 and 36 is 12. 24 ÷ 12 = 2 and 36 ÷ 12 = 3. The answer is 2/3.

3. The common denominator is 63. 4/9 = 28/63 and 3/7 = 27/63. So 4/9 is bigger.

12.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] Work out 2/5 + 3/10. Give your answer in its simplest form.
  2. [3 marks] Work out 5/6 − 3/8.
  3. [3 marks] Work out 2½ ÷ 5/6.
  4. [4 marks] A water tank is 3/4 full. Then 2/3 of the water in the tank is used. What fraction of the whole tank has been used, and what fraction of the tank is still full?
Show answers

1. 2/5 = 4/10, so 4/10 + 3/10 = 7/10.

2. The LCM of 6 and 8 is 24. 5/6 = 20/24 and 3/8 = 9/24. So 20/24 − 9/24 = 11/24.

3. 2½ = 5/2. Then 5/2 ÷ 5/6 = 5/2 × 6/5 = 30/10 = 3.

4. Used = 2/3 × 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2 of the whole tank. Still full = 3/4 − 1/2 = 3/4 − 2/4 = 1/4 of the tank.

13.Key Points

  • A fraction describes equal pieces: the denominator says how many pieces the whole has, and the numerator says how many you have.
  • To add or subtract, the pieces must be the same size. Find a common denominator, then combine only the numerators.
  • Equivalent fractions have the same value. Multiply or divide top and bottom by the same number.
  • Always simplify by dividing top and bottom by their greatest common factor.
  • To compare, convert to a common denominator and compare the numerators.
  • To multiply, multiply across. To divide, flip the second fraction and multiply.

14.For Teachers and Parents

Students often treat fractions as two separate whole numbers, which is why they add the tops and the bottoms. Concrete models help: paper strips, a chocolate bar, or a drawn bar split into equal parts. Ask the learner to draw the problem before using the rule. Use the “broken answer” approach in this guide: show a wrong answer and ask, “what went wrong, and how do we know?” Praise estimation (“will the answer be more or less than one half?”) as much as correct calculation. At home, cooking, sharing a pizza and measuring lengths are easy chances to use fractions.
