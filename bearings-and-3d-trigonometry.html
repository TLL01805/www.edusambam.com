Bearings & 3D Trigonometry

Describe direction with three-figure bearings and back bearings, solve navigation problems, and find lengths and angles inside cuboids and pyramids by spotting the hidden right-angled triangle.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|20 min read|Last updated: November 2026|Mathematics

A hiker following a compass, a ship leaving harbour, an aeroplane on its way to another city: all of them describe direction with a bearing. A second application of trigonometry takes you into three dimensions: how long is the longest rod that fits in a box, and how steeply does a sloping edge of a pyramid rise? Both topics use the same skills you already know, which are right-angled triangles, Pythagoras and the sine and cosine rules. The new skill is drawing the right triangle hidden inside the situation. This guide teaches you how to do that, step by step.

1.What Is a Bearing?

A bearing describes a direction as an angle. It follows three rules, and breaking any one of them gives a wrong answer:

  1. It is measured from North.
  2. It is measured clockwise.
  3. It is written with three figures, adding zeros at the front if needed (045°, not 45°; 005°, not 5°).
Figure 1. The arrow turns clockwise from North to 060°. The four main directions are 000°, 090°, 180° and 270°.

Because it is measured clockwise through a full turn, a bearing is always between 000° and 360°. (360° is the same direction as 000°, and we write 000°.) The three-figure rule means that every bearing looks the same length. That makes mistakes harder to hide when a pilot or a ship’s navigator reads one out.

2.Compass Points and Bearings

The eight main compass points turn into bearings by counting 45° at a time:

DirectionBearingDirectionBearing
North (N)000°South (S)180°
North-east (NE)045°South-west (SW)225°
East (E)090°West (W)270°
South-east (SE)135°North-west (NW)315°

Sometimes a direction is given in the older style “N 30° E”, meaning “start facing north and turn 30° towards east”. Convert it by thinking where you end up:

Example 1 · Converting to a three-figure bearing

(a) N 30° E: 30° clockwise from north, so 030°.

(b) S 40° E: start at south (180°) and turn 40° back towards east (which is less than 180°): 180° − 40° = 140°.

(c) S 40° W: start at south (180°) and turn 40° on towards west (more than 180°): 180° + 40° = 220°.

(d) N 25° W: this is 25° anticlockwise from north, which is 360° − 25° = 335°.

“The bearing of north-west is 45°”

45° is the angle between north and north-west, but a bearing is always measured clockwise from north all the way round.

The fix: to reach north-west you turn clockwise through east (090°), south (180°) and west (270°), and then another 45°. That is 270° + 45° = 315°. Picture the clock face: you always sweep clockwise, never back.

3.“Bearing of B from A” and Back Bearings

The words “bearing of B from A” tell you where to put the North line: at A, the point you are starting from. Then measure clockwise from that North line to the line AB. To go the other way (“bearing of A from B”) you put a North line at B instead.

Figure 2. The two North lines are parallel. The bearing of A from B is 180° more than the bearing of B from A.

The bearing of the return journey is called the back bearing. Why is it 180° different? The two North lines are parallel, so the angles on the same side of the line AB add up to 180° (co-interior angles). Going from the bearing of 060° to the back bearing means turning through a half-turn, so:

If the bearing is…Back bearing is…
less than 180°bearing + 180°
180° or morebearing − 180°

The rule keeps the answer between 000° and 360°. If you add 180° and get more than 360°, subtract 360° as well.

Example 2 · Back bearings

(a) Bearing of B from A is 075°. It is less than 180°, so the back bearing is 075° + 180° = 255°.

(b) Bearing of D from C is 210°. It is 180° or more, so the back bearing is 210° − 180° = 030°.

(c) 340° → 340° − 180° = 160°. (d) 000° → 180°. (e) 250° → 250° − 180° = 070°.

“The back bearing of 250° is 250° + 180° = 430°”

A bearing can never be more than 360°.

The fix: 250° is already 180° or more, so you subtract 180°: 250° − 180° = 070°. If you ever do reach a number above 360°, take away 360° to bring it back: 430° − 360° = 070°. Both routes give the same answer, which is a good check.

4.How Far East and How Far North?

A journey on a bearing is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle whose other two sides point east and north. With the bearing b measured from North:

east = distance × sin b     north = distance × cos b

This works for every bearing. A negative answer simply means west (for east) or south (for north).

Example 3 · Components

(a) A ship sails 40 km on a bearing of 060°. East = 40 × sin 60° = 34.6 km. North = 40 × cos 60° = 20.0 km.

(b) A boat is 8 km from a port on a bearing of 125°. East = 8 × sin 125° = 8 × 0.8192 = 6.55 km. North = 8 × cos 125° = 8 × (−0.5736) = −4.59, so it is 4.59 km south. (You can see this from the diagram too: 125° is 55° past east, so the boat is south-east.)

(c) A plane flies 12 km on 070°: east = 12 sin 70° = 11.28 km and north = 12 cos 70° = 4.10 km.

5.Solving Bearing Problems

Bearing problems are triangle problems in disguise. Follow the same steps every time:

  1. Draw a clear diagram. Put a North line at every point where a direction changes.
  2. Mark the given bearings and distances.
  3. Find the angles inside the triangle using parallel North lines (co-interior or alternate angles) or back bearings.
  4. Choose the tool: Pythagoras or SOH-CAH-TOA if there is a right angle, the sine rule or cosine rule if there is not.
  5. Answer in the form asked: a distance, an angle or a three-figure bearing.
Example 4 · A right angle at the turn

A ship sails from port P on a bearing of 060° for 40 km to Q. It then sails on a bearing of 150° for 25 km to R. Find (a) the distance PR and (b) the bearing of R from P.

Angle at Q. The back bearing of Q from P is 060° + 180° = 240°. The new course is 150°. The angle PQR is the difference: 240° − 150° = 90°.

(a) With a right angle at Q, Pythagoras gives PR² = 40² + 25² = 1600 + 625 = 2225, so PR = 47.2 km.

(b) In the right-angled triangle, tan(angle QPR) = 25 ÷ 40 = 0.625, so angle QPR = 32.0°. R is clockwise from Q as seen from P, so the bearing of R from P is 060° + 32.0° = 092.0°.

Check with components: east = 40 sin 60° + 25 sin 150° = 34.64 + 12.5 = 47.14 km and north = 40 cos 60° + 25 cos 150° = 20 − 21.65 = −1.65 km (a little south). R is just south of due east, so 092° makes sense.

Example 5 · No right angle: use the cosine and sine rules

A walker goes 5 km on a bearing of 040° from A to B, then 8 km on a bearing of 120° to C. Find the distance AC and the bearing of C from A.

Angle at B. The back bearing of B from A is 040° + 180° = 220°. The new course is 120°. So angle ABC = 220° − 120° = 100°.

Distance. By the cosine rule: AC² = 5² + 8² − 2 × 5 × 8 × cos 100° = 25 + 64 − 80 × (−0.1736) = 89 + 13.89 = 102.89. So AC = 10.1 km (10.14 km before rounding).

Bearing. By the sine rule: sin A ÷ 8 = sin 100° ÷ 10.14, so sin A = 8 × 0.9848 ÷ 10.14 = 0.7768 and angle BAC = 51.0°. This angle is acute because angle B (100°) is the largest angle. The bearing of C from A is 040° + 51.0° = 091.0°.

“The angle at Q is just 150° − 60° = 90°, so I subtract the two bearings”

This happened to give 90°, but only by luck. The correct angle uses the back bearing.

The fix: the angle at Q is between the line back to P and the line on to R. The line back to P has bearing 240° (the back bearing), not 060°. So the angle is 240° − 150° = 90°. In Example 5, subtracting the bearings directly would give 120° − 040° = 80°, which is wrong. The correct angle is 220° − 120° = 100°. Always use the back bearing of the first leg.

6.Trigonometry in 3D: Find the Hidden Triangle

A solid shape cannot be drawn flat, so a 3D problem looks harder than it is. The key idea is that every 3D problem is a collection of ordinary right-angled triangles. Your job is to find the right one and redraw it on its own, as a flat triangle with its sides and angles labelled. The method:

  1. Identify the line or angle you are asked about.
  2. Find a right-angled triangle that contains it. Often it is a vertical triangle (a vertical edge or the height) standing on a horizontal line in the base.
  3. Redraw that triangle flat, with the known lengths marked.
  4. Use Pythagoras or SOH-CAH-TOA. If you need a side you do not have, find another hidden triangle first.

7.The Space Diagonal of a Cuboid

The space diagonal of a cuboid joins one corner to the opposite corner through the inside of the solid. It is the longest straight line that fits in a cuboid. Find it with two applications of Pythagoras: first the diagonal of the base, then a vertical triangle that uses that diagonal and the height.

Figure 3. The gold line is the base diagonal. The red space diagonal stands on it with the vertical edge of height 5 cm. Each line draws in turn.
Example 6 · Space diagonal

A cuboid has length 8 cm, width 6 cm and height 5 cm.

Step 1: base diagonal = √(8² + 6²) = √100 = 10 cm.

Step 2: space diagonal = √(10² + 5²) = √125 = 11.2 cm.

Putting the two steps together gives a shortcut: space diagonal = √(l² + w² + h²). Check: √(64 + 36 + 25) = √125 ✓.

Example 7 · Exact answer

A cuboid is 9 cm by 12 cm by 8 cm. Space diagonal = √(81 + 144 + 64) = √289 = 17 cm exactly. The base diagonal is √(81 + 144) = 15 cm, so this is a “15, 8, 17” right-angled triangle.

“Space diagonal = 8 + 6 + 5 = 19 cm”

This adds the three edges as if they were laid end to end in a straight line.

The fix: the diagonal is a straight shortcut across the inside of the box, so it must be shorter than walking along three edges. Use Pythagoras twice: √(8² + 6² + 5²) = 11.2 cm, which is less than 19 cm, as it should be. A quick check: the space diagonal must be longer than the longest edge (8 cm) but shorter than the sum of the edges (19 cm).

8.The Angle Between a Line and a Plane

The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its own shadow on the plane (its projection, as if a light shone straight down on it). The shadow is found by dropping a perpendicular from the top end of the line to the plane. The line, its shadow and the perpendicular make a right-angled triangle.

Example 8 · Space diagonal and the base

For the cuboid in Example 6, find the angle between the space diagonal and the base.

The shadow of the space diagonal on the base is the base diagonal (10 cm). The height is 5 cm. The right-angled triangle has opposite = 5 cm and adjacent = 10 cm: tan θ = 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5, so θ = tan⁻¹(0.5) = 26.6°.

The angle between the space diagonal and a vertical edge is the other angle in the same triangle: 90° − 26.6° = 63.4°. Check: cos(angle) = 5 ÷ √125 = 0.4472, and cos⁻¹(0.4472) = 63.4° ✓.

Example 9 · Exact answer, another cuboid

For the 9 by 12 by 8 cuboid, the base diagonal is 15 cm. The angle between the space diagonal and the base satisfies tan θ = 8 ÷ 15 = 0.5333, so θ = 28.1°.

9.Pyramids

A pyramid with a square base has its apex directly above the centre of the base. The foot of the height is the point where the two diagonals cross, so the distance from the centre to a corner is half the base diagonal.

Figure 4. Triangle VOA is right-angled at O. The gold side is half the base diagonal, the dashed side is the height, and the red side is a sloping edge.
Example 10 · A sloping edge

A square-based pyramid has base side 8 cm and height 10 cm. Find (a) the length of a sloping edge and (b) the angle it makes with the base.

Half the base diagonal: the base diagonal is √(8² + 8²) = √128 = 11.31 cm, so half is 5.66 cm (that is √32).

(a) Sloping edge = √(10² + 5.66²) = √132 = 11.5 cm.

(b) tan θ = 10 ÷ 5.66 = 1.768, so θ = 60.5°.

Example 11 · A sloping face

For the same pyramid, find the angle between a triangular face and the base. This time use the middle of a base edge. The distance from the centre to the middle of an edge is half the side: 4 cm. The height is 10 cm, so tan φ = 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 and φ = 68.2°. The slant height of the face is √(10² + 4²) = √116 = 10.8 cm.

10.Bearings and 3D in Real Life

  • Navigation. Pilots and ship’s officers use bearings (and the idea of back bearings) every day. Hikers use a compass bearing to walk straight to a landmark.
  • Search and rescue. Two lookouts can each report the bearing of a boat. Drawing both bearings from their positions finds where the boat is, by the sine rule.
  • Packing. The longest rod that fits in a box 40 cm by 30 cm by 50 cm is √(40² + 30² + 50²) = √5000 = 70.7 cm. A 1.2 m pole will not fit in a suitcase 0.8 m by 0.5 m by 0.4 m, because the longest straight line inside is √(0.64 + 0.25 + 0.16) = √1.05 = 1.025 m, which is less than 1.2 m.
  • Building and design. Roof angles, support beams and tent poles are lengths and angles in 3D shapes. A pyramid-shaped roof needs the length of its sloping edge and the angle it makes with the walls.

11.Common Mistakes

  • Measuring a bearing anticlockwise or from the wrong direction. Always clockwise, always from North.
  • Writing 60° instead of 060°. Always three figures.
  • Putting the North line at the wrong point. “Bearing of B from A” puts it at A.
  • Back bearing errors. Add 180° if the bearing is under 180°, subtract if it is 180° or more. Never give an answer above 360°.
  • Using the first bearing, not its back bearing, when finding the angle at a turning point.
  • Not drawing a diagram. Every bearing problem needs a clear sketch with North lines.
  • In 3D, adding edges instead of using Pythagoras, or using a wrong triangle: the perpendicular height must be at right angles to the base.
  • Mixing up the base diagonal and half the diagonal in a pyramid.

12.Try It Yourself

  1. Find the back bearing of 118°.
  2. Write N 20° W as a three-figure bearing.
  3. Find the space diagonal of a cuboid measuring 2 cm by 3 cm by 6 cm.
Show answers

1. 118° is less than 180°, so add 180°: 298°.

2. 20° anticlockwise from north: 360° − 20° = 340°.

3. √(2² + 3² + 6²) = √(4 + 9 + 36) = √49 = 7 cm.

13.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] The bearing of Q from P is 130°. Find the bearing of P from Q.
  2. [3 marks] A ship sails 12 km on a bearing of 070°. How far east and how far north of its starting point is it?
  3. [5 marks] A ship sails from a harbour H on a bearing of 050° for 15 km to X. It then sails on a bearing of 140° for 20 km to Y. Find (a) angle HXY, (b) the distance HY, (c) the bearing of Y from H.
  4. [3 marks] A cuboid measures 9 cm by 12 cm by 8 cm. Find the length of its space diagonal.
  5. [3 marks] For the same cuboid, find the angle between the space diagonal and the base.
  6. [4 marks] A pyramid has a square base of side 10 cm. Its apex is 12 cm vertically above the centre of the base. Find (a) the length of a sloping edge and (b) the angle between a sloping edge and the base.
  7. [5 marks] Ship A sails 6 km on a bearing of 030° from a port. Ship B sails 9 km on a bearing of 100° from the same port. How far apart are the two ships?
Show answers

1. 130° is less than 180°, so the back bearing is 130° + 180° = 310°.

2. East = 12 sin 70° = 11.28 km. North = 12 cos 70° = 4.10 km.

3. (a) The back bearing from X to H is 050° + 180° = 230°. The angle HXY = 230° − 140° = 90°. (b) HY = √(15² + 20²) = √625 = 25 km. (c) tan(angle XHY) = 20 ÷ 15, so angle XHY = 53.1°. The bearing of Y from H = 050° + 53.1° = 103.1°.

4. √(9² + 12² + 8²) = √289 = 17 cm.

5. The base diagonal is √(9² + 12²) = 15 cm. tan θ = 8 ÷ 15, so θ = 28.1°.

6. The half diagonal of the base is 10√2 ÷ 2 = 7.07 cm. (a) Edge = √(12² + 7.07²) = √194 = 13.9 cm. (b) tan θ = 12 ÷ 7.07 = 1.697, so θ = 59.5°.

7. The angle between the two courses at the port is 100° − 030° = 70°. By the cosine rule: d² = 6² + 9² − 2 × 6 × 9 × cos 70° = 117 − 108 × 0.3420 = 117 − 36.94 = 80.06, so d = 8.95 km.

14.Key Points

  • A bearing is measured clockwise from North and written with three figures.
  • “Bearing of B from A”: put the North line at A.
  • Back bearing: add 180° if the bearing is under 180°, otherwise subtract 180°.
  • East = distance × sin(bearing). North = distance × cos(bearing).
  • To find an angle at a turn, use the back bearing of the first leg. Then use Pythagoras, SOH-CAH-TOA, or the sine and cosine rules.
  • Space diagonal of a cuboid = √(l² + w² + h²).
  • The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its shadow on the plane.
  • In a square-based pyramid, the foot of the height is at the centre, half a diagonal from each corner.

15.For Teachers and Parents

Bearings are best learned outdoors. Take a compass and a protractor into a playground, choose a landmark, and have learners find its bearing, then walk to it and find the back bearing. For the angle at a turn, ask learners to draw the North line at every vertex before they write a single number, and to shade the angle they actually need. For 3D work, a cuboid made from a cardboard box and a piece of string (stretched from a bottom corner to the opposite top corner) makes the space diagonal real. A pyramid made from straws and modelling clay shows why the height lands on the centre. The most useful habit to build is redrawing the hidden triangle flat before attempting any calculation.
