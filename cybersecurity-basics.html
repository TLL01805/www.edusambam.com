Cybersecurity Basics: Threats, Defences and Good Digital Habits

Most attacks rely on a reused password, a missed update or one rushed click. This guide explains how malware, phishing and weak passwords work, and which defences matter most, with diagrams, worked examples and a quiz.

Every phone, laptop and online account holds something worth protecting: photos, messages, school work, money, or simply the ability to use the device at all. Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting those things from people who try to steal, change, or lock them. The good news is that most attacks are not clever. They rely on a reused password, a missed update, or one rushed click. This guide explains what is being protected, how the common attacks work, and which defences matter most, so the advice you hear ("use a strong password", "don't click that link") becomes something you understand rather than a rule you memorise. Everything here is defensive: it is about protecting yourself, not about attacking anyone.

Helpful background: What Is the Internet, Really? explains how data travels, and Search Engines & Web Browsers explains how web addresses work, which helps with the section on suspicious links.

1.What Does Cybersecurity Protect?

Security professionals describe the goals of protection using three words, often called the CIA triad (nothing to do with any agency):

  • Confidentiality: information can be seen only by the people who are meant to see it.
  • Integrity: information is accurate and has not been changed in ways it should not have been.
  • Availability: information and services can be used when they are needed.
Figure 1. Good security keeps all three goals in balance. The dashed outline moves gently unless your device is set to reduce motion.
Worked Example 1: Which goal was broken?

For each incident, decide which goal is mainly affected.

  • A stranger reads your private messages. Only the right people should see them, so this breaks confidentiality.
  • Someone secretly changes the marks stored in a school database. The information is no longer accurate, so this breaks integrity.
  • Ransomware locks every file on a family computer. The files cannot be used, so this breaks availability. (If the attackers also copied the files, confidentiality is broken as well.)
  • A website is flooded with so much traffic that real visitors cannot get in. This breaks availability.

One incident can break more than one goal, so it helps to ask the question for each of the three.

2.Four Words Worth Knowing: Asset, Threat, Vulnerability, Risk

  • An asset is anything valuable you want to protect, such as an account, a photo library or a device.
  • A threat is something that could cause harm, such as a criminal sending scam messages.
  • A vulnerability is a weakness that a threat could use, such as an out-of-date app or a password used on many sites. Using a vulnerability to get in is called an exploit.
  • Risk is how likely it is that harm will happen, combined with how bad it would be.

Thinking in terms of risk helps you decide what to fix first, because you cannot fix everything at once.

Worked Example 2: Ranking risks

A simple method is to score likelihood from 1 (very unlikely) to 5 (very likely) and impact from 1 (minor) to 5 (severe), then multiply: risk = likelihood × impact. The scores below are judgements made for teaching, not measurements.

SituationLikelihoodImpactRisk
Same password used on five sites, including email4520
Phone with no screen lock is lost3412
Phone updates ignored for months339
A classmate glimpses your lock-screen notification212

Conclusion: fix the password reuse first (20), then add a screen lock (12), then turn on updates (9). The last item is worth ignoring for now. The numbers are subjective, but the habit of ranking is useful.

Attackers have different motives, and most are criminals looking for money. Most attacks on ordinary people are opportunistic: huge numbers of messages are sent or passwords tested, to see who falls for it. That is why "nobody would want to hack me" is not a good defence. Automated attacks do not choose targets by name.

3.Malware: Software Built to Cause Harm

Malware (short for malicious software) is any program designed to damage a system, steal from it, or take control of it. Common types are:

TypeWhat it does
VirusAttaches itself to a legitimate file and spreads when that file is shared or opened
WormSpreads on its own across networks, without needing a file to attach to
TrojanPretends to be useful software so that a person installs it willingly
RansomwareLocks (encrypts) a victim's files and demands payment for the key
SpywareSecretly records activity and sends it to an attacker; a keylogger records what is typed
BotnetA group of infected devices controlled together, often used to send spam or flood websites

Malware usually arrives through ordinary routes: an email attachment or link, a fake or pirated download, a malicious advertisement or website, an infected USB drive, or a weakness in software that has not been updated. Warning signs include unexpected pop-ups, programs you did not install, a device that is suddenly much slower or hotter, a battery that drains unusually fast, or security tools that have been switched off. None of these proves infection by itself, but together they are worth checking.

Real-World Example: WannaCry (2017)

On 12 May 2017, a ransomware worm called WannaCry spread rapidly around the world and disrupted many organisations, including services in the National Health Service in England. It used a flaw in Windows for which Microsoft had already released a fix in March 2017. Computers that had not applied that update were exposed. The lesson was clear and still applies: updates matter because attackers actively use known flaws, and a patch only protects the devices on which it is installed.

4.Phishing and Social Engineering: Attacking the Person

Many successful attacks never break any software. Instead, they trick a person. This is called social engineering. The best known form is phishing: a message, usually an email or text, that pretends to be from a trusted source to get you to click a link, open a file, or enter a password on a fake page. Related forms include smishing (by text message), vishing (by phone call) and spear phishing, which targets one person using details found about them, so the message looks far more convincing than a mass email.

Phishing works by pressing human buttons: urgency ("act in 24 hours"), fear ("your account will be closed"), greed ("you have won") or authority ("this is your headteacher/bank"). The aim is to make you act before you stop to think.

Figure 2. All names and addresses are invented. Any one sign is a reason to stop and check; several together are a strong warning.
Worked Example 3: Which website does this link really go to?

The link in Figure 2 reads www.mybank.example.verify-login.example. The words "example" are used here because it is a name reserved for teaching, so no real site is involved.

Step 1: Find where the address ends. The address part finishes at the first single "/" (or at the end if there is none). Here there is no "/", so the whole thing is the address.

Step 2: Read from the right. The real website is the last two parts, the domain: verify-login.example. Everything to the left (www.mybank.example.) is only a prefix chosen by whoever owns that domain.

Step 3: Compare. The genuine bank's domain would be mybank.example, with nothing after it before the first "/". Here the bank's name appears only as part of the prefix, to look familiar.

Conclusion: this link goes to verify-login.example, not the bank. Do not click it. Instead, open the bank's app or type its address yourself.

Note: some countries use two-part endings such as .co.uk, so the rule is "the name just before the ending", but the habit of reading from the right still works.

What to do with a suspicious message: do not click links or open attachments, do not reply, check the claim by going to the real website or app yourself, report it (most email and messaging services have a report option), and delete it. In school or at work, tell the person responsible for IT.

5.Passwords: Length, Uniqueness and Reuse

A password protects an account only if an attacker cannot guess it or find it elsewhere. There are two main dangers:

  • Guessing. Attackers try lists of the most common passwords first (such as names, keyboard patterns and simple words), then try more combinations. Programs can try enormous numbers of guesses very quickly when they have a stolen copy of scrambled passwords to work on.
  • Reuse. When a website is breached and its stored account details are stolen, attackers try the same email-and-password pair on many other sites. This is called credential stuffing. If you reused the password, the breach spreads to every account that shares it.
Worked Example 4: Why length beats cleverness

The number of possible passwords is (number of possible characters) raised to the power of (length). Suppose an attacker can test 10 billion guesses per second against a stolen file. That figure is only an illustration. Real speeds vary hugely depending on how the website stored the password, so treat these as comparisons, not promises.

  • 8 random lowercase letters: 268 ≈ 2.1 × 1011 possibilities. At 1010 a second, that is about 21 seconds.
  • 8 random characters from the full keyboard (94 symbols): 948 ≈ 6.1 × 1015, about 7 days.
  • 12 random lowercase letters: 2612 ≈ 9.5 × 1016, about 110 days.
  • 4 random words from a 2,000-word list: 20004 = 1.6 × 1013, about 27 minutes.
  • 6 random words from the same list: 20006 = 6.4 × 1019, about 203 years.
  • 12 random characters from the full keyboard: 9412 ≈ 4.8 × 1023, about 1.5 million years.

What this shows: each extra character multiplies the work, so a few extra characters make a huge difference. A long passphrase of random words can match a short, tricky-looking password but is easier for a person to remember.

Important limit: these figures assume the characters or words are chosen truly at random. Passwords made of names, dates or familiar patterns such as "Summer2026!" are guessed much faster because attackers try those first. Random choice is what makes the arithmetic work.

Figure 3. Based on Worked Example 4 (10 billion guesses a second, fully random choices). The numbers are illustrations; the pattern is what matters.

What current expert guidance says

Modern guidance, including the United States' NIST Digital Identity Guidelines (SP 800-63B) and the CISA "Secure Our World" campaign, points the same way: use long passwords, make them unique for every account, and use a password manager, which is a program that creates and stores strong passwords for you so you only remember one strong master password. NIST also advises that services should not force strange symbol rules or force people to change passwords on a timetable. Changing a password is needed when there is evidence it may be compromised, for example after a breach. Making people change passwords constantly tends to produce weaker, more predictable ones.

6.Multi-Factor Authentication and Passkeys

Authentication means proving you are who you say you are. There are three kinds of proof:

  • Something you know: a password or PIN.
  • Something you have: your phone, an authenticator app or a physical security key.
  • Something you are: a fingerprint or face scan.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA), also called two-step verification, requires proof from at least two different kinds. If someone steals your password, they still cannot log in without the second factor. Not all second factors are equally strong. A code from an authenticator app is generally safer than a code sent by text message, and a physical security key resists phishing even better. Any form of MFA is still much better than none. Turn it on first for your email, because email can be used to reset other accounts.

Passkeys are a newer way to log in. Instead of a password, your device holds a secret cryptographic key and proves possession of it, usually after you unlock the device with a fingerprint, face or PIN. Because there is no password to type, there is nothing for a fake website to steal in the usual way. Many major services now support passkeys, though support varies.

If you get a login approval request or code you did not ask for, do not approve it. Someone may have your password, so change it.

7.Encryption: Making Stolen Data Useless

Encryption scrambles data using a mathematical key so that only someone with the right key can read it. The original readable data is called plaintext and the scrambled version is ciphertext.

Worked Example 5: A very old cipher, and why it is weak

The Caesar cipher is named after the Roman general Julius Caesar, who is said to have used something similar. It shifts each letter forward by a fixed number. The key is the shift. Let us use a shift of 3.

Encrypt "CYBER": C→F, Y→B (after Z the alphabet wraps back to A), B→E, E→H, R→U. The ciphertext is FBEHU.

Decrypt: shift each letter back by 3: F→C, B→Y, E→B, H→E, U→R, giving CYBER again.

Why it is weak: there are only 25 possible shifts, so anyone can try them all in moments. Modern encryption works on the same idea (a key scrambles the data) but uses keys with so many possibilities that trying them all is impossible. For example, a 128-bit key has 2128 (about 3.4 × 1038) possible values.

You rely on encryption every day:

  • HTTPS encrypts data between your browser and a website, so people on the same network cannot read it. The padlock (or similar icon) shows this. It does not prove the site is honest.
  • End-to-end encrypted messaging means that only the sender and the recipient can read a message, not even the company that carries it.
  • Hashing is related but different. Well-run websites do not store your actual password. They store a "hash", a one-way scrambled version that cannot be turned back into the password. When you log in, they hash what you typed and compare the results.

A VPN (virtual private network) encrypts your connection between your device and the VPN company's server. It can protect against snooping on the local network and hide your address from the sites you visit, but it does not make you anonymous. Instead, you are trusting the VPN company with the data your internet provider would otherwise see.

8.Defence in Depth: Many Layers

No single protection is perfect, so security uses layers. This idea is called defence in depth. If one layer fails, another can still stop the attack, just as a castle has a moat, walls and guards.

Figure 4. Defence in depth. An attacker has to get past several layers, and each layer you strengthen makes the next one less likely to be needed.

Here are the main tools and what each one does:

  • Software updates (patches) fix known vulnerabilities. Turning on automatic updates is one of the highest-value steps you can take. A zero-day vulnerability is one the maker does not yet know about, so no fix exists, which is why layers matter.
  • Firewalls filter network traffic by rules, blocking connections that should not be allowed. Most devices and routers include one.
  • Anti-malware software looks for known malicious programs and suspicious behaviour. It is helpful but not perfect, especially against brand-new threats.
  • Least privilege means giving each user or program only the access it needs. Using a normal account for daily use, instead of an administrator account, limits what malware can do.
  • Screen locks and device encryption protect data if a device is lost or stolen.

9.Backups and Ransomware

Ransomware shows why backups are a security tool and not just a convenience. If a clean copy of your files exists somewhere the attacker cannot reach, a ransomware attack becomes an inconvenience rather than a disaster. A widely used rule of thumb is 3-2-1: keep 3 copies of important files, on 2 different kinds of storage, with 1 kept off-site or offline. Remember that an always-connected synced folder is not a true backup, because deletions and encrypted files can sync too (see Cloud Computing for the difference).

If ransomware strikes, disconnect the device from the network to limit the spread, tell the responsible adult or IT team, and do not rush to pay. Law-enforcement and security agencies generally advise against paying, because payment does not guarantee that the files will be returned and it encourages further attacks.

10.Staying Safe on Phones, Wi-Fi and Social Media

  • Phones: set a screen lock, install apps only from the official store, check which permissions an app asks for (does a torch app really need your contacts?), and turn on "find my device".
  • Public Wi-Fi: it is convenient but you do not control it. HTTPS protects most of what you do, but avoid entering sensitive details on unfamiliar networks, and be careful about networks with names that merely look official.
  • Oversharing: information you post, such as your school, birthday, pet's name or holiday dates, can be used to guess passwords or to make scams more convincing. Review who can see your posts.
  • Scam warning signs: pressure to act fast, requests for secrecy, unusual payment methods such as gift cards, and offers that seem too good to be true.

For more on what you leave behind online, see Digital Footprint & Online Privacy.

11.If Something Goes Wrong

Mistakes happen to careful people too. The most important rule is to act quickly and tell someone instead of hiding it. If you think an account has been taken over:

  1. Use a device you trust to change the password, making it new and unique.
  2. Turn on MFA, and sign out of all other devices if the service offers that option.
  3. Check the account's recovery email and phone number, and any forwarding rules, in case the attacker changed them.
  4. Change the password anywhere else you used the same one.
  5. Tell a trusted adult, teacher or IT team, and report the problem to the service.

If money was sent, contact the bank or payment provider immediately. If a message or photo is being used to threaten you, keep the evidence, do not give in, and talk to a trusted adult.

12.Law and Ethics

Getting into someone else's computer, account or network without permission is illegal in most countries, even "just to see". Security is a respected career, and ethical hackers test systems only with the owner's clear permission. If you are curious, practise on systems you own or on environments built for learning.

13.Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

  • "I have nothing worth stealing." Your account can be used to scam your contacts, and attacks are mostly automated.
  • "Phones and Macs cannot get malware." Every kind of device can be attacked, even though the details differ.
  • "Antivirus is enough." It is one layer. Updates, unique passwords, MFA and careful habits matter just as much.
  • "I must change my password every month." Current guidance recommends changing it when there is a reason, such as a breach.
  • "The padlock means the site is safe." It means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is trustworthy.

14.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer. A scored multiple-choice quiz follows below.

1. What do the letters C, I and A stand for in the CIA triad?

Confidentiality (only the right people can see data), Integrity (data is accurate and not wrongly changed) and Availability (data can be used when needed).

2. What is the difference between a threat and a vulnerability?

A threat is something that could cause harm, such as a scammer. A vulnerability is a weakness that the threat could use, such as an out-of-date app or a reused password.

3. What is the real website in the link www.shop.example.cheap-deals.example/login?

The address ends at the first single "/", so read from the right: the domain is cheap-deals.example. The "shop.example" part is only a prefix.

4. Why does password length matter so much?

Each extra character multiplies the number of possible passwords, so the work needed to guess it grows enormously. Random choice is also essential, because predictable passwords are tried first.

5. Why is MFA helpful even if your password is stolen?

The attacker also needs the second factor, such as a code from your authenticator app or a security key, so the password alone is not enough to log in.

6. Why is a synced folder not a good defence against ransomware?

Because the encrypted or deleted files can sync too, so every copy may be damaged. A separate backup, ideally off-line or off-site, can survive an attack.

15.Summary

  • Security protects confidentiality, integrity and availability of data and services.
  • Risk combines how likely harm is with how severe it would be, so fix the highest risks first.
  • Malware comes in many forms and usually arrives by ordinary routes, so updates and caution matter.
  • Phishing attacks people through urgency, fear and trust. Check where a link really goes before you click.
  • Long, random and unique passwords stored in a password manager protect against guessing and reuse.
  • Multi-factor authentication and passkeys add a strong second barrier.
  • Encryption keeps stolen data unreadable, and layers of defence mean one failure is not fatal.
  • Separate backups turn ransomware from a disaster into a nuisance.
  • If something goes wrong, act quickly and tell someone you trust.
For Teachers and Parents

Spot the phish (no accounts needed): print several made-up messages, some genuine-looking and some fake, and have pairs mark every warning sign. Use only invented addresses such as the reserved ".example" names.

Password arithmetic: ask students to recalculate Worked Example 4 with their own password-length ideas, using a calculator, and discuss why random choice is necessary.

Safety note: please keep all practice to invented examples. Do not test real passwords, scan other people's networks or try to access accounts that are not your own. Tell students to speak to a trusted adult if they are being threatened or pressured online.
