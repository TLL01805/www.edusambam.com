Mean, Median & Mode: Understanding and Interpreting Data

Learn how to find and compare the mean, median and mode from lists, frequency tables and grouped data, work backwards from a known mean, combine groups, and choose the fairest measure of the centre.

When a data set is large, it is hard to understand by reading every value. A measure of central tendency gives one number that describes where the data are centred. The three most common are the mean, the median and the mode. They often give different answers, because each one answers a different question. This guide explains how to find each measure from a list, from a frequency table and from grouped data, how to work backwards from a known mean, how to combine groups, and how to decide which measure is the fairest summary. It has 25 worked examples checked by computer, diagrams, three calculators, a practice drill, exam questions with answers, and a 20-question quiz.

1.What Is Central Tendency?

Central tendency means a typical or central value of a data set. Instead of listing every observation, we use one representative number.

MeasureBasic ideaHow to find it
MeanThe equal-share valueAdd all the values and divide by how many there are
MedianThe middle valuePut the values in order and take the middle one
ModeThe most common valueFind the value that occurs most often

In everyday speech, “the average” usually means the mean, but news reports and advertisements sometimes use it for the median or the mode without saying so. In mathematics you should always name the measure you are using. The mean of a data set is often written x̄, read “x bar”.

2.The Mean

Mean = Sum of all values ÷ Number of values

In symbols, x̄ = Σx ÷ n, where Σx (“sigma x”) means the sum of the values and n is the number of values.

Example 1 · Finding a mean

(a) Find the mean of 6, 8, 10, 12 and 14. The sum is 6 + 8 + 10 + 12 + 14 = 50 and there are 5 values, so the mean is 50 ÷ 5 = 10.

(b) Find the mean of 7, 9, 4 and 12. The sum is 32 and there are 4 values, so the mean is 8.

(c) Find the mean of 2, 3, 5 and 9. The sum is 19, so the mean is 19 ÷ 4 = 4.75. The mean does not have to be one of the original values, or even a whole number.

The mean as an equal share, and as a balance point

Imagine towers of blocks with 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 blocks. There are 40 blocks altogether. If you share them equally among the five towers, each tower gets 8 blocks. That is the mean. Another way to see it: if the values sit on a number line, the mean is the point where the line would balance, because the distances to the left of the mean cancel the distances to the right (Figure 1).

Figure 1. For 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12, the differences from the mean 8 are −4, −2, 0, 2 and 4, and they add up to zero.
Example 2 · Equal sharing

Five containers hold 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 litres. Total = 40 litres. Shared equally, each gets 40 ÷ 5 = 8 litres, so the mean is 8. Check: the differences from 8 are −4, −2, 0, +2, +4, and their sum is 0. The differences from the mean always add up to zero.

3.The Median

The median is the middle value when the data are arranged in order. The most important rule is: sort the data first. Unsorted data have no “middle”.

An odd number of values

Example 3 · Odd number of values

Data: 9, 3, 7, 5, 11. Ordered: 3, 5, 7, 9, 11. There are 5 values, so the 3rd value is the middle one. Median = 7.

An even number of values

When there are two middle values, the median is the mean of those two.

Median = (the two middle values added together) ÷ 2
Figure 2. The ordered data 4, 5, 7, 9, 12, 15 have two middle values, 7 and 9. The median is their mean, 8.
Example 4 · Even number of values

Data: 4, 12, 7, 9, 15, 5. Ordered: 4, 5, 7, 9, 12, 15. The middle values are 7 and 9, so the median is (7 + 9) ÷ 2 = 8. Notice that 8 is not in the list.

The median position

For n ordered values, if n is odd the median is at position (n + 1) ÷ 2. If n is even, the middle values are at positions n ÷ 2 and (n ÷ 2) + 1.

Example 5 · Median positions

9 values: position (9 + 1) ÷ 2 = 5th. 101 values: position (101 + 1) ÷ 2 = 51st. 10 values: positions 5th and 6th. 20 values: positions 10th and 11th.

“Data: 5, 9, 2, 7, 4. There are five values, so the median is the 3rd value, which is 2.”

What did the writer forget to do first?

The fix: the data must be put in order first: 2, 4, 5, 7, 9. The 3rd value is then 5, so the median is 5. The 3rd value of the unsorted list has no special meaning.

4.The Mode

The mode is the value that occurs most often. Finding it needs counting, not calculating, so it also works for categories.

Example 6 · Mode, two modes, and no mode

(a) Data: 2, 4, 4, 5, 7, 4, 9. The value 4 occurs three times, and every other value occurs less often. Mode = 4.

(b) Data: 2, 2, 4, 5, 5, 7, 8. Both 2 and 5 occur twice. The data are bimodal and the modes are 2 and 5.

(c) Data: 1, 2, 3, 4. Every value occurs once, so there is no mode.

If more than two values share the highest frequency, the data are sometimes called multimodal. Some teachers prefer to say there is no mode when every value occurs equally often. Follow the convention used in your own course.

“Data: 2, 2, 2, 5, 5, 9. The mode is 3, because 3 is the highest count.”

Is the mode the count or the value?

The fix: the mode is the value that occurs most often, not how many times it occurs. The value 2 occurs 3 times, so the mode is 2. The number 3 is the frequency of the mode.

5.Comparing the Three Measures

Example 7 · One data set, three answers

Data: 2, 3, 3, 4, 8. Mean = 20 ÷ 5 = 4. Median = the 3rd value = 3. Mode = 3, because it occurs twice. The three answers differ because they measure different things.

FeatureMeanMedianMode
Uses every value?YesNo (only the middle)No (only the most common)
Needs ordered data?NoYesNo
Always exists?Yes, for numbersYes, for ordered dataNot always, and there may be several
Affected by extreme values?StronglyLittleUsually not
Works for categories?NoOnly if the categories can be put in orderYes

6.Extreme Values and Skewed Data

The mean uses every value, so one unusually large or small value pulls it towards itself. The median depends only on the middle, so it hardly moves.

Figure 3. Changing 14 to 100 moves the mean from 12 to 29.2, but the median stays at 12.
Example 8 · An extreme value

Set A: 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 has mean 12 and median 12. Replace 14 with 100 to get 10, 11, 12, 13, 100. The sum is 146, so the mean is 146 ÷ 5 = 29.2, but the median is still 12. Four of the five values are close to 12, so 29.2 is not a fair picture of a typical value.

Example 9 · A shop’s wages

Five people in a small business earn Rs 30 000, Rs 32 000, Rs 35 000, Rs 36 000 and Rs 200 000 a month. The total is Rs 333 000, so the mean is Rs 66 600. The median is the 3rd value, Rs 35 000. Four of the five earn between Rs 30 000 and Rs 36 000, so the median describes a typical wage better. An owner who wants to say that “the average wage is high” might choose the mean.

A data set with a long tail on the right (a few very large values) is called right-skewed, and the mean is usually pulled above the median. With a long tail on the left, it is left-skewed, and the mean is usually pulled below the median. Incomes and house prices are often right-skewed. For symmetrical data with one peak, the mean, median and mode are close to each other or equal, but their being close does not prove the data are symmetrical.

Example 10 · All measures from one list

A batter scores 12, 45, 0, 78, 23, 45 and 31 in seven innings. Ordered: 0, 12, 23, 31, 45, 45, 78. The sum is 234, so the mean is 234 ÷ 7 = 33.43 (2 decimal places). The median is 31, the mode is 45, and the range is 78 − 0 = 78.

7.Frequency Tables

When values repeat, a frequency table is shorter than a list. Here f is the frequency and x is the value.

Mean = Σfx ÷ Σf

Multiply each value by its frequency to get fx, then add the fx column and divide by the total frequency. For the median, add up the frequencies cumulatively and find which value the middle position falls in. The mode is the value with the highest frequency.

Mean = 46 ÷ 10 = 4.6. For the median, write the values out: 2, 2, 2, 4, 4, 6, 6, 6, 6, 8. There are 10 values, so use the 5th and 6th, which are 4 and 6. Median = (4 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5. The highest frequency is 4, belonging to the value 6, so the mode is 6.

Example 12 · Median from cumulative frequency

The total frequency is 10, so the middle positions are 5 and 6. The cumulative frequencies show that the 5th value is 2 (positions 3 to 5 are 2) and the 6th value is 3 (positions 6 to 9 are 3). Median = (2 + 3) ÷ 2 = 2.5. The mean is (2 + 6 + 12 + 4) ÷ 10 = 2.4 and the mode is 3.

“To find the mean of the table above, add the values and divide by how many: (1 + 2 + 3 + 4) ÷ 4 = 2.5.”

What has been ignored?

The fix: the frequencies were ignored. The value 3 occurs four times but was counted once. The correct mean is Σfx ÷ Σf = 24 ÷ 10 = 2.4. Never divide by the number of rows. Divide by the total frequency.

8.Working with Totals

Since Mean = Sum ÷ Number, we also have Sum = Mean × Number of values. This one idea solves many exam questions.

Example 13 · A missing value

The mean of five values is 18. Four values are 12, 16, 20 and 21. The required total is 18 × 5 = 90. The known total is 69. The missing value is 90 − 69 = 21.

Example 14 · Adding a value

Five values have mean 12. A sixth value, 18, is added. Original total = 60. New total = 78. New mean = 78 ÷ 6 = 13.

Example 15 · Removing a value

Eight values have mean 15. One value, 22, is removed. Original total = 120. New total = 98. Seven values remain, so the new mean is 98 ÷ 7 = 14.

Example 16 · The mean of part of a group

The mean of 5 numbers is 8. Three of them have a mean of 6. Find the mean of the other two. Total of all five = 5 × 8 = 40. Total of the three = 3 × 6 = 18. Total of the other two = 40 − 18 = 22. Their mean is 22 ÷ 2 = 11.

Combining two groups

Combined mean = (total of group A + total of group B) ÷ (size of A + size of B)
Example 17 · Combined mean

Group A has 20 values with mean 12. Group B has 30 values with mean 18. Totals: 20 × 12 = 240 and 30 × 18 = 540. Combined mean = 780 ÷ 50 = 15.6.

Example 18 · The unequal-groups trap

One group has 10 values with mean 20. Another has 90 values with mean 30. The simple average of the means is (20 + 30) ÷ 2 = 25, which wrongly treats the groups as equal in size. The correct combined mean is (10 × 20 + 90 × 30) ÷ 100 = 29.

“Class A has mean 70 and class B has mean 80, so the mean of both classes together is 75.”

When is this correct?

The fix: averaging two means is correct only when both groups have the same size. If class A has 10 students and class B has 30, the combined mean is (10 × 70 + 30 × 80) ÷ 40 = 77.5, closer to the larger group’s mean. Always combine the totals.

Weighted mean

A weighted mean gives each value a weight showing how important it is.

Weighted mean = Σ(weight × value) ÷ Σ(weights)
Example 19 · Weighted means

(a) Scores 70, 80 and 90 have weights 2, 3 and 5. Weighted total = 140 + 240 + 450 = 830. Total weight = 10. Weighted mean = 83.

(b) A course gives a mark of 72 a weight of 3 and a mark of 85 a weight of 2. Weighted mean = (216 + 170) ÷ 5 = 386 ÷ 5 = 77.2. The simple mean, 78.5, would give both marks equal importance.

9.Grouped Data

When data are grouped into classes, such as 10 ≤ t < 20 (“10 up to but not including 20”), we no longer know the exact values. We can only estimate the mean and the median. For the mode we can name the modal class, the class with the highest frequency.

  • Midpoint of a class = (lower boundary + upper boundary) ÷ 2.
  • Estimated mean = Σ(f × midpoint) ÷ Σf. Every value in a class is treated as if it were at the midpoint.
  • Estimated median = L + [(N ÷ 2 − CF) ÷ f] × h, where L is the lower boundary of the median class, N is the total frequency, CF is the cumulative frequency before that class, f is its frequency and h is its width. The median class is the one that contains the (N ÷ 2)th value.
  • Estimated mode = L + [(f₁ − f₀) ÷ (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)] × h, using the modal class, where f₁ is its frequency, f₀ the frequency of the class before it and f₂ the frequency of the class after it. This formula assumes equal class widths. Many courses ask only for the modal class.

Example 20 · Grouped-data estimates

Estimated mean: 630 ÷ 30 = 21 minutes.

Modal class: the highest frequency is 12, so the modal class is 20 ≤ t < 30.

Estimated median: N ÷ 2 = 15, and the cumulative frequencies 4, 13, 25, 30 show that the 15th value is in the class 20 ≤ t < 30. Here L = 20, CF = 13, f = 12 and h = 10. Median ≈ 20 + (15 − 13) ÷ 12 × 10 = 20 + 1.67 = 21.7 minutes (1 decimal place).

Estimated mode: f₁ = 12, f₀ = 9, f₂ = 5. Mode ≈ 20 + (12 − 9) ÷ (24 − 9 − 5) × 10 = 20 + 3 = 23 minutes.

The median can also be read from a cumulative frequency graph. Plot each cumulative frequency at the upper boundary of its class, join the points, draw a line across from N ÷ 2 = 15, and read the value below where it meets the curve (Figure 5). The graph gives about 21.7, matching the calculation. These are estimates, because we never knew the exact times inside each class.

Figure 5. The cumulative frequency graph for the journey times. The dashed line from 15 meets the graph at about 21.7 minutes.

10.Categorical Data

Some data are categories, such as transport type or favourite fruit. Nominal categories have no natural order, so the mean and median make no sense. The mode still tells you the most common category.

Example 21 · Categorical mode

Transport choices: bus, car, bus, bicycle, bus, train, car. Bus occurs 3 times, car 2 times, and the others once. The mode is bus.

11.What Happens When Every Value Changes?

If the same number is added to every value, the mean, median and mode all go up by that number, and the range does not change. If every value is multiplied by the same number, the mean, median and mode are all multiplied by it as well.

Example 22 · Adding a constant

Data: 4, 6, 8, 8, 10 has mean 7.2, median 8 and mode 8. Add 5 to every value: 9, 11, 13, 13, 15 has mean 12.2, median 13 and mode 13.

Example 23 · Multiplying

Data: 2, 3, 3, 5, 7 has mean 4, median 3 and mode 3. Multiply every value by 2: 4, 6, 6, 10, 14 has mean 8, median 6 and mode 6.

This is useful when converting units. If the mean of some lengths is 12 cm, the mean in millimetres is 120 mm.

12.Centre and Spread

The range is a measure of spread, not of centre: Range = Maximum − Minimum. Two data sets can have the same mean and be very different.

Example 24 · Centre versus spread

For 5, 6, 6, 7, 16: the mean is 8, the median is 6, the mode is 6, and the range is 16 − 5 = 11.

Team A scores 40, 50 and 60. Team B scores 10, 50 and 90. Both have mean 50. The range for A is 20 and for B is 80, so A is much more consistent. A mean alone would hide that.

Checking that an answer is sensible

A mean must lie between the smallest and largest values (it can equal one of them only if all values are equal). The median and the mode always lie within the same range. If your answer falls outside it, there is an error.

Example 25 · A sanity check

All the values lie between 20 and 50, but a student gets a mean of 57. That cannot be right. Recheck the addition and the number of values you divided by.

13.Choosing the Best Measure

SituationUseful measureWhy
Numbers with no extreme valuesMeanUses every value
Numbers with extreme values or a skewed shape (wages, house prices)MedianNot pulled by the extremes
The most popular size, colour or choiceModeShows the most common item
Categories with no orderModeMean and median are meaningless
Values with different importanceWeighted meanWeights show importance
Real-Life Example

A shoe shop wants to know which size to stock the most of. The mode (the most common size) answers that. A town planner studying household income might report the median, because a few very high incomes would drag the mean upwards. A teacher working out a final grade from tests with different weights uses a weighted mean.

Whenever someone quotes an “average”, ask which one it is, how many values it is based on, and how spread out the data are. The same data can be described honestly with the median and misleadingly with the mean, or the other way round.

14.Common Mistakes

  • Not ordering the data before finding the median.
  • Dividing by the wrong number. Count the values carefully, and in a frequency table divide by Σf, not by the number of rows.
  • Giving the frequency instead of the value as the mode.
  • Taking the largest value as the mode.
  • Assuming every data set has one mode, or that the mean must be one of the values.
  • Averaging two group means without checking the group sizes.
  • Using mean or median for categories.
  • Confusing range with a measure of the centre.
  • Using the wrong boundary in grouped data, or treating an estimate as an exact value.
  • Rounding too early, before the final step.

15.A Reliable Method

  1. Decide which measure is asked for, and whether the data are numbers or categories.
  2. For the median, put the data in order and find the middle position or positions.
  3. For the mode, count how often each value occurs.
  4. For the mean, find the total and the number of values (use fx and Σf for a table).
  5. For grouped data, use midpoints and say that the answer is an estimate.
  6. Check that the answer is between the smallest and largest values.
  7. Give the answer with units and interpret it in the context of the question.

16.Try It Yourself

  1. Find the mean, median and mode of 4, 9, 5, 9, 3, 6.
  2. The mean of 4 numbers is 15. A fifth number, 25, is added. What is the new mean?
  3. Values 1, 2, 3 have frequencies 2, 5, 3. Find the mean.
Show answers

1. Ordered: 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 9. The sum is 36, so the mean is 36 ÷ 6 = 6. The median is (5 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5.5. The mode is 9.

2. Original total = 4 × 15 = 60. New total = 85. New mean = 85 ÷ 5 = 17.

3. Σfx = 2 + 10 + 9 = 21 and Σf = 10. Mean = 2.1.

17.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [4 marks] Find the mean, median, mode and range of 6, 2, 9, 2, 8, 2, 5, 10.
  2. [3 marks] The mean of 5 numbers is 8. Three of the numbers have a mean of 6. Find the mean of the other two.
  3. [5 marks] The numbers of books read in a month by 24 students are: 0 books (5 students), 1 book (8), 2 books (6), 3 books (3), 4 books (2). Find (a) the mean to 2 decimal places, (b) the median, (c) the mode.
  4. [3 marks] 12 students have a mean mark of 60. 8 other students have a mean mark of 75. Find the mean mark of all 20 students.
  5. [6 marks] The heights of 30 students are: 150 ≤ h < 160 (6 students), 160 ≤ h < 170 (14), 170 ≤ h < 180 (8), 180 ≤ h < 190 (2). Find (a) the modal class, (b) an estimate of the mean, (c) an estimate of the median, to 1 decimal place.
  6. [3 marks] Five people earn Rs 30 000, Rs 32 000, Rs 35 000, Rs 36 000 and Rs 200 000 a month. Find the mean and the median, and say which better represents a typical wage.
  7. [3 marks] The mean of 6 numbers is 9. A seventh number is added and the mean becomes 10. Find the seventh number.
Show answers

1. Ordered: 2, 2, 2, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10. The sum is 44, so the mean is 44 ÷ 8 = 5.5. The median is (5 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5.5. The mode is 2. The range is 10 − 2 = 8.

2. Total of five = 40. Total of three = 18. Total of the other two = 22. Their mean is 11.

3. Σf = 24 and Σfx = 0 + 8 + 12 + 9 + 8 = 37. (a) Mean = 37 ÷ 24 = 1.54. (b) Use the 12th and 13th values. The cumulative frequencies are 5, 13, 19, 22, 24, so both are 1 and the median is 1. (c) The highest frequency is 8, so the mode is 1.

4. Totals: 12 × 60 = 720 and 8 × 75 = 600. Combined mean = 1320 ÷ 20 = 66.

5. (a) The highest frequency is 14, so the modal class is 160 ≤ h < 170. (b) Midpoints 155, 165, 175, 185 give Σfx = 930 + 2310 + 1400 + 370 = 5010. Mean ≈ 5010 ÷ 30 = 167. (c) N ÷ 2 = 15. The cumulative frequencies are 6, 20, 28, 30, so the median class is 160 ≤ h < 170. Median ≈ 160 + (15 − 6) ÷ 14 × 10 = 160 + 6.43 = 166.4.

6. Mean = 333 000 ÷ 5 = Rs 66 600. Median = Rs 35 000. The median is more typical, because four of the five earn between Rs 30 000 and Rs 36 000 and the one very high wage pulls the mean up.

7. Old total = 6 × 9 = 54. New total = 7 × 10 = 70. The seventh number is 70 − 54 = 16.

19.Key Points

  • The mean is the equal-share value, found by total ÷ number. The differences from the mean add up to zero.
  • The median is the middle of the ordered data. With an even number of values, take the mean of the two middle ones.
  • The mode is the most frequent value or category. There may be none, one, or several.
  • The mean is pulled by extreme values. The median is not. Use the median for skewed data.
  • In a frequency table, use Σfx ÷ Σf for the mean and cumulative frequency for the median.
  • Use totals to find missing values and to combine groups. Never average two means without checking the group sizes.
  • For grouped data, the mean and median are estimates, and the mode is a modal class.
  • Always say which average you used, and check that your answer lies between the smallest and largest values.

20.For Teachers and Parents

The “equal share” idea is the best way in. Give learners towers of linking cubes of different heights and ask them to level the towers by moving cubes: the final height is the mean. For the median, ask learners to stand in a line in order of height and find the middle person. For the mode, make a quick class survey (for example, how learners travel to school) and ask which is the most common. Then collect a real set of measurements, such as arm spans or journey times, and ask learners to predict whether the mean or the median will be higher before they calculate. The Data Calculator, Frequency Table Calculator and Grouped Data Calculator on this page let learners check their own working, including the working for a median with an even number of values. Encourage learners to say in a sentence what each number means.
