Brackets, Fractions & Decimals in BODMAS

BODMAS covers four kinds of brackets, not just one. Once fractions and decimals join an expression, a few extra habits become essential. This guide fills in both gaps.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|15 min read|Last updated: October 2026|Mathematics

Our BODMAS & DMAS Rules article covered the core order of operations. This companion guide goes deeper into the places where students most often lose marks: the different kinds of brackets and the order they are removed in, the fraction bar that works as a hidden bracket, the word “of”, and how fractions and decimals behave once they are dropped into a longer expression. Every example is worked line by line and checked.

1.Why Bracket Order Matters

The “B” in BODMAS is often taught as if there is only one kind of bracket. In fact there are four kinds of grouping symbol, and long expressions often use several at once so that the grouping is easy to see. One rule is the same everywhere: always work from the innermost group outwards. The rest of this guide shows how to do that without slips.

2.The Four Types of Brackets

BracketSymbolAlso Called
Bar4 − 2Vinculum, line bracket
Parentheses( )Round brackets, small brackets
Curly Brackets{ }Braces
Square Brackets[ ]Box brackets

The bar (vinculum) is the oldest and least common today. It is a line drawn directly over the terms it groups, instead of symbols on either side. It still appears in three places: over the terms on the top or bottom of a fraction, over everything inside a square root sign, and over repeating digits in a decimal.

3.The Removal Order

Many South Asian textbooks, including those used for school boards in Pakistan and India, teach a fixed order for mixed brackets. It matches how those books nest the symbols, with the bar innermost and the square brackets outermost:

OrderBracket
1stBar (vinculum)
2ndParentheses ( )
3rdCurly brackets { }
4thSquare brackets [ ]
Good to Know

This is a convention, not a law of mathematics. Some textbooks in other countries nest the symbols differently, for example ( ) inside [ ] inside { }. What never changes is the idea underneath: the innermost group goes first. When you meet a nested expression, follow your own textbook’s order, and if the brackets are nested properly, “innermost first” gives the same answer.

Work from the innermost layer to the outermost, like peeling an onion.
Example 1: Bar First

8 + 4 − 2: the bar groups “4 − 2” as if it were in its own brackets. Resolve the bar first: 4 − 2 = 2. Then 8 + 2 = 10. (Without the bar, 8 + 4 − 2 would be 12 − 2 = 10 as well, but the bar matters when the operation in front is a minus: 8 − 4 − 2 = 8 − 2 = 6, whereas 8 − 4 − 2 = 2.)

Example 2: All Four Brackets Together

100 − [20 + {10 − (6 ÷ 2)}] ÷ 2
Step 1: parentheses (innermost): 6 ÷ 2 = 3
Step 2: curly brackets: 10 − 3 = 7
Step 3: square brackets: 20 + 7 = 27
Step 4: division: 27 ÷ 2 = 13.5
Step 5: subtraction: 100 − 13.5 = 86.5

The highlighted part is the step being worked out on each line.
Opening the outer square brackets first, because they “look like the main part”

Starting from the outside means you try to use numbers that have not been worked out yet.

The fix: always work from the innermost group outwards: bar first, then ( ), then { }, then [ ] last. In Example 2 the answer to (6 ÷ 2) is needed before { } can be finished, and { } is needed before [ ] can be finished. The square brackets are almost always the outermost layer, so they are resolved last.

4.The Fraction Bar Is a Bracket Too

A fraction’s horizontal bar does more than separate the numerator from the denominator. It silently groups everything written above it and everything written below it, exactly like a bracket. Only terms actually written on the fraction bar are grouped, and anything sitting outside the fraction stays separate.

The dashed boxes show what the bar groups. Check exactly what sits above and below.
Example 3

(a) (3 + 5) ÷ 4 = 8 ÷ 4 = 2. Both 3 and 5 sit “on the bar”, so they are grouped together before dividing.

(b) 3 + 5 ÷ 4 = 3 + 1.25 = 4.25. Here only the 5 is over the bar, so only 5 ÷ 4 is grouped. The 3 stays separate.

(c) A fraction with sums on both levels: (6 + 2)/(5 − 1) means (6 + 2) ÷ (5 − 1) = 8 ÷ 4 = 2. Work out the top, work out the bottom, then divide.

Treating “3 + 5/4” as if it means “(3 + 5)/4”

Without brackets, only the term actually written as part of the fraction gets grouped by the fraction bar.

The fix: 3 + 5/4 means 3 + (5 ÷ 4) = 3 + 1.25 = 4.25. If both 3 and 5 were meant to be added before dividing by 4, the expression would need explicit brackets: (3 + 5)/4 = 2. Always check exactly what sits directly above and below the fraction bar.

5.The Word “Of” in Fraction Problems

Fraction problems often use the word “of”, as in “half of 20”. It means multiply: 1/2 of 20 = 1/2 × 20 = 10. In many South Asian textbooks, the “O” in BODMAS stands for “of”, and “of” is worked out straight after the brackets, before division and multiplication. In the UK and many other places, the “O” stands for “orders” (powers and roots), and “of” is simply treated as a multiplication.

Example 4

(a) 1/2 of 36 − 4: “of” first: 1/2 × 36 = 18. Then 18 − 4 = 14. Both conventions agree here.

(b) 8 ÷ 2 of 2: with the “of first” rule, 2 of 2 = 4, so 8 ÷ 4 = 2. With plain left-to-right division and multiplication, (8 ÷ 2) × 2 = 8.

The two conventions disagree on (b). The safe habit is to write brackets so nobody can misread it: 8 ÷ (2 of 2) or (8 ÷ 2) × 2. Follow the rule in your own syllabus in an exam, and use brackets in your own writing.

6.Fractions in BODMAS Expressions

Once fractions appear inside a larger expression, BODMAS still applies exactly as before: brackets first, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction. The only difference is that you use fraction arithmetic at each step (see Fraction Fix-It and Multiply & Divide Fractions).

Example 5

(1/2 + 1/4) × 8
Brackets first, common denominator: 1/2 + 1/4 = 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4
Then multiply: 3/4 × 8 = 6

Example 6

3/4 × (2/3 + 1/6)
Brackets first: 2/3 + 1/6 = 4/6 + 1/6 = 5/6
Then multiply: 3/4 × 5/6 = 15/24 = 5/8

Example 7

2/3 + 1/3 × 3/5
Multiply first: 1/3 × 3/5 = 3/15 = 1/5
Then add: 2/3 + 1/5 = 10/15 + 3/15 = 13/15

Example 8: Mixed Numbers

2½ × (1/2 + 1/3)
Brackets first: 1/2 + 1/3 = 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6
Change 2½ to 5/2, then multiply: 5/2 × 5/6 = 25/12 = 2 1/12

Example 9: Division

(3/4 ÷ 1/2) − 1/4
Brackets first: 3/4 ÷ 1/2 = 3/4 × 2/1 = 6/4 = 3/2
Then subtract: 3/2 − 1/4 = 6/4 − 1/4 = 5/4, which is 1¼

Example 10: Fractions Inside a Fraction

(1/2 + 1/3) / (1/6)
Top first: 1/2 + 1/3 = 5/6. The bar means divide, so 5/6 ÷ 1/6 = 5/6 × 6/1 = 5.

7.Decimals in BODMAS Expressions

Decimals follow BODMAS exactly the same way. The only extra care needed is keeping the decimal point in the right place through each multiplication, division and addition step.

Two useful tricks: to multiply decimals, multiply as whole numbers and then count the decimal places (0.3 × 0.2: 3 × 2 = 6, and there are 2 decimal places in total, so 0.06). To divide by a decimal, multiply both numbers by 10 or 100 until the divisor is whole (6.4 ÷ 0.8 = 64 ÷ 8 = 8).

Example 11

2.5 + 3 × 0.4
Multiply first: 3 × 0.4 = 1.2
Then add: 2.5 + 1.2 = 3.7

Example 12

2.5 + 3 × 0.4 − (1.2 ÷ 0.6)
Brackets first: 1.2 ÷ 0.6 = 2
Multiply: 3 × 0.4 = 1.2
Left to right: 2.5 + 1.2 = 3.7, then 3.7 − 2 = 1.7

Example 13

0.5 × (3.6 + 2.4) − 1.8
Brackets first: 3.6 + 2.4 = 6.0
Multiply: 0.5 × 6.0 = 3.0
Subtract: 3.0 − 1.8 = 1.2

6.4 ÷ 0.8 + 1.5 × 2 solved as 6.4 ÷ (0.8 + 1.5) × 2

Grouping terms that are not actually in brackets is one of the most common decimal-BODMAS mistakes.

The fix: with no brackets present, division and multiplication are resolved left to right as separate steps: 6.4 ÷ 0.8 = 8, and 1.5 × 2 = 3. Then add: 8 + 3 = 11. Never combine terms across an operation unless brackets say so.

8.Mixing Fractions and Decimals

When a question mixes both, convert everything to one form first. Use decimals when they are short and exact, and fractions when a decimal would go on for ever.

Example 14

(a) 1/4 + 0.5: change 1/4 to 0.25, so 0.25 + 0.5 = 0.75.

(b) 3/4 − 1/3: do not use 0.333…, which is never exact. Use fractions: 9/12 − 4/12 = 5/12.

(c) (1/2)² + 0.25 × 2: power first, (1/2)² = 1/4 = 0.25. Multiply: 0.25 × 2 = 0.5. Add: 0.25 + 0.5 = 0.75.

(d) √0.36 + 1/5: the root first, √0.36 = 0.6. Then 0.6 + 0.2 = 0.8.

9.Real-Life Problems

Real-World Example

A recipe calls for (3/4 + 1/2) cups of flour per batch, tripled for a party: (3/4 + 1/2) × 3 = (5/4) × 3 = 15/4 = 3¾ cups. A shopping receipt calculating a discount uses decimals the same way: Rs 4,500 − (0.20 × Rs 4,500) = Rs 4,500 − Rs 900 = Rs 3,600. Brackets, then multiplication, then subtraction, exactly as BODMAS requires.

Example 15: Average Speed

A driver travels 3.5 hours at 60 km/h and then 1.25 hours at 80 km/h. What is the average speed for the whole trip?

Distance: 3.5 × 60 + 1.25 × 80 = 210 + 100 = 310 km.

Time: 3.5 + 1.25 = 4.75 hours.

Average speed = 310 ÷ (3.5 + 1.25) = 310 ÷ 4.75 ≈ 65.26 km/h. The brackets are essential. Without them, 310 ÷ 3.5 + 1.25 would give about 89.8, which is nonsense.

Notice that the average speed is not (60 + 80) ÷ 2 = 70 km/h, because more time was spent at the slower speed.

Did You Know?

The vinculum bar is also used to mark repeating decimals: a bar drawn over the repeating digits, like 0.3 to mean 0.333… for ever. It is the same grouping symbol, used for a completely different purpose.

10.Common Mistakes

  • Opening the outer brackets first. Always start with the innermost group.
  • Ignoring what sits on a fraction bar. 3 + 5/4 is not (3 + 5)/4.
  • Treating a bar as optional. 8 − 4 − 2 = 6, but 8 − 4 − 2 = 2.
  • Forgetting that division by a fraction means “flip and multiply”. 3/4 ÷ 1/2 = 3/2, not 3/8.
  • Grouping decimals that are not in brackets. Work left to right within each level.
  • Using a rounded decimal for a fraction. 1/3 is not 0.33. Keep it as a fraction until the end.
  • Assuming “of” follows the same convention everywhere. If a question could be read two ways, add brackets.

11.Try It Yourself

  1. Work out (1/3 + 1/6) × 4.
  2. Work out 3.6 ÷ (0.9 + 0.3) − 1.
  3. Work out 5 + 12/(2 + 4).
Show answers

1. Brackets: 1/3 + 1/6 = 2/6 + 1/6 = 1/2. Then 1/2 × 4 = 2.

2. Brackets: 0.9 + 0.3 = 1.2. Then 3.6 ÷ 1.2 = 3. Then 3 − 1 = 2.

3. The bottom of the fraction first: 2 + 4 = 6. Then 12 ÷ 6 = 2. Then 5 + 2 = 7.

12.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [3 marks] Work out 24 − [8 + {6 − (4 ÷ 2)}].
  2. [3 marks] Work out (2/3 + 1/4) × 6. Give your answer as a mixed number.
  3. [3 marks] Work out 1.5 + 0.5 × (4.2 − 1.8).
  4. [4 marks] A car travels 3.5 hours at 60 km/h and then 1.25 hours at 80 km/h. Find the average speed for the whole journey, correct to 2 decimal places.
Show answers

1. Parentheses: 4 ÷ 2 = 2. Curly: 6 − 2 = 4. Square: 8 + 4 = 12. Then 24 − 12 = 12.

2. Brackets: 2/3 + 1/4 = 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12. Then 11/12 × 6 = 66/12 = 11/2 = 5½.

3. Brackets: 4.2 − 1.8 = 2.4. Multiply: 0.5 × 2.4 = 1.2. Add: 1.5 + 1.2 = 2.7.

4. Distance = 3.5 × 60 + 1.25 × 80 = 210 + 100 = 310 km. Time = 3.5 + 1.25 = 4.75 h. Average speed = 310 ÷ 4.75 = 65.26 km/h (2 d.p.).

13.Key Points

  • Always work from the innermost bracket outwards. Many South Asian textbooks list the order bar, ( ), { }, [ ].
  • A fraction bar groups everything written above it and below it, like a bracket.
  • “Of” means multiply. Conventions differ on its priority, so add brackets to remove doubt.
  • With fractions, use fraction arithmetic at each step. With decimals, keep the point in the right place.
  • Convert to one form before mixing fractions and decimals. Keep fractions like 1/3 exact.
  • Write one step per line, and copy down everything that has not changed.

14.For Teachers and Parents

Ask learners to colour each bracket layer a different colour and to peel the expression from the inside, like an onion. Make them say aloud what is grouped by a fraction bar before they calculate. Tell them which bracket convention your syllabus uses, and also tell them that other books may differ, so that they are not surprised. Use real tasks (scaling a recipe, finding an average speed, working out a discount) so that the brackets have a reason to be there. Encourage one step per line, which is the most dependable way to cut errors.

15.Why This Topic Matters

Real formulas in recipes, finance, engineering and science almost never stick to whole numbers or a single bracket type. Being fluent with mixed brackets, fractions and decimals together is what turns BODMAS from a classroom rule into a usable everyday skill. It is also the direct preparation for algebra, where the same grouping rules apply to letters.
