Algebra: Basic, Intermediate & Advanced

A complete guide to algebra in three levels: notation, brackets and factorising, then equations, inequalities and quadratics, then sequences, graphs, functions, algebraic fractions, variation and polynomials.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|28 min read|Last updated: November 2026|Mathematics

Algebra is the language of patterns, relationships and unknown quantities. It can look like arithmetic with letters added, but its real purpose is more powerful: it lets you describe a general rule, solve for an unknown, model a real situation and reason about quantities that can change. Algebra is the foundation under equations, graphs, sequences, functions, coordinate geometry, variation, indices and surds. This guide builds the subject in three levels. Basic algebra covers notation, substitution, like terms and brackets. Intermediate algebra covers factorising, indices, surds, equations, inequalities, simultaneous equations and quadratics. Advanced algebra covers sequences, straight-line graphs, functions, algebraic fractions, variation and polynomials. It has 30 worked examples checked by computer, five diagrams, three tools, a practice drill, exam questions with answers, and a 20-question quiz.

Figure 1. Algebra develops from notation and manipulation into equations, then into functions, graphs and modelling.

1.What Algebra Really Means

Arithmetic asks you to calculate with known numbers. Algebra introduces variables so that one statement can stand for many possible values. In 3x + 5, the letter x represents a number whose value may be found later, may be restricted by a condition, or may be allowed to vary.

Example 1 · Translating words into algebra

“Five more than twice a number n” is 2n + 5. “Seven less than three times x” is 3x − 7. “The product of x and the number after x” is x(x + 1). Take care with order: “five less than x” is x − 5, not 5 − x.

2.Notation, Substitution and Evaluation

Multiplication is written without a sign: 3 × x = 3x, and xy means x × y. A square means a number multiplied by itself, so x² = x × x. The expression 2x² means 2 × x², not (2x)². Substitution means replacing a variable with a known value. Put the whole value in brackets, especially when it is negative or a fraction.

Example 2 · Substitution

If x = −3, evaluate 2x² − 5x + 1. Substitute: 2(−3)² − 5(−3) + 1 = 2(9) + 15 + 1 = 34. The brackets matter: (−3)² = 9, but −3² is read as −(3²) = −9.

In the formula A = ½bh, with b = 8 and h = 5: A = ½(8)(5) = 20 square units. Always state the unit when a formula describes a physical quantity.

3.Collecting Like Terms

Like terms have exactly the same variable part, including the same powers. So 3x and −7x are like terms, but 3x and 3x² are not. To simplify, add the coefficients and keep the variable part unchanged. This is different from multiplying: 3x + 5x = 8x, but 3x × 5x = 15x².

Example 3 · Simplifying

4x + 7 − 2x + 3 = (4x − 2x) + (7 + 3) = 2x + 10.

5a² − 3a + 2a² + 8a = 7a² + 5a.

3xy + 5x − 2xy − x = xy + 4x.

The expression x + x² cannot be written as 2x² or 2x, because x and x² are unlike terms. It is already as simple as it can be.

4.Expanding Brackets

The distributive law says a(b + c) = ab + ac. Multiply every term inside the bracket by the factor outside, and watch the signs. For two brackets, multiply every term in the first by every term in the second.

Example 4 · Single and double brackets

3(2x − 5) = 6x − 15. −2(4x + 3) = −8x − 6.

(x + 4)(x + 3) = x² + 3x + 4x + 12 = x² + 7x + 12.

(2x − 1)(x + 5) = 2x² + 10x − x − 5 = 2x² + 9x − 5.

Figure 2. An area model for (x + 4)(x + 3). The four parts of the rectangle are x², 4x, 3x and 12.

Three identities are worth learning because they save time:

(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²    (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²    (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²
Example 5 · Using the identities

(x + 3)² = x² + 6x + 9. (x − 5)² = x² − 10x + 25. (x + 7)(x − 7) = x² − 49.

“(x + 3)² = x² + 9.”

Test it with x = 2: what do you get on each side?

The fix: (x + 3)² means (x + 3)(x + 3), so the middle term 2 × x × 3 = 6x is needed: x² + 6x + 9. With x = 2 the left side is 5² = 25, but x² + 9 gives only 13. A square of a sum is not the sum of the squares.

5.Factorising

Factorising is the reverse of expanding: it writes an expression as a product. Always look first for a common factor, and take out the greatest one.

Example 6 · Common factors and grouping

6x² + 9x = 3x(2x + 3).

xy + 2x + 3y + 6 = x(y + 2) + 3(y + 2) = (x + 3)(y + 2).

For a quadratic x² + bx + c, find two numbers with product c and sum b. For ax² + bx + c with a ≠ 1, multiply a × c, find two numbers with that product that add to b, and use them to split the middle term.

Example 7 · Quadratic expressions

(a) x² + 7x + 12: numbers with product 12 and sum 7 are 3 and 4, so (x + 3)(x + 4).

(b) x² − x − 12: product −12 and sum −1 gives −4 and 3, so (x − 4)(x + 3).

(c) 2x² + 7x + 3: a × c = 6, and the numbers 6 and 1 add to 7. So 2x² + 6x + x + 3 = 2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3) = (2x + 1)(x + 3).

(d) 3x² − 10x + 8: a × c = 24, and −4 and −6 add to −10. So 3x² − 4x − 6x + 8 = x(3x − 4) − 2(3x − 4) = (3x − 4)(x − 2).

Example 8 · Difference of two squares

a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b). So 9x² − 25 = (3x − 5)(3x + 5), and 2x² − 50 = 2(x² − 25) = 2(x − 5)(x + 5). Always take out a common factor first.

6.Indices and Surds

For the same non-zero base: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ, aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ and (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ. Also a⁰ = 1 and a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ. Fractional indices link powers and roots: a^(1/2) = √a and a^(1/3) is the cube root of a.

Example 9 · Index laws

x³ × x⁵ = x⁸. y⁹ ÷ y⁴ = y⁵. (2a³)² = 4a⁶. 16x⁻² = 16/x² for x ≠ 0. 8^(1/3) = 2, and 8^(2/3) = 2² = 4.

A surd is an exact root that is not a rational number, such as √2. To simplify, take out square factors: √72 = √(36 × 2) = 6√2. Surds add only when the simplified root is the same: 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2, but √2 + √3 cannot be combined.

Example 10 · Surds

(a) √50 = √(25 × 2) = 5√2.

(b) Rationalise 1/√3: multiply the top and bottom by √3 to get √3/3.

(c) Rationalise 2/(2 + √3): multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate (2 − √3). The bottom becomes (2 + √3)(2 − √3) = 4 − 3 = 1, so the result is 2(2 − √3) = 4 − 2√3.

7.Linear Equations and Inequalities

To solve an equation, keep it balanced: whatever you do to one side, do to the other. The full method, with brackets, fractions and special cases, is in our algebraic equations guide.

Example 11 · Linear equations

(a) 5x − 7 = 18. Add 7: 5x = 25. Divide by 5: x = 5.

(b) x/3 + 2 = 7. Subtract 2: x/3 = 5. Multiply by 3: x = 15.

Inequalities follow the same steps with one critical exception: when you multiply or divide by a negative number, reverse the inequality sign.

Example 12 · Inequalities

(a) −2x > 8. Divide by −2 and reverse the sign: x < −4.

(b) 3 − 2x < 9. Subtract 3: −2x < 6. Divide by −2 and reverse: x > −3. Check x = 0: 3 − 0 = 3, which is less than 9. ✓

(c) 2 < x + 3 ≤ 7. Subtract 3 from every part: −1 < x ≤ 4.

“−2x > 8, so x > −4.”

Test x = −3 and x = −5 in the original inequality.

The fix: x = −5 works (−2 × −5 = 10 > 8) but x = −3 does not (−2 × −3 = 6, which is not above 8). So the solutions are x < −4. Dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality sign.

8.Simultaneous Equations

Two equations with two unknowns are solved together by elimination, substitution or a graph, where the crossing point gives the solution.

Example 13 · Elimination

Solve 2x + y = 11 and 3x − y = 9. Add the equations: 5x = 20, so x = 4. Substitute: 8 + y = 11, so y = 3. Check: 3(4) − 3 = 9. ✓

Example 14 · Substitution

Solve 4x + 3y = 17 and y = 2x − 1. Substitute y: 4x + 3(2x − 1) = 17, so 10x − 3 = 17 and x = 2. Then y = 2(2) − 1 = 3. Check: 4(2) + 3(3) = 17. ✓

9.Quadratic Equations

A quadratic equation has the form ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0. It can have two, one or no real solutions. When it factorises, use the zero-product rule: if AB = 0, then A = 0 or B = 0.

Example 15 · Factorising

x² − 5x + 6 = 0 gives (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0, so x = 2 or x = 3.

When a quadratic does not factorise easily, use the quadratic formula:

x = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)] ÷ 2a
Example 16 · The formula

For 2x² + 3x − 2 = 0: a = 2, b = 3, c = −2. Then b² − 4ac = 9 + 16 = 25 and x = (−3 ± 5) ÷ 4. So x = 1/2 or x = −2.

Completing the square rewrites a quadratic as a squared bracket plus a number. It shows the turning point and can solve equations.

Example 17 · Completing the square

x² + 6x + 5 = (x + 3)² − 9 + 5 = (x + 3)² − 4. Since a square is never negative, the lowest value of the expression is −4, at x = −3. To solve x² + 6x + 5 = 0: (x + 3)² = 4, so x + 3 = ±2 and x = −1 or x = −5.

Figure 3. The graph of y = x² + 6x + 5 meets the x-axis at −5 and −1, and has its lowest point at (−3, −4), matching (x + 3)² − 4.

The part under the square root, b² − 4ac, is the discriminant. If it is positive there are two different real roots, if it is zero there is one repeated root, and if it is negative there are no real roots.

Example 18 · The discriminant

(a) Show that 2x² − 3x + 5 = 0 has no real roots: b² − 4ac = 9 − 40 = −31, which is negative.

(b) Find k so that x² + kx + 4 = 0 has a repeated root: k² − 16 = 0, so k = 4 or k = −4. For example, k = 4 gives x² + 4x + 4 = (x + 2)².

10.Changing the Subject of a Formula

Rearranging a formula uses the same balance rules as solving an equation.

Example 19 · Rearranging

(a) A = πr². Divide by π: A/π = r². Take the positive square root: r = √(A/π).

(b) v = u + at. Subtract u: v − u = at. Divide by a: t = (v − u)/a.

(c) y = (x + 1)/(x − 2), where x appears twice. Multiply by (x − 2): y(x − 2) = x + 1, so yx − 2y = x + 1. Collect the x terms: yx − x = 2y + 1, so x(y − 1) = 2y + 1 and x = (2y + 1)/(y − 1). Check with x = 3: y = 4/1 = 4, and (2 × 4 + 1)/(4 − 1) = 3. ✓

11.Sequences and Series

A sequence is an ordered list that follows a rule. In an arithmetic sequence the difference between terms is constant, so the nth term is a + (n − 1)d, where a is the first term and d the common difference.

Example 20 · An arithmetic sequence

5, 8, 11, 14, … has d = 3 and first term 5, so the nth term is 5 + 3(n − 1) = 3n + 2. The 20th term is 3(20) + 2 = 62.

The sum of the first n terms is Sₙ = n/2 × [2a + (n − 1)d]. For 20 terms: S₂₀ = 10 × (10 + 57) = 670. Equivalently, 20/2 × (first + last) = 10 × (5 + 62) = 670.

In a quadratic sequence the second differences are constant, and if the second difference is 2a, the nth term starts with an².

Example 21 · A quadratic sequence

2, 7, 14, 23, … has first differences 5, 7, 9 and constant second difference 2, so the nth term starts with n². Subtract n² (1, 4, 9, 16) from the terms: 1, 3, 5, 7, which is 2n − 1. So the nth term is n² + 2n − 1. Check n = 4: 16 + 8 − 1 = 23. ✓

In a geometric sequence each term is multiplied by a constant ratio r, so the nth term is a × rⁿ⁻¹.

Example 22 · A geometric sequence

3, 6, 12, 24, … has a = 3 and r = 2. The nth term is 3 × 2ⁿ⁻¹, so the 10th term is 3 × 512 = 1536. The sum of n terms is a(rⁿ − 1)/(r − 1), so the sum of the first 10 terms is 3(1024 − 1) = 3069.

12.Straight-Line Graphs

The equation y = mx + c describes a straight line with gradient m and y-intercept c. The gradient between two points is m = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁). Parallel lines have the same gradient. Perpendicular lines have gradients whose product is −1.

Figure 4. The line through (2, 5) and (6, 13) has a rise of 8 for a run of 4, so its gradient is 2.
Example 23 · The equation of a line

A line passes through (2, 5) and (6, 13). The gradient is (13 − 5) ÷ (6 − 2) = 8 ÷ 4 = 2. Put the point (2, 5) into y = 2x + c: 5 = 4 + c, so c = 1. The line is y = 2x + 1. Check the other point: 2(6) + 1 = 13. ✓

The midpoint of the two points is ((2 + 6) ÷ 2, (5 + 13) ÷ 2) = (4, 9). A line perpendicular to y = 2x + 1 has gradient −1/2, so y = −x/2 + 3 is perpendicular to it and crosses the y-axis at 3.

13.Functions

A function gives one output for each input. The notation f(x) means “the value of f when the input is x”, not f multiplied by x. A composite function fg(x) means f(g(x)): apply g first, then f. An inverse function f⁻¹ undoes f.

Figure 5. The function f(x) = 3x − 4 as a machine, and its inverse, which undoes the steps in reverse order.
Example 24 · Function notation

If f(x) = 3x − 4, then f(5) = 3(5) − 4 = 11. To solve f(x) = 8, set 3x − 4 = 8 so x = 4.

To find the inverse, write y = 3x − 4, swap x and y to get x = 3y − 4, and solve for y: f⁻¹(x) = (x + 4)/3. Check: f⁻¹(11) = 15 ÷ 3 = 5, the original input. ✓

Example 25 · Composite functions

Let f(x) = 2x + 1 and g(x) = x². Then fg(3) = f(g(3)) = f(9) = 19, but gf(3) = g(f(3)) = g(7) = 49. The order matters.

“If f(x) = 2x + 1, then f(3) + f(2) = f(5).”

Work out each side.

The fix: f(3) = 7 and f(2) = 5, so f(3) + f(2) = 12. But f(5) = 11. Function notation is not multiplication, so f(a) + f(b) is not f(a + b) in general.

14.Algebraic Fractions

Algebraic fractions follow the rules of numerical fractions. Factorise first, then cancel factors, never separate terms joined by + or −. Note any values that make a denominator zero.

Example 26 · Algebraic fractions

(a) Simplify (x² − 9)/(x² + 5x + 6) = [(x − 3)(x + 3)] / [(x + 2)(x + 3)] = (x − 3)/(x + 2), with x ≠ −3 and x ≠ −2.

(b) 1/x + 1/(x + 2) = [(x + 2) + x] / [x(x + 2)] = (2x + 2)/[x(x + 2)], with x ≠ 0 and x ≠ −2.

(c) Solve 3/(x − 1) = 2/(x + 1). Cross-multiply: 3(x + 1) = 2(x − 1), so 3x + 3 = 2x − 2 and x = −5. Check: 3/(−6) = −0.5 and 2/(−4) = −0.5. ✓

“(x + 2)/x = 2, because the x cancels.”

Try x = 4.

The fix: with x = 4, (4 + 2)/4 = 1.5, not 2. You can cancel only a factor of the whole top and the whole bottom. The x in x + 2 is a term, not a factor, so (x + 2)/x cannot be simplified this way. By contrast, (2x + 4)/(x + 2) = 2(x + 2)/(x + 2) = 2, because (x + 2) is a factor of the whole top.

15.Variation

If y is directly proportional to x, then y = kx. If y is inversely proportional to x, then y = k/x. Powers can appear, such as y = kx². Find k from one pair of values, then use the formula.

Example 27 · Variation

(a) y ∝ x² and y = 20 when x = 2. Then 20 = k(4), so k = 5 and y = 5x². When x = 3, y = 45.

(b) y is inversely proportional to x, and y = 6 when x = 4. Then k = 6 × 4 = 24, so y = 24/x. When x = 8, y = 3.

16.Polynomials: Remainder and Factor Theorems

A polynomial such as x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6 has whole-number powers of x. The remainder theorem says that when a polynomial f(x) is divided by (x − a), the remainder is f(a). The factor theorem says that (x − a) is a factor exactly when f(a) = 0.

Example 28 · Remainder and factor theorems

(a) The remainder when x² + 3x + 1 is divided by (x − 2) is f(2) = 4 + 6 + 1 = 11.

(b) Factorise f(x) = x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6. Try f(1) = 1 − 2 − 5 + 6 = 0, so (x − 1) is a factor. Dividing gives x² − x − 6 = (x − 3)(x + 2). So f(x) = (x − 1)(x − 3)(x + 2), and the roots are 1, 3 and −2. Check: f(3) = 27 − 18 − 15 + 6 = 0 and f(−2) = −8 − 8 + 10 + 6 = 0. ✓

17.Modelling and Problem Solving

Many problems hide an equation. Define the variables, translate each condition, solve, and then interpret the answer. A correct solution to the equation can still be wrong for the situation, for example a negative length.

Example 29 · A garden

A rectangular garden is x m wide and (x + 4) m long, with area 60 m². Then x(x + 4) = 60, so x² + 4x − 60 = 0 and (x + 10)(x − 6) = 0. So x = −10 or x = 6. A width cannot be negative, so x = 6. The garden is 6 m by 10 m, and 6 × 10 = 60. ✓

  1. Define each variable and say what it stands for.
  2. Translate the words into equations, one condition at a time.
  3. Choose the simplest method: factorise before using the formula, substitute before eliminating.
  4. Keep fractions and surds exact until the final step.
  5. Check restrictions, such as denominators that cannot be zero.
  6. Check the answer in the original problem and state it with its unit.
Example 30 · Choosing a method

Solve x² − 4x − 5 = 0. It factorises: (x − 5)(x + 1) = 0, so x = 5 or x = −1. The formula would give the same answer (b² − 4ac = 16 + 20 = 36), but factorising is quicker. If the factors are not obvious, check the discriminant first: if it is not a perfect square, the roots are not rational and the formula is the right tool.

The progression is deliberate: language, then manipulation, then equations, then functions and graphs, then modelling. If the early skills are secure, advanced questions become combinations of familiar ideas.

19.Common Algebra Mistakes

  • Dropping a negative sign: −(2x − 3) = −2x + 3, not −2x − 3.
  • Combining unlike terms: 3x + 2x² cannot become 5x².
  • Incomplete distribution: 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6, not 2x + 3.
  • Wrong index law: x² × x³ = x⁵, not x⁶.
  • Squaring a sum term by term: (x + 3)² is not x² + 9.
  • Forgetting to reverse an inequality when dividing by a negative number.
  • Cancelling terms instead of factors in an algebraic fraction.
  • Losing a quadratic solution, or dividing both sides by an expression that could be zero.
  • Treating f(a + b) as f(a) + f(b).
  • Ignoring excluded values, or leaving a negative length in a real-life answer.
  • Rounding too early, or giving an answer with no explanation or unit.

20.Try It Yourself

  1. Expand and simplify (x − 3)².
  2. Solve x/2 − 3 = 5.
  3. Find the nth term of the sequence 12, 9, 6, 3, …
Show answers

1. (x − 3)(x − 3) = x² − 3x − 3x + 9 = x² − 6x + 9.

2. Add 3: x/2 = 8. Multiply by 2: x = 16.

3. The common difference is −3, so the nth term starts with −3n. When n = 1 the term is 12, so we add 15: −3n + 15 (or 15 − 3n).

21.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [4 marks] (a) Expand and simplify (2x − 1)(x + 5). (b) Factorise 6x² + 9x.
  2. [4 marks] (a) Solve 3(x − 2) ≤ 2x + 5. (b) Solve 2 < x + 3 ≤ 7.
  3. [5 marks] (a) Solve 2x² + 3x − 2 = 0. (b) Write x² + 6x + 5 in the form (x + p)² + q and state the minimum value of x² + 6x + 5.
  4. [4 marks] Solve the simultaneous equations 4x + 3y = 17 and 2x − y = 1.
  5. [4 marks] A sequence begins 5, 9, 13, 17, … (a) Find the nth term. (b) Find the 50th term. (c) Is 100 a term of the sequence? Explain.
  6. [5 marks] f(x) = 2x − 3 and g(x) = x² + 1. Find (a) f(4), (b) fg(x), (c) f⁻¹(x).
  7. [4 marks] y is inversely proportional to x, and y = 6 when x = 4. (a) Find a formula for y in terms of x. (b) Find y when x = 8. (c) Find x when y = 2.
Show answers

1. (a) 2x² + 10x − x − 5 = 2x² + 9x − 5. (b) 3x(2x + 3).

2. (a) 3x − 6 ≤ 2x + 5, so x ≤ 11. Check x = 11: 27 ≤ 27. ✓ (b) Subtract 3 from every part: −1 < x ≤ 4.

3. (a) (2x − 1)(x + 2) = 0, so x = 1/2 or x = −2. (b) x² + 6x + 5 = (x + 3)² − 9 + 5 = (x + 3)² − 4. The minimum value is −4, at x = −3.

4. From 2x − y = 1, y = 2x − 1. Substitute: 4x + 3(2x − 1) = 17, so 10x − 3 = 17 and x = 2. Then y = 3. Check: 8 + 9 = 17 and 4 − 3 = 1.

5. (a) The difference is 4 and the first term is 5, so the nth term is 4n + 1. (b) 4(50) + 1 = 201. (c) 4n + 1 = 100 gives n = 24.75, which is not a whole number, so 100 is not a term.

6. (a) f(4) = 8 − 3 = 5. (b) fg(x) = f(x² + 1) = 2(x² + 1) − 3 = 2x² − 1. (c) y = 2x − 3, swap: x = 2y − 3, so y = (x + 3)/2. f⁻¹(x) = (x + 3)/2.

7. (a) y = k/x and k = 6 × 4 = 24, so y = 24/x. (b) y = 24/8 = 3. (c) 2 = 24/x, so x = 12.

22.Key Points

  • Only like terms combine, and every term in a bracket is multiplied when you expand.
  • (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b², and a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b).
  • Factorise with a common factor first, then use the product and sum method or split the middle term.
  • Reverse an inequality sign when you multiply or divide by a negative number.
  • Quadratics: factorise, complete the square or use the formula. The discriminant b² − 4ac shows how many real roots there are.
  • Arithmetic nth term: a + (n − 1)d. Geometric nth term: arⁿ⁻¹. Quadratic sequences have constant second differences.
  • Gradient = rise ÷ run, and y = mx + c.
  • Composite functions depend on order, and the inverse undoes a function in reverse order.
  • Cancel factors, never terms. By the factor theorem, (x − a) is a factor when f(a) = 0.
  • Check every answer in the original problem, and state it with its unit.

23.For Teachers and Parents

Algebra is easier when learners see what the symbols mean. Use area models, like the one for (x + 4)(x + 3), to show why expanding and factorising are opposites. Ask learners to test an algebraic claim with a number before they trust it: if (x + 3)² = x² + 9, try x = 2. Use function machines for equations and inverse functions, and ask learners to describe each step in words. The Quadratic Explorer, Straight-Line Calculator and Sequence Calculator on this page let learners check their own working, but they should always show the algebra first. Encourage a final check by substitution every time.
