Weather & Climate: How the Atmosphere Works

"Weather" and "climate" get used interchangeably, but they describe two very different timescales. This article explains what drives day-to-day weather, how clouds and precipitation form, and why some regions of the world stay hot, cold, wet, or dry year after year.

Ask what the weather is like today and the answer may change by tomorrow. Ask what the climate of a region is like and the answer stays much the same for decades. Both come from the same thin layer of gas wrapped around our planet, the atmosphere, but they describe it on very different timescales. This article explains what the atmosphere is made of, what drives wind, clouds and storms, why regions have different climates, and how human activity is changing the climate.

In this article

1.Weather vs Climate: What Is the Difference?

Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time: today's temperature, whether it is raining, how strong the wind is. It can change within hours. Climate is the average pattern of weather in a region over a long period, usually 30 years or more. A desert can have a rare rainy day (weather) and still have a dry climate.

A simple way to remember

Climate is what you expect; weather is what you get. You expect a cold, snowy climate in the Himalayas in January, but the weather on one particular day could still be clear and sunny.

This difference matters. One unusually cold week does not change a region's climate, and one hot day does not prove the climate is warming. Climate is judged from long records, not single days.

2.The Atmosphere: Earth's Layered Blanket

The atmosphere is the layer of gases held around Earth by gravity. Dry air is about 78% nitrogen, about 21% oxygen, about 0.9% argon and about 0.04% carbon dioxide, with tiny amounts of other gases. It also holds a variable amount of water vapour, which is the invisible gas form of water, and this is the part most important for weather.

The atmosphere is divided into layers according to how temperature changes with height.

Figure 1. Layers of the atmosphere (not to scale). The red line shows how temperature changes with height: it falls, rises, falls, then rises again.
LayerApprox. heightKey features
Troposphere0 to about 12 kmNearly all weather and clouds; temperature falls as you go up
Stratosphereabout 12 to 50 kmContains the ozone layer, which absorbs much harmful ultraviolet (UV) light
Mesosphereabout 50 to 85 kmColdest layer; most meteors burn up here
Thermosphere and exosphereabove about 85 kmExtremely thin air; auroras occur here, and many satellites orbit here

The heights are approximate. The troposphere is thinner near the poles (roughly 8 km) and thicker near the equator (roughly 16 to 18 km). For comparison, Mount Everest is about 8.8 km high, so the whole mountain lies inside the troposphere.

3.Sunlight, Seasons and Temperature

Almost all weather is powered by the Sun. But the Sun does not heat the Earth evenly. Near the equator, sunlight arrives almost straight down and is concentrated on a small area. Near the poles, the same sunlight arrives at a slant and is spread over a larger area, so each square metre receives less heat.

Figure 2. The same beam of sunlight covers a small area at the equator but a bigger area near the pole, so the pole is cooler.

Seasons are caused by tilt, not distance. Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.5 degrees. When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, it has summer and longer days; six months later it is tilted away and has winter. In fact, Earth is slightly closer to the Sun in early January, in the middle of the northern winter, so distance cannot be the cause of the seasons.

Land also heats and cools faster than water, because water needs much more heat energy to warm up. This is why inland places usually have bigger temperature swings than coastal places.

4.Air Pressure and Wind

Air pressure is the weight of the air pressing down on a surface. At sea level it averages about 1013 hectopascals (hPa), and it falls as you climb. Unequal heating creates differences in pressure, and air always flows from high pressure towards low pressure. That moving air is wind.

  • Warm air is less dense, so it rises. Rising air leaves lower pressure at the surface, so clouds and rain are more likely.
  • Cool air is denser, so it sinks. Sinking air gives higher pressure at the surface, so skies are usually clearer and calmer.

On a global scale, the spinning of the Earth bends the path of moving air. This is the Coriolis effect: winds are turned to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. Together with unequal heating, it produces the main wind belts: the trade winds in the tropics, the westerlies in the middle latitudes and the polar easterlies near the poles. Near the equator, the trade winds from both hemispheres meet in a belt of rising air with heavy rainfall.

The same idea works on a small scale. During a sunny day, land heats faster than the sea, the warm air above the land rises, and cooler air flows in from the sea. This is a sea breeze.

5.The Water Cycle and Clouds

Water moves continuously between the oceans, the air and the land. The Sun's heat makes water evaporate into vapour. When the moist air rises it cools, and the vapour condenses into tiny droplets or ice crystals around specks of dust or salt, forming clouds. When droplets grow too heavy, they fall as precipitation, and water flows back to the sea as runoff.

Figure 3. The water cycle. Watch the vapour rising and the rain falling.
Cloud typeAppearanceUsually signals
CumulusPuffy, cotton-likeFair weather, though it can build into storms
StratusFlat, grey, layeredOvercast skies, light drizzle
NimbostratusThick, dark grey layerSteady, continuous rain or snow
CirrusThin, wispy, high up, made of ice crystalsOften fair weather for now; can come before a change
CumulonimbusTall, dark, tower-shapedThunderstorms, heavy rain, hail

Dew and fog. Air can hold only so much water vapour, and cold air holds less than warm air. When air cools to its dew point, vapour condenses. On grass this makes dew; in the air near the ground it makes fog, which is simply a cloud at ground level.

Rain, snow, sleet and hail. If the air is warm all the way down, droplets fall as rain. If it is below freezing, they fall as snow. Hail forms inside powerful cumulonimbus clouds, where strong updrafts carry ice pellets up into freezing air again and again, adding a new layer of ice each time, like an onion, until the hailstone is too heavy to be held up.

6.Fronts, Highs and Lows

A large body of air with similar temperature and moisture is called an air mass. The boundary where two different air masses meet is a front.

  • Cold front: cold, dense air pushes under warm air, forcing it up quickly. This often brings towering clouds, short heavy showers or thunderstorms, and then cooler, clearer weather.
  • Warm front: warm air slides gradually over cooler air, often bringing layers of cloud and longer periods of steady rain.

Weather maps mark areas of high pressure (H) and low pressure (L). Around a low, air spirals inwards and upwards, which brings cloud and rain; in the Northern Hemisphere it circulates anticlockwise. Around a high, air sinks and spirals outwards, bringing settled weather; in the Northern Hemisphere it circulates clockwise. In the Southern Hemisphere these directions are reversed.

7.Storms and Monsoons

Thunderstorms grow from cumulonimbus clouds. Lightning is a huge electrical spark that heats the air so fast that it expands explosively, which we hear as thunder. Light reaches us almost instantly, but sound travels much more slowly, at about 343 metres per second in air. That gives a handy rule: every 3 seconds between a flash and its thunder is about 1 kilometre of distance.

Worked example 1: How far away is the lightning?

Question: You see a flash and hear thunder 9 seconds later. How far away was the lightning?

Step 1. Sound travels about 1 km in 3 seconds.

Step 2. Divide the delay by 3: 9 ÷ 3 = 3.

Answer: About 3 km away. (Check: 343 m/s × 9 s ≈ 3,087 m.) A short delay means the storm is close; go indoors.

Tropical cyclones are giant spinning storms with a centre of very low pressure. They form over warm ocean water, roughly 26.5 °C or warmer, and weaken when they move over land or colder water. They are called hurricanes in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific, typhoons in the north-western Pacific, and cyclones in the Indian Ocean. A tornado is a narrow, rotating column of air that reaches the ground from a thunderstorm.

Monsoons. A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of wind direction. In South Asia, the land heats strongly in summer, air above it rises, and moist winds are drawn in from the Indian Ocean and the Arabian Sea, bringing heavy rain roughly from June to September. In winter the winds blow the other way and are much drier. Monsoon rains are vital for farming, but unusually heavy ones can cause floods; in 2022, extreme monsoon rainfall led to devastating floods in Pakistan.

El Niño. Every few years, the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean becomes warmer than usual, an event called El Niño. It changes weather patterns in many parts of the world. It is a reminder that the ocean and atmosphere work as one system.

8.Climate Zones Around the World

Climate settles into recognisable patterns. A common system, first developed by the climatologist Wladimir Köppen in the late 1800s and early 1900s, groups climates into five main types:

GroupDescriptionExample regions
TropicalHot all year, often with heavy rainfallAmazon rainforest, Singapore
Dry (arid and semi-arid)Very low rainfall; hot deserts and cooler grassland steppesSahara, Thar Desert
TemperateMild, with distinct seasonsMuch of western Europe, parts of eastern China
ContinentalLarge swings between hot summers and cold wintersCentral Russia, the interior of North America
PolarCold all yearAntarctica, Greenland's ice sheet

Pakistan shows how much climate can vary inside one country: much of it is arid or semi-arid, the monsoon brings heavy summer rain to parts of the east and north, and the high Karakoram and Himalayan ranges in the north are cold, with large glaciers.

9.What Shapes a Region's Climate?

  • Latitude. Places nearer the equator get more direct sunlight all year than places near the poles.
  • Altitude. Temperature falls with height, by roughly 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m on average, which is why mountain peaks near the equator can be snow-capped.
  • Distance from the sea. Water warms and cools slowly, so coastal areas have milder, steadier temperatures than inland areas.
  • Ocean currents. A warm current such as the Gulf Stream helps keep north-western Europe milder than other places at the same latitude.
  • Prevailing winds and mountains. Winds carry moisture. When moist air is forced up a mountain it drops rain on the windward side, leaving a dry rain shadow on the other side.
Worked example 2: Temperature on a mountain

Question: A town at sea level has a temperature of 30 °C. Estimate the temperature at a village 2,000 m higher on the same day.

Step 1. Use the average rate: 6.5 °C fall per 1,000 m.

Step 2. For 2,000 m, the fall is 6.5 × 2 = 13 °C.

Step 3. 30 − 13 = 17 °C.

Answer: About 17 °C. This is an estimate; real values depend on weather and local conditions.

Worked example 3: Which climate group?

Data (example numbers): Station A: average temperature 27 °C all year, about 2,500 mm of rain per year. Station B: average 22 °C, about 100 mm of rain per year.

Step 1. Look at rainfall first. B has very low rainfall.

Step 2. B is therefore in the dry group.

Step 3. A is hot all year with heavy rainfall, which fits the tropical group.

Answer: A is tropical; B is dry.

10.The Greenhouse Effect and Climate Change

Earth is kept warm by the greenhouse effect. Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the surface. The surface gives off heat as infrared radiation, and some of that is absorbed and sent back down by greenhouse gases such as water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane. Without this natural effect, Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about 15 °C, and life as we know it would be very difficult.

Figure 4. The greenhouse effect. Some outgoing heat is absorbed by greenhouse gases and sent back towards the surface.

The concern today is the enhanced greenhouse effect. Burning coal, oil and gas, and clearing forests, have raised the amount of carbon dioxide in the air from about 280 parts per million before the industrial era to above 420 parts per million. Major scientific assessments, including those by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), conclude that human activities are the main cause of the warming observed since the mid-20th century. Global average temperature has risen by about 1.1 °C compared with 1850 to 1900, and effects include rising sea levels, melting glaciers and ice sheets, and more frequent heat extremes in many regions.

Climate change is a different problem from the ozone layer issue. Chemicals called CFCs damaged the ozone layer, and an international agreement (the Montreal Protocol, 1987) phased them out, so the ozone layer is slowly recovering. People often mix the two up. Climate scientists also remind us that climate projections describe long-term trends and ranges, not exact weather on a particular day.

11.How Forecasts Are Made

Forecasters collect measurements from weather stations, balloons, radar, ships and satellites. Powerful computers use physics equations to calculate how the atmosphere will change. Because the atmosphere is complex, forecasts become less certain the further ahead they look, and they are often given as probabilities. A "70% chance of rain" tells you how likely rain is, not how heavy or long it will be.

Worked example 4: Find the average

Question (example numbers): The highest temperatures on five days were 31, 33, 30, 34 and 32 °C. What is the average? Is this weather or climate?

Step 1. Add: 31 + 33 + 30 + 34 + 32 = 160.

Step 2. Divide by 5: 160 ÷ 5 = 32.

Answer: 32 °C. This describes five days of weather. Climate would need averages from many years, usually 30 or more.

13.Common Mistakes and Myths

  • "Weather and climate are the same." No. Weather is short-term; climate is the long-term average.
  • "Summer happens because Earth is closer to the Sun." No. The tilt of the Earth's axis causes the seasons.
  • "It is colder up high because you are farther from the Sun." The extra distance is tiny. The air is cooler higher up because thinner air holds less heat and is mostly warmed from the surface below.
  • "A cold winter disproves climate change." One cold spell is weather, and climate is judged from long-term averages.
  • "The ozone hole causes global warming." These are separate issues, although both involve human-made gases.
  • "Clouds are smoke." Clouds are made of tiny water droplets or ice crystals.

14.Summary

  • Weather is short-term; climate is the long-term average, usually over 30 years or more.
  • Nearly all weather happens in the troposphere.
  • Uneven heating from the Sun creates pressure differences, which create wind.
  • Rising moist air cools, condenses and forms clouds; the water cycle keeps water moving.
  • Latitude, altitude, distance from the sea, ocean currents and winds shape climate.
  • The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth habitable; extra greenhouse gases from human activity are warming the climate.

15.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. What is the difference between weather and climate?

Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a place and time. Climate is the average weather over a long period, usually 30 years or more.

2. In which layer of the atmosphere does nearly all weather happen?

The troposphere.

3. What causes the seasons?

The tilt of Earth's axis (about 23.5 degrees), not the distance from the Sun.

4. Why does air move from high pressure to low pressure, and what do we call this?

Air flows to even out pressure differences. This moving air is wind.

5. You hear thunder 12 seconds after a flash. About how far away is the storm?

12 ÷ 3 = about 4 km.

6. What is the difference between the greenhouse effect and the enhanced greenhouse effect?

The greenhouse effect is the natural warming that keeps Earth habitable. The enhanced greenhouse effect is extra warming caused by additional greenhouse gases from human activity.

For Teachers and Parents

Suggested activities: keep a two-week weather diary (temperature, cloud type, wind, rain) and calculate averages; build a simple rain gauge from a cut plastic bottle; make a "cloud in a jar" with warm water, ice and a little smoke or hairspray to show condensation. Discussion prompts: How is a weather forecast different from a climate prediction? Why can a hot day not prove that the climate is changing? The facts here follow widely accepted science, and numbers are given as approximate values. This article is general education for students up to age 18.
