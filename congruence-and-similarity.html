Congruence & Similarity

Learn the four congruence tests and three similarity tests for triangles, how to prove geometric facts, how scale factors change lengths, areas and volumes, and how to use similar triangles to find lengths you cannot measure.

Two shapes can be related in two important ways. They can be congruent: exactly the same shape and exactly the same size, so one fits perfectly on the other. Or they can be similar: exactly the same shape, but one is an enlargement or reduction of the other. These two ideas let us prove geometric facts without measuring, find lengths we cannot measure directly (the height of a tree, the width of a river), and understand how area and volume change when a shape is scaled. This guide explains every test, shows why each one works, and ends with the scale-factor rules for lengths, areas and volumes.

1.What Congruence Means

Two shapes are congruent if you can move one onto the other exactly, using only a slide (translation), a turn (rotation) and a flip (reflection), with no change of size. Congruent shapes have equal corresponding sides and equal corresponding angles. We write △ABC ≡ △PQR, and the order of the letters matters: A matches P, B matches Q and C matches R. So AB = PQ, BC = QR, and angle B = angle Q, and so on.

To prove two triangles congruent you do not need to check all six measurements. Four short tests are enough.

2.The Four Congruence Tests

Figure 1. Matching tick marks show equal sides, and matching arcs show equal angles. Each panel shows the information that is enough to prove congruence.
TestWhat must be equalWatch out
SSSAll three pairs of sidesNothing else is needed
SASTwo pairs of sides and the angle between themThe angle must be included between the two sides
ASA or AASTwo pairs of angles and one pair of sidesWith two angles, the third is also known, so the side can be anywhere
RHSA right angle, the hypotenuse and one other sideRight-angled triangles only

Why SSA is not a test

Two sides and an angle that is not between them (SSA) does not always fix the triangle. In the sine-rule article we met angle A = 30°, a = 5 cm and b = 8 cm, which gives two different triangles, with third sides 9.93 cm and 3.93 cm. They have the same SSA data but are not congruent. SSA does work in two situations: when the angle is a right angle (this is RHS), and when the given angle is opposite the longer of the two given sides. Because it is easy to get wrong, SSA is not listed as a test at this level. Likewise, AAA (three equal angles) is never a congruence test. It proves only similarity.

Example 1 · Choosing the test

(a) AB = 7 cm, BC = 9 cm and angle B = 62° in triangle ABC. DE = 7 cm, EF = 9 cm and angle E = 62° in triangle DEF. The angle is between the equal sides, so the triangles are congruent by SAS, and △ABC ≡ △DEF.

(b) Triangle PQR has P = 50°, Q = 70° and QR = 6 cm. Triangle XYZ has X = 50°, Z = 60° and YZ = 6 cm. In XYZ the third angle is Y = 180 − 50 − 60 = 70°. So P = X and Q = Y, and QR (opposite P) = YZ (opposite X). Two angles and a corresponding side are equal, so the triangles are congruent by AAS, and △PQR ≡ △XYZ.

(c) Two right-angled triangles have hypotenuses of 13 cm and one side of 5 cm each. They are congruent by RHS. (The third sides are both 12 cm.)

“Both triangles have angles of 40°, 60° and 80°, so they are congruent.”

Do equal angles fix the size?

The fix: three equal angles (AAA) say nothing about size. A small triangle and a huge one can have the same angles. They are similar, not necessarily congruent. For congruence, at least one pair of corresponding sides must also be equal.

3.Using Congruence to Prove Facts

Congruence is the standard tool for proof. Write each step with a statement and a reason, and finish by naming the test.

Example 2 · Diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other

In parallelogram ABCD the diagonals AC and BD meet at O. Compare triangles AOB and COD.

Angle OAB = angle OCD (alternate angles, AB ∥ DC). Angle OBA = angle ODC (alternate angles, AB ∥ DC). AB = DC (opposite sides of a parallelogram). So △AOB ≡ △COD (ASA: two angles and the side between them). Therefore AO = CO and BO = DO, so the diagonals bisect each other.

Example 3 · The base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal

In triangle ABC, AB = AC. Draw AD bisecting angle A, with D on BC. Compare triangles ABD and ACD.

AB = AC (given). Angle BAD = angle CAD (AD bisects angle A). AD is common to both. So △ABD ≡ △ACD (SAS). Therefore angle B = angle C, which proves the base angles are equal.

Example 4 · A point on a perpendicular bisector is equidistant from the ends

M is the midpoint of AB, and PM is perpendicular to AB. Compare triangles PMA and PMB.

AM = BM (M is the midpoint). Angle PMA = angle PMB = 90° (given). PM is common. So △PMA ≡ △PMB (SAS), and PA = PB. This is why the perpendicular bisector in the constructions article is the locus of points equidistant from two points.

“△ABC ≡ △QPR, because the triangles are the same.” (but A matches P and B matches Q)

Look at the order of the letters.

The fix: in a congruence statement the letters must be in matching order. If A matches P, B matches Q and C matches R, write △ABC ≡ △PQR. Writing △QPR swaps the vertices, so a reader would think A matches Q. Always list corresponding vertices in the same position.

4.What Similarity Means

Two shapes are similar if one is an enlargement (or reduction) of the other. This needs two things:

  • all corresponding angles are equal, and
  • all corresponding sides are in the same ratio. That ratio is the scale factor, k.

Congruent shapes are the special case where k = 1. For triangles either condition alone is enough, because each forces the other. For other shapes both are needed. A 2 cm by 4 cm rectangle and a 2 cm by 5 cm rectangle have all angles equal (90°) but are not similar, since 2 ÷ 2 = 1 and 5 ÷ 4 = 1.25. A square and a rhombus have all sides in the same ratio but the angles can differ. On the other hand, all circles are similar to each other, and so are all squares.

5.The Similarity Tests for Triangles

TestWhat must match
AATwo pairs of angles are equal (the third pair then must be equal too)
SAS (similarity)Two pairs of sides are in the same ratio, and the angle between them is equal
SSS (similarity)All three pairs of sides are in the same ratio
Figure 2. Triangle ABC is enlarged by scale factor 2.5 to give triangle DEF. The red angles are equal, the blue angles are equal, and 15 ÷ 6 = 20 ÷ 8 = 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5.
Example 5 · Proving similarity

(a) AA. Triangle PQR has P = 55° and Q = 70°. Triangle XYZ has X = 55° and Y = 70°. Two pairs of angles are equal, so the triangles are similar. (The third angles are both 180 − 55 − 70 = 55°.)

(b) SSS. Triangle ABC has sides 3, 4 and 5. Triangle DEF has sides 9, 12 and 15. Compare in order: 9 ÷ 3 = 3, 12 ÷ 4 = 3, 15 ÷ 5 = 3. All ratios are equal, so the triangles are similar with k = 3. Always pair the shortest side with the shortest, and the longest with the longest.

(c) Not similar. Triangles with sides 3, 4, 5 and 6, 8, 11: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 and 8 ÷ 4 = 2, but 11 ÷ 5 = 2.2. The ratios differ, so these are not similar.

6.Scale Factor and Missing Lengths

The scale factor is (new length) ÷ (original length) for any pair of matching sides. A factor above 1 enlarges, and a factor between 0 and 1 reduces. Once you know it, every other length follows. Perimeters scale by k as well, because the perimeter is a sum of lengths.

Example 6 · A missing length

Triangles ABC and DEF are similar, with AB = 6 cm matching DE = 15 cm. If BC = 8 cm, find EF, and the perimeter ratio.

k = 15 ÷ 6 = 2.5. EF = 8 × 2.5 = 20 cm. If the perimeter of ABC is 24 cm, the perimeter of DEF is 24 × 2.5 = 60 cm.

You can also use a ratio equation: AB ÷ DE = BC ÷ EF, so 6 ÷ 15 = 8 ÷ EF and EF = 8 × 15 ÷ 6 = 20 cm. Both methods give the same answer. Check each time that you have matched the correct sides (the sides opposite equal angles).

7.A Line Parallel to One Side of a Triangle

When a line DE is drawn parallel to the side BC of triangle ABC, with D on AB and E on AC, the angles at D and B are corresponding angles, and so are the angles at E and C. Angle A is shared. So triangle ADE is similar to triangle ABC (AA).

Figure 3. DE is parallel to BC, so △ADE ~ △ABC and AD ÷ AB = AE ÷ AC. (The arrows show the parallel lines.)
Example 7 · EC from a parallel line

AD = 4 cm, DB = 6 cm and AE = 5 cm. Find EC.

AB = 4 + 6 = 10. By similarity AD ÷ AB = AE ÷ AC, so 4 ÷ 10 = 5 ÷ AC and AC = 5 × 10 ÷ 4 = 12.5 cm. Then EC = 12.5 − 5 = 7.5 cm.

Shortcut: the parallel line cuts both sides in the same ratio: AD ÷ DB = AE ÷ EC, so 4 ÷ 6 = 5 ÷ EC, and EC = 7.5 cm.

Example 8 · Midpoints, and crossing lines

(a) If D and E are the midpoints of AB and AC, then k = 2, so DE = ½ BC and DE ∥ BC. If BC = 14 cm, then DE = 7 cm.

(b) Lines AC and BD cross at O, with AB parallel to CD. Triangles AOB and COD are similar (vertically opposite angles, and alternate angles). If AO = 6 cm, OC = 9 cm and AB = 8 cm, then AO ÷ OC = AB ÷ CD, so 6 ÷ 9 = 8 ÷ CD, giving CD = 12 cm.

“AD ÷ DB = AE ÷ AC, because the triangles are similar.”

Are the sides on each side of the equation matched?

The fix: that mixes a part (DB) with a whole (AC). Match like with like: part ÷ whole on both sides (AD ÷ AB = AE ÷ AC), or part ÷ part on both sides (AD ÷ DB = AE ÷ EC). To check, ask whether each fraction compares a small triangle’s side with the matching side of the big triangle.

8.Areas and Volumes of Similar Shapes

If the lengths of two similar shapes are in the ratio 1 : k, then:

  • their perimeters (and all lengths) are in the ratio 1 : k,
  • their areas are in the ratio 1 : k²,
  • their volumes (for similar solids) are in the ratio 1 : k³.
Figure 4. Doubling the side gives 4 times the area (a 2 × 2 square contains 4 unit squares). Tripling it gives 9 times the area, and 27 times the volume for cubes.

The reason: area involves two lengths multiplied together, so each length scales by k and the product by k × k. Volume involves three lengths, giving k × k × k.

Example 9 · Using k², k³

(a) Two similar cylinders have heights 4 cm and 10 cm, so k = 2.5. Their areas are in the ratio 2.5² = 6.25 and their volumes in the ratio 2.5³ = 15.625. If the small one holds 50 cm³, the big one holds 50 × 15.625 = 781.25 cm³.

(b) Two similar triangles have areas of 18 cm² and 50 cm². The area ratio is 18 : 50 = 9 : 25, so the length ratio is √9 : √25 = 3 : 5, and k = 5 ÷ 3. A 6 cm side on the smaller triangle matches 6 × 5 ÷ 3 = 10 cm on the larger.

(c) Two similar solids have volumes of 8 cm³ and 27 cm³. The length ratio is ∛8 : ∛27 = 2 : 3.

(d) Models. A model ship is 40 cm long and the real ship is 20 m = 2000 cm long, so k = 50. The real ship has 50² = 2500 times the surface area and 50³ = 125 000 times the volume.

(e) Maps. On a map of scale 1 : 50 000, 1 cm shows 500 m. So 1 cm² shows 500 m × 500 m = 250 000 m² = 0.25 km².

“The scale factor is 3, so the area of the big shape is 3 times as large.”

What happens to both length and width?

The fix: area scales by k², so a scale factor of 3 gives 9 times the area. A square of side 1 cm has area 1 cm², and a square of side 3 cm has area 9 cm². Use k for lengths, k² for areas and k³ for volumes. When you go the other way, from an area ratio to a length ratio, take the square root (and the cube root for volume).

9.Similar Triangles Inside a Right-Angled Triangle

Draw the perpendicular AD from the right-angled corner A to the hypotenuse BC. This splits the triangle into two smaller triangles, and all three triangles have the same angles, so they are similar.

Figure 5. In the right-angled triangle ABC (right angle at A), the altitude AD makes triangles ABD and ADC similar to ABC.
Example 10 · Lengths from the altitude

AB = 6 and AC = 8, so BC = √(36 + 64) = 10. Triangles ABD and ABC are similar (angle B is shared, and each has a right angle). So BD ÷ AB = AB ÷ BC, which gives BD = 6 × 6 ÷ 10 = 3.6. In the same way DC = 8 × 8 ÷ 10 = 6.4, and BD + DC = 10 as expected. For the height, AD ÷ AB = AC ÷ BC, so AD = 6 × 8 ÷ 10 = 4.8.

A proof of Pythagoras. From the similar triangles, AB² = BD × BC and AC² = DC × BC. Adding gives AB² + AC² = BC × (BD + DC) = BC × BC = BC². This is Pythagoras’ theorem, proved with similar triangles.

10.Using Similar Triangles in Real Life

  • Shadows. At one moment, sunlight reaches the ground at the same angle everywhere. A 1.5 m person casts a 2.4 m shadow, and a tree casts a 16 m shadow, so the tree’s height is 1.5 × 16 ÷ 2.4 = 10 m. Ancient sources report that the Greek thinker Thales used a method like this to find the height of a pyramid in Egypt. The accounts differ in detail and were written much later, so historians treat the story with care.
  • Maps and scale drawings. A scale of 1 : 50 000 is a scale factor, and areas must use its square.
  • Surveying. Distances across rivers or ravines are found from similar triangles in a measured layout.
  • Photography and projection. An enlarged print is similar to the original, and a projector uses the same idea.
Did You Know?

The rules k, k² and k³ explain why animals cannot simply be scaled up. If an animal grew 10 times larger in every direction, its weight (a volume) would grow 1000 times, but the strength of its bones and muscles (set by cross-section area) would only grow 100 times. Galileo discussed this problem in his book Two New Sciences (1638). That is why large animals have proportionally thicker legs than small ones.

11.A Short History

Euclid’s Elements (around 300 BCE) contains the first systematic proofs of the congruence tests. Proposition 4 of Book I proves SAS, Proposition 8 proves SSS and Proposition 26 proves ASA and AAS. Similar figures are treated in Book VI. Tradition links Thales of Miletus (around 600 BCE) with early work on similar triangles, but no writing of his survives, and what we know comes from later writers, so details are uncertain.

12.Common Mistakes

  • Using SSA or AAA as a congruence test.
  • Mismatching corresponding sides. Pair the side opposite each angle with the side opposite the equal angle.
  • Writing a statement with letters out of order. △ABC ≡ △PQR means A ↔ P, B ↔ Q, C ↔ R.
  • Applying k to area or volume. Use k² and k³.
  • Believing equal angles make any shape similar. This is true for triangles only.
  • Mixing parts and wholes in a ratio, such as AD ÷ DB = AE ÷ AC.
  • Forgetting units (cm, cm², cm³), or taking a square root of a volume ratio when you need a cube root.

13.Try It Yourself

  1. Are triangles with sides 2, 3, 4 and 5, 7.5, 10 similar? If so, what is the scale factor?
  2. Two similar shapes have areas in the ratio 4 : 9. What is the ratio of their lengths, and of their volumes (if they were solids)?
  3. A pole 3 m high casts a shadow 4.5 m long. A building casts a shadow 36 m long at the same time. How tall is the building?
Show answers

1. 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5, 7.5 ÷ 3 = 2.5, 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5. All ratios agree, so they are similar with scale factor 2.5.

2. Lengths: √4 : √9 = 2 : 3. Volumes: 2³ : 3³ = 8 : 27.

3. Scale factor 36 ÷ 4.5 = 8, so the building is 3 × 8 = 24 m tall.

14.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [3 marks] In triangles ABC and PQR, AB = PQ = 8 cm, BC = QR = 11 cm and angle B = angle Q = 40°. Name the test that shows they are congruent, write the congruence statement, and state which side equals AC.
  2. [4 marks] In triangle ABC, D is on AB and E is on AC with DE parallel to BC. AD = 5 cm, DB = 7.5 cm, DE = 6 cm and AE = 4 cm. Find BC and AC.
  3. [3 marks] Two similar triangles have areas of 18 cm² and 50 cm². A side of the smaller triangle is 6 cm. Find the matching side of the larger triangle.
  4. [3 marks] A model car is built to a scale of 1 : 20. The windscreen of the model has an area of 30 cm². Find the area of the real windscreen in m².
  5. [4 marks] Two similar solids have surface areas of 36 cm² and 100 cm². The smaller solid has volume 54 cm³. Find the volume of the larger solid.
  6. [4 marks] In triangle ABC the angle at A is 90°, AB = 6 cm and AC = 8 cm. The perpendicular from A meets BC at D. Find BC, BD, DC and AD.
  7. [4 marks] Prove that the diagonals of a rectangle are equal in length.
Show answers

1. Two sides and the included angle are equal, so the triangles are congruent by SAS. The statement is △ABC ≡ △PQR, and AC = PR.

2. AB = 5 + 7.5 = 12.5. The scale factor from ADE to ABC is 12.5 ÷ 5 = 2.5. So BC = 6 × 2.5 = 15 cm and AC = 4 × 2.5 = 10 cm (and EC = 6 cm).

3. Area ratio 18 : 50 = 9 : 25, so the length ratio is 3 : 5. The matching side is 6 × 5 ÷ 3 = 10 cm.

4. Length scale factor 20, so the area scale factor is 20² = 400. The real area is 30 × 400 = 12 000 cm² = 1.2 m² (divide by 10 000).

5. Area ratio 36 : 100, so k = √(100 ÷ 36) = 10 ÷ 6 = 5 ÷ 3. Volume factor k³ = 125 ÷ 27. Volume = 54 × 125 ÷ 27 = 250 cm³.

6. BC = √(6² + 8²) = 10 cm. BD = 6² ÷ 10 = 3.6 cm. DC = 8² ÷ 10 = 6.4 cm. AD = 6 × 8 ÷ 10 = 4.8 cm. (Check: BD + DC = 10, and AD² = 3.6 × 6.4 = 23.04 = 4.8².)

7. Let the rectangle be ABCD, with diagonals AC and BD. Compare triangles ABC and DCB. AB = DC (opposite sides of a rectangle are equal). Angle ABC = angle DCB = 90° (angles of a rectangle). BC is common. So △ABC ≡ △DCB (SAS), and therefore AC = DB. The diagonals are equal.

15.Key Points

  • Congruent shapes are identical in shape and size. Triangles are congruent by SSS, SAS, ASA or AAS, or RHS. SSA and AAA are not tests.
  • Write congruence statements with the letters in matching order.
  • Similar shapes have equal angles and sides in the same ratio. Triangles are similar by AA, SAS (ratio) or SSS (ratio).
  • Scale factor k = new length ÷ original length, for matching sides.
  • A line parallel to one side of a triangle makes a smaller similar triangle. It cuts the other two sides in the same ratio.
  • Lengths scale by k, areas by k², volumes by k³. To go back, use square and cube roots.
  • The altitude to the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle makes two triangles similar to the whole one.

16.For Teachers and Parents

The distinction between congruent and similar is best made physical. Cut out two triangles from card and ask learners to place one exactly on top of the other, and then use a photocopier set to 150% to show a similar copy. For the four congruence tests, give learners three straws or lengths of string and some angles to hold, and ask: “Is the triangle fixed, or can you make a different one?” This leads naturally to SSS and SAS, and to the reason SSA fails. For area and volume, ask learners to build squares and cubes from unit blocks and count them, since the factors 4 and 9, and 8 and 27, are then seen and not just memorised. The Similarity Checker on this page tests whether two triangles with given sides are similar or congruent, and the Scale Factor Converter converts between length, area and volume ratios. Always ask learners to say which test they are using and to list the matching vertices.
