British and American English: Vocabulary, Spelling and Usage Differences

Lift or elevator? Flat or apartment? Biscuit or cookie? Learn the vocabulary, spelling and usage differences between British and American English.

Do you take the lift or the elevator? Do you live in a flat or an apartment? Do you eat a biscuit or a cookie? English is spoken by hundreds of millions of people on every continent, and its two biggest varieties, British and American English, differ in vocabulary, spelling, grammar and punctuation. Knowing the differences helps you to read, write and speak clearly, and it protects you from mixing the two styles in an examination.

This guide explains how the two varieties came apart, which one to use, and the main differences. Its main part gives 197 vocabulary pairs in nine themed tables, a table of 18 words that mean different things in the two varieties, 104 spelling pairs organised by rule, and 26 grammar, date, time and punctuation differences. It also covers common mistakes, key points, ten practice questions and a teacher activity.

It connects with Formal vs. Informal English, Commonly Misspelled Words, Commonly Mispronounced Words and Common Errors in English. This guide uses British English throughout, except where American forms are shown for comparison.

1.How Did the Two Varieties Develop?

English was brought to North America by settlers in the seventeenth century. Over the next centuries, the language in the two places developed separately, and each variety kept some older forms and created new ones. American English borrowed words from Native American, Spanish, Dutch, French and other languages, and invented new words for new things, such as the railroad and the highway.

Spelling was also changed deliberately. The American writer Noah Webster published a dictionary in 1806 and a much larger one, An American Dictionary of the English Language, in 1828. He argued for simpler spellings that were closer to pronunciation, which is why many American words drop the u in colour and end in -er instead of -re. Meanwhile, British spelling followed the conventions fixed by printers and by dictionaries such as Samuel Johnson's of 1755.

Both varieties are fully correct, and they are almost completely mutually understood. Other English-speaking countries use a mixture: Canada, for instance, uses some British and some American forms, and many Commonwealth countries follow British spelling.

2.Which Variety Should You Use?

  • Follow your syllabus. Many examination boards in the United Kingdom, South Asia, Africa and the Caribbean expect British spelling.
  • Be consistent. Do not write colour and color, or lift and elevator, in the same piece of writing.
  • Set your spell-checker. Choose English (UK) or English (US) in your word processor.
  • Know your audience. Use the style that your reader expects.
  • Quote exactly. If you are quoting an American writer, keep the American spelling inside the quotation marks.

15.Pronunciation Differences

Pronunciation differs as well as vocabulary. Common examples include tomato (tuh-MAH-toh in British, tuh-MAY-toh in American), schedule (SHED-yool and SKED-jool), herb (HURB and URB) and vitamin (VIT-uh-min and VY-tuh-min). See Commonly Mispronounced Words for many more, with audio.

16.Common Mistakes

MistakeBetter habit
Mixing styles: colour and organize, or favorite and flatChoose one variety and use it throughout.
Using an American word in a British text: I live on the second floor of an apartment building.In British English: I live on the first floor of a block of flats.
Writing the date as 03/11/2026 for an international readerWrite the month in words: 3 November 2026.
Changing spelling inside a quotationKeep the original spelling of a quotation.
Using gotten in British EnglishUse got: She has got better.
Writing Mr. and Dr. in a British textWrite Mr and Dr without a full stop.
Using slang pairs such as quid / buck in an essayUse the standard word: pound / dollar.
Assuming that one variety is wrongBoth are correct; the difference is regional.
Key points to remember
  • British and American English differ in vocabulary, spelling, grammar, punctuation and pronunciation, but both are correct.
  • Choose one variety, follow your syllabus, and be consistent in a piece of writing.
  • Spelling patterns are regular: -our / -or, -re / -er, -ise / -ize, double l / single l.
  • Some words exist in both varieties with different meanings: biscuit, pants, football, first floor, table.
  • Dates differ: 3 November 2026 and November 3, 2026; write the month in words to avoid confusion.
  • Learn the key vocabulary pairs: lift / elevator, flat / apartment, lorry / truck, pavement / sidewalk.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students a short paragraph written in American English and ask them to rewrite it in British English, changing the vocabulary, the spelling and the punctuation, then to swap with a partner for checking. A matching game with British word cards and American word cards also works well. Younger students can sort pictures, such as a lorry / truck or a biscuit / cookie, into two columns.

Please note: this guide uses British English. Some words have several accepted forms, and usage is changing, because the two varieties influence each other through films, music and the internet. Students should follow the style of their own syllabus.
