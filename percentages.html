Percentages: Averages, Profit & Loss, Discounts & Interest

A percentage is just a ratio to 100, but that one idea powers exam marks, sale prices, bank interest and business profit. Learn the patterns behind every percentage problem.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|14 min read|Last updated: October 2026|Mathematics

Sale tags, exam results, bank interest, tax, tips and company profits all use percentages. Few topics in mathematics are used as often in adult life. A percentage is just a ratio to 100, and once you see that, the rest follows from a handful of patterns. This guide takes you from the basic idea to profit and loss, discounts and compound interest, with worked examples and real-life problems. It builds on Ratio & Proportion.

1.What Is a Percentage?

A percentage is a ratio that always compares a part to a whole of exactly 100. The symbol % literally means “per hundred”.

Example 1

25% means 25 out of every 100. That is the same amount as the fraction 25/100, which is 1/4 in simplest form, and the decimal 0.25.

The gold squares glow gently. Counting the shaded squares gives the percentage directly.

  • Decimal to percentage: multiply by 100 (move the point two places right). 0.08 = 8%.

Learn the first few rows by heart. They make mental calculation much faster, as the next section shows.

3.Finding a Percentage of a Quantity

Turn the percentage into a decimal, then multiply.

Example 2

Find 25% of 80. Convert: 25% = 0.25. Multiply: 0.25 × 80 = 20.

Mental shortcuts (build any percentage from easy ones):

  • 10% = divide by 10. 1% = divide by 100. 50% = halve. 25% = halve twice.
  • 15% of 240: 10% = 24, 5% = 12, so 15% = 24 + 12 = 36.
  • 12.5% of 200 = one eighth of 200 = 25.
Example 3: A Tip and a Fee

A restaurant bill is Rs 3,500 and a 10% service charge is added. Charge = 0.10 × 3,500 = Rs 350, so the total is Rs 3,850. (Shortcut: 3,500 × 1.10 = 3,850.)

4.Finding What Percentage One Number Is of Another

Write the part over the whole as a fraction, then multiply by 100: percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100.

Example 4

(a) 45 out of 180: (45 ÷ 180) × 100 = 25%.

(b) A student scores 68 out of 80 in a test: (68 ÷ 80) × 100 = 85%.

5.Percentage Increase and Decrease

To find a percentage change, work out the change, divide by the original value, then multiply by 100. Percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100.

Example 5: Increase

A price rises from Rs 80 to Rs 100. Increase = 20. Percentage increase = (20 ÷ 80) × 100 = 25%.

Example 6: Decrease

A price falls from Rs 100 to Rs 80. Decrease = 20. Percentage decrease = (20 ÷ 100) × 100 = 20%.

Notice that the same Rs 20 gives a 25% rise one way and a 20% fall the other way. The base is different each time.

The multiplier method

This is a fast way to increase or decrease an amount in one step. For an increase of r%, multiply by 1 + r/100. For a decrease of r%, multiply by 1 − r/100.

Example 7

(a) Increase Rs 2,500 by 12%: multiplier 1.12, so 2,500 × 1.12 = Rs 2,800.

(b) Decrease Rs 4,000 by 35%: multiplier 0.65, so 4,000 × 0.65 = Rs 2,600.

“A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease brings you back to the original value”

This feels intuitive, but each percentage change is calculated from a different base value.

The fix: 100 increased by 20% becomes 120. But 120 decreased by 20% is 120 − 24 = 96, not 100. The second percentage is taken of the new, larger value, so equal and opposite percentage changes never cancel exactly. With multipliers: 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96.

The bars rise one after another. The last bar stops below the dashed start line.

6.Finding the Original Value (Reverse Percentages)

Sometimes you know the new value and the percentage change, and you must go back to the start. The new value stands for a percentage of the original: after a 20% increase it is 120% of the original, and after a 20% decrease it is 80% of the original. Divide by the multiplier.

Example 8

(a) After a 20% increase, a price is Rs 120. Original = 120 ÷ 1.20 = Rs 100.

(b) A jacket is on sale at Rs 2,000 after a 20% discount. It is 80% of the marked price, so the original = 2,000 ÷ 0.80 = Rs 2,500.

(c) A price including 18% tax is Rs 5,900. The price before tax = 5,900 ÷ 1.18 = Rs 5,000.

“Price after 20% discount is Rs 2,000, so the original is 2,000 + 20% of 2,000 = Rs 2,400”

This takes the percentage of the sale price, but the 20% was taken off the original price.

The fix: the discount was 20% of the original, so Rs 2,000 is 80% of the original. Divide by 0.80: 2,000 ÷ 0.80 = Rs 2,500. Check: 20% of 2,500 = 500, and 2,500 − 500 = 2,000. ✓

7.Averages (Mean)

The average (or mean) of a set of numbers is their sum divided by how many numbers there are. Like a percentage, it describes a whole group with one number. (See Basic Data & Statistics for median, mode and range.)

Example 9

Find the average of 12, 15, 18, 21 and 24. Sum = 90 and the count is 5, so the average = 90 ÷ 5 = 18.

Example 10: Averaging Percentages

A student scores 60%, 75% and 90% in three equal tests. Average = (60 + 75 + 90) ÷ 3 = 75%.

Now suppose the tests are different sizes: Test 1 is out of 20 (score 15, which is 75%) and Test 2 is out of 80 (score 56, which is 70%). You cannot just average 75% and 70%. Add the marks instead: (15 + 56) ÷ (20 + 80) = 71 ÷ 100 = 71%. The simple average of the two percentages would give 72.5%, which is wrong because the bigger test counts for more.

8.Profit and Loss

Every sale has a Cost Price (CP, what the seller paid) and a Selling Price (SP, what the buyer pays). If SP is higher, there is a profit. If SP is lower, there is a loss. Both are always worked out as a percentage of the Cost Price, never the selling price.

Example 11: Profit

CP = Rs 200 and SP = Rs 250. Profit = 250 − 200 = Rs 50. Profit% = (50 ÷ 200) × 100 = 25%.

Example 12: Loss

CP = Rs 300 and SP = Rs 270. Loss = 300 − 270 = Rs 30. Loss% = (30 ÷ 300) × 100 = 10%.

Calculating profit% as (SP − CP) ÷ SP × 100

Dividing by the selling price instead of the cost price gives a smaller, incorrect percentage.

The fix: profit and loss percentages are always calculated against the Cost Price, since that is the seller’s original investment. For CP = Rs 200 and SP = Rs 250, the correct profit% is 50 ÷ 200 × 100 = 25%, not 50 ÷ 250 × 100 = 20%.

Example 13: Finding SP and CP

(a) A trader buys a fan for Rs 800 and wants a 15% profit. SP = 800 × 1.15 = Rs 920.

(b) A shop sells a bag for Rs 540 at a 10% loss. SP is 90% of CP, so CP = 540 ÷ 0.90 = Rs 600.

9.Discounts

A discount is a percentage reduction off a Marked Price (MP), the original listed price before any sale.

Example 14

MP = Rs 500 and the discount is 15%. Discount = 0.15 × 500 = Rs 75. Selling price = 500 − 75 = Rs 425. (Or in one step: 500 × 0.85 = 425.)

“A 20% discount followed by a further 10% discount is a 30% discount”

Adding the percentages ignores that the second discount is taken from the already-reduced price.

The fix: multiply the multipliers. 0.80 × 0.90 = 0.72, so you pay 72% of the marked price, a total discount of 28%, not 30%. On Rs 1,000: after 20% off you pay Rs 800, then 10% off Rs 800 is Rs 80, leaving Rs 720.

10.Simple Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount borrowed or invested, the Principal (P), using SI = (P × R × T) ÷ 100, where R is the yearly rate (%) and T is the time in years.

Example 15

Principal = Rs 1,000, rate = 5% per year, time = 3 years. SI = (1,000 × 5 × 3) ÷ 100 = Rs 150. Total amount = 1,000 + 150 = Rs 1,150.

Example 16: Finding the Time

Rs 5,000 earns Rs 600 simple interest at 4% a year. For how long? From SI = PRT/100: T = (SI × 100) ÷ (P × R) = (600 × 100) ÷ (5,000 × 4) = 60,000 ÷ 20,000 = 3 years.

11.Compound Interest

Unlike simple interest, compound interest is recalculated on the growing total each year, so interest earns interest. After n years: A = P × (1 + R/100)n.

Example 17

Principal = Rs 1,000, rate = 5%, time = 2 years. Amount = 1,000 × 1.052 = Rs 1,102.50, so the compound interest is Rs 102.50. Simple interest over the same 2 years would be Rs 100. The extra Rs 2.50 comes from year 2’s interest being worked out on Rs 1,050, not on Rs 1,000.

The bars grow in turn. The gap between simple and compound interest widens every year.
Example 18: Long-Run Comparison

Rs 10,000 at 8% for 3 years: simple interest gives 10,000 + 2,400 = Rs 12,400. Compound interest gives 10,000 × 1.083 = Rs 12,597.12. Compounding adds Rs 197.12 after only 3 years, and the gap keeps growing. (These are practice numbers, not real bank rates.)

The rule of 72: a handy estimate for how long money takes to double at a compound rate is 72 ÷ rate. At 8% that is about 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years. (Check: 1.089 ≈ 1.999, so the estimate is very close.)

Real-World Example

A store marks a jacket at Rs 8,000 and discounts it 25%, so it sells for Rs 6,000. A shopper later resells it for Rs 7,500, a profit of Rs 1,500 on a cost of Rs 6,000, which is 25%. Meanwhile, Rs 200,000 saved at 4% simple interest for 3 years earns (200,000 × 4 × 3) ÷ 100 = Rs 24,000 in interest.

Did You Know?

The percent sign is generally believed to have developed from the Italian phrase “per cento”, meaning “for a hundred”, which scribes gradually shortened over centuries into the two-circles-and-a-slash symbol we use today.

12.Common Mistakes

  • Using the wrong base. Percentage change is divided by the original value. Profit and loss use the cost price.
  • Adding successive percentages. A 20% rise then a 20% fall is not zero. Multiply the multipliers instead.
  • Adding instead of dividing in reverse problems. If a price is Rs 2,000 after a 20% discount, the original is 2,000 ÷ 0.80, not 2,000 + 20%.
  • Averaging averages. When groups have different sizes, add up the marks and divide by the total, not the percentages.
  • Forgetting to convert. 5% is 0.05, not 0.5. Move the decimal point two places.
  • Mixing up percent and percentage points. A rise from 10% to 15% is 5 percentage points, but a 50% increase.
  • Using the wrong interest formula. Simple interest uses PRT ÷ 100. Compound interest uses P × (1 + R/100)n, and the question tells you which one to use.

13.Try It Yourself

  1. Find 35% of Rs 2,400.
  2. A phone costs Rs 40,000 and its price rises to Rs 46,000. What is the percentage increase?
  3. After a 15% discount a pair of shoes costs Rs 1,700. What was the marked price?
Show answers

1. 0.35 × 2,400 = Rs 840. (Shortcut: 10% = 240, so 30% = 720, and 5% = 120, giving 840.)

2. Increase = 6,000. (6,000 ÷ 40,000) × 100 = 15%.

3. The sale price is 85% of the marked price. 1,700 ÷ 0.85 = Rs 2,000.

14.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] Write 3/8 as a percentage.
  2. [3 marks] A shopkeeper buys a table for Rs 12,000 and sells it for Rs 14,400. Find his profit percentage.
  3. [3 marks] A price of Rs 5,900 includes 18% tax. Find the price before tax.
  4. [4 marks] Rs 20,000 is invested for 3 years at 5% compound interest per year. Find the total amount and the interest earned.
Show answers

1. 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375, so 37.5%.

2. Profit = 2,400. Profit% = (2,400 ÷ 12,000) × 100 = 20%.

3. 5,900 ÷ 1.18 = Rs 5,000.

4. Amount = 20,000 × 1.053 = 20,000 × 1.157625 = Rs 23,152.50. Interest = 23,152.50 − 20,000 = Rs 3,152.50.

15.Key Points

  • A percentage is a number out of 100. 25% = 25/100 = 1/4 = 0.25.
  • Percentage of a quantity: convert to a decimal and multiply.
  • Percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100. Multipliers: 1 + r/100 for a rise, 1 − r/100 for a fall.
  • To reverse a percentage change, divide by the multiplier.
  • Profit and loss are percentages of the cost price. Discounts are percentages of the marked price.
  • Simple interest: PRT ÷ 100. Compound interest: P × (1 + R/100)n.

16.For Teachers and Parents

Percentages become meaningful when learners use them on real numbers: prices on a shop shelf, their own exam marks, the interest in a savings plan. Start with the hundred square so the idea “out of 100” is visible. Teach the multiplier method early, because it makes reverse percentages and repeated changes much simpler. Ask “percent of what?” every time. This question prevents the most common errors (the wrong base) and also builds the habit of reading news and advertisements critically.

17.Why This Topic Matters

Percentages are among the most consistently used pieces of mathematics in adult life: reading a sale discount, comparing loan interest rates, working out a tip or a tax, understanding exam grades and reading a company’s profit margin. The skills above give you the tools to check every one of those numbers for yourself, instead of taking them on trust.
