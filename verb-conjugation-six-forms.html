Verb Conjugation: The Six Forms of a Verb

Write, writes, wrote, written, writing, to write. Learn what each of the six verb forms does, which ones can be the main verb, and how to stop the most common verb mistakes.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 25 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Grammar

A verb is not one word but a family of six forms. Write, writes, wrote, written, writing and to write are all the same verb, and each form has its own job. Once you know which form does which job, most verb mistakes disappear.

Students are often taught three forms of a verb (base, past, past participle), or four if the -ing form is included. That leaves two big gaps. Learners see to write or to play in a sentence and call it the main verb, and they are unsure when to add s or es. This guide fills both gaps with six forms, shows which of them can be a main verb, and gives the spelling and usage rules for each.

It builds on Parts of Speech and Tenses & Verb Forms. A full A to Z table of over 1,000 verbs with all six forms is in the companion article, The Complete Verb Forms List. Spelling follows British English.

1.The Big Idea: What Is Conjugation?

A verb shows an action (write), a state (seem) or an occurrence (happen). Conjugation means changing the form of a verb to show tense (time), person (I, you, he), number (one or many) and function (what job the word does in the sentence).

Golden rule

Not every verb form can be the main verb. Only some forms do the main job. The others are helpers, descriptions or names for the action.

3.Finite and Non-Finite: Which Form Can Be the Main Verb?

This is the key to the confusion. A finite verb changes with the subject and the tense, so it can stand alone as the main verb. A non-finite verb does not show tense or agree with a subject on its own, so it can never be the main verb by itself.

FormFinite or non-finite?Main verb on its own?
Base (write)Finite in the present: I writeYes
S / ES (writes)FiniteYes
Past (wrote)FiniteYes
Past participle (written)Non-finiteNo. It needs a helper: has written
Present participle (writing)Non-finiteNo. It needs a helper: is writing
Infinitive (to write)Non-finiteNever
Remember

When you see to write or to play in a sentence, it is NOT the main verb. It is an infinitive. The main verb is somewhere else in the sentence.

Worked examples

Example 1

I want to write a letter. Cross out to write. Change to yesterday: I wanted to write a letter. The finite main verb is want.

Example 2

She likes to play cricket. Main verb: likes. Infinitive: to play.

Example 3

To err is human. Here the infinitive is the subject. The main verb is is.

Example 4

The boys playing outside are my cousins. Playing has no helper, so it is a participle describing boys. The main verb is are.

4.Form 1: The Base Form (V1)

The base form is the dictionary form with no ending: write, play, go, study. It is used in these places:

  1. Present simple with I, you, we, they and plural nouns: I write. They play. The students study.
  2. After modal verbs: can write, must go, should study, will play.
  3. After do, does and did: Do you play? She does not write. He did not go.
  4. Commands (imperatives): Write your name. Play fairly.
  5. Inside the infinitive: to write, to play.
  6. After let, make and help, and with see or hear plus an object: Let him go. She made me laugh. I saw him cross the road.

After does, did and modal verbs, the verb stays in the base form. Do not add -s or -ed a second time.

WrongRight
She does not likes tea.She does not like tea.
He did not went.He did not go.
They can plays chess.They can play chess.

5.Form 2: The S / ES Form

This form is used only in the present simple with a third-person singular subject: he, she, it, a singular noun or a name. He writes. The teacher plays. Ali goes. My sister studies.

RuleExample
Most verbs: add -swrite → writes, play → plays, read → reads
Verbs ending in s, ss, sh, ch, x, z: add -espass → passes, wash → washes, watch → watches, fix → fixes, buzz → buzzes
Verbs ending in o: add -esgo → goes, do → does, echo → echoes
Consonant + y: change y to iesstudy → studies, try → tries, carry → carries
Vowel + y: just add -splay → plays, enjoy → enjoys, say → says
have is irregularhave → has
be is irregularI am, he / she / it is, you / we / they are

How the ending sounds

SoundAfterExamples
/s/p, t, k, f soundsstops, writes, works, laughs
/z/voiced sounds and vowelsplays, begins, reads, goes
/ɪz/s, z, sh, ch, x, j soundswashes, watches, fixes, judges

Clearing the S / ES confusion

Students mix up the plural -s of nouns and the -s of verbs. Use the "only one S" check in simple present sentences. If the subject is singular, the verb takes the s. If the subject is plural, the noun takes the s and the verb does not.

SubjectSentence
He / she / itShe writes poems.
The girl (singular)The girl writes poems.
The girls (plural)The girls write poems.
I / you / we / theyThey write poems.

Watch out for exceptions. Some nouns end in s but are singular: news, mathematics, physics, economics. The news is good. Mathematics is my favourite subject. Some plural nouns do not end in s: people, children, men, women. The children play. The people are waiting. Also remember that the S/ES form belongs to the present simple only. In the past tense there is no -s: She wrote. The boy played.

6.Form 3: The Past Simple (V2, Second Form)

The past simple shows a finished action in the past: I wrote a letter yesterday. They played well. It has one form for all subjects, except was / were.

Rule for regular verbsExample
Most verbs: add -edplay → played, work → worked
Verbs ending in e: add -dlike → liked, live → lived
Consonant + y: change y to iedstudy → studied, try → tried
Vowel + y: add -edplay → played, enjoy → enjoyed
Short verbs ending in one vowel + one consonant: double the consonantstop → stopped, plan → planned, admit → admitted, prefer → preferred
Last syllable not stressed: do not doublevisit → visited, open → opened, offer → offered
Verbs ending in l (British spelling): double the ltravel → travelled, cancel → cancelled

How -ed sounds

SoundAfterExamples
/t/p, k, f, s, sh, ch soundsworked, stopped, washed, laughed
/d/voiced sounds and vowelsplayed, opened, called, stayed
/ɪd/t and dwanted, needed, visited, decided

Questions and negatives. Use did plus the base form. The past marking moves to did: She wrote a letter. Did she write a letter? She did not write a letter.

7.Form 4: The Past Participle (V3, Third Form)

The past participle is the form used after have, has, had and be. It is never the main verb on its own. For regular verbs it looks exactly like the past simple (played / played). For irregular verbs it may differ: write → wrote → written, go → went → gone, see → saw → seen.

UseExample
Perfect tenses (have / has / had + V3)I have written. She has gone. They had finished.
Passive voice (be + V3)The letter was written by Sara. English is spoken here.
As an adjectivea broken window, a finished project, a closed door
After a modal + haveshould have written, could have gone, must have forgotten
WrongRightReason
She has went home.She has gone home.After has, use V3, not V2.
I have wrote it.I have written it.V3 of write is written.
He was ate by the lion.He was eaten by the lion.The passive needs V3.
They seen the film.They saw / have seen the film.Seen always needs a helper.

9.Form 5: The Present Participle (V4, the -ing Form)

The present participle is the base form plus -ing: writing, playing, going, studying.

RuleExample
Most verbs: add -ingplay → playing, study → studying, go → going
Ending in silent e: drop the ewrite → writing, make → making, come → coming
Ending in ee, ye, oe: keep the esee → seeing, be → being, dye → dyeing
Ending in ie: change to yinglie → lying, die → dying, tie → tying
Short verbs ending in one vowel + one consonant: double the consonantrun → running, stop → stopping, begin → beginning
Last syllable not stressed: do not doublevisit → visiting, open → opening
Ending in w, x, y: do not doublesnow → snowing, fix → fixing, play → playing
Ending in c (some verbs): add kpicnic → picnicking, panic → panicking
UseExample
Continuous tenses (be + V4)She is writing. They were playing. I will be studying.
As an adjectivea running tap, a smiling child, an interesting book
An action happening at the same timeWalking home, he met a friend.
After see, hear, watch, find + objectI saw him crossing the road.
Remember

-ing alone is never the main verb. The boy playing outside is not a sentence. The boy is playing outside is.

Present participle or gerund?

A gerund also ends in -ing, but it works as a noun. Test: if you can replace the -ing word with it or something, it is a gerund.

Present participle (verb or adjective)Gerund (noun)
She is writing a letter.Writing is her passion.
The girl sitting there is my cousin.He enjoys swimming.

10.Form 6: The Infinitive (to + V1)

The infinitive is the verb in its "name" form: to write, to play, to go. The word to is called the infinitive marker. The infinitive does not change for subject or tense, so it is never a main verb.

SentenceMain verbInfinitive
I want to write a story.wantto write
She hopes to play in the final.hopesto play
He decided to go home.decidedto go
To learn is a pleasure.isTo learn (the subject)

Two kinds of infinitive

  • To-infinitive: to write, to play.
  • Bare infinitive (no to), used after modals and some verbs: I can write. He made me laugh. Let her go.

Jobs of the infinitive

Which verbs take which form?

PatternTypical verbsExample

Is "to" always an infinitive marker?

No. Sometimes to is a preposition, and then a noun or an -ing form follows. I want to write. (infinitive) but I look forward to writing to you. (preposition) and She is used to getting up early. Test: if you can put a noun there (I look forward to the trip), to is a preposition.

12.Auxiliary and Main Verbs

TypeVerbsJob
Main (lexical) verbwrite, play, study, runCarries the meaning
Primary auxiliarybe, have, doHelps build tenses, questions and negatives
Modal auxiliarycan, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would, ought toShows ability, permission, possibility or obligation

Be, have and do can also be main verbs. She has a car. (main verb: possession) but She has finished. (auxiliary). A verb phrase can have one main verb and up to three helpers: She will have been writing since morning. The main verb is writing; will, have, been are helpers.

13.Advanced Extras

The subjunctive mood

The subjunctive uses the base form for all persons (no -s), and were for all persons in unreal situations. I suggest that he write the report. (not writes) It is essential that she be present. (not is) If I were you, I would apologise. (formal usage; was is common in speech)

Modal perfect forms

PatternMeaningExample
must have + V3strong deduction about the pastHe must have forgotten.
should have + V3criticism or regretYou should have written.
could have + V3past possibilityShe could have won.
might have + V3uncertain past possibilityThey might have left.
needn't have + V3the action was unnecessaryI needn't have gone.

Participle clauses

  • Walking home, he saw an accident. (present participle clause; both parts must have the same subject)
  • Written in 1950, the novel is still popular. (past participle clause)
  • Having finished the exam, she went out. (perfect participle, showing the earlier action)

Dangling participle (error): Walking home, the rain started. The rain was not walking. Correct: Walking home, I felt the rain start.

Non-finite clauses

A non-finite verb heads a clause that has no tense or subject of its own: To succeed, you must work hard. (infinitive clause) Having no money, he walked. (participle clause) Swimming is good exercise. (gerund clause as subject).

Phrasal verbs conjugate normally

The verb part changes and the particle stays the same: look up, looks up, looked up, looked up, looking up, to look up.

14.Key Terms

Conjugation: changing the form of a verb for tense, person and number.
Base form: the verb in its plain dictionary form.
Infinitive: to + the base form; the verb's "name".
Finite verb: a verb that shows tense and agrees with its subject.
Non-finite verb: an infinitive, participle or gerund; it has no tense or agreement of its own.
Participle: a verb form used with helpers or as an adjective (-ing or -ed / -en).
Gerund: an -ing form working as a noun.
Auxiliary verb: a helping verb such as be, have or do.
Modal verb: an auxiliary showing ability, possibility, permission or obligation.
Regular verb: a verb that forms its past and past participle by adding -ed.
Irregular verb: a verb that does not follow the -ed rule.
Third-person singular: he, she, it or a singular noun.
Voice: active (the subject does the action) or passive (the subject receives it).
Mood: the attitude of the verb: indicative, imperative or subjunctive.
Subject-verb agreement: the verb matches its subject in number and person.

17.Why This Matters

Verb errors are among the most visible mistakes in writing, and most of them come from using the right verb in the wrong form. Once you know that only the base, S/ES and past forms can be the main verb, that the past participle and -ing form need helpers, and that the infinitive is never the main verb, sentence analysis becomes much easier. Continue with Tenses & Verb Forms, then use The Complete Verb Forms List whenever you need to check a form.

Key points to remember
  • A verb has six forms: infinitive, base, S/ES, past, past participle and -ing.
  • Only the base, S/ES and past forms can be the main verb on their own.
  • To + verb is an infinitive. It is never the main verb.
  • After do, does, did and modal verbs, always use the base form.
  • After have, has and had, use the past participle (V3).
  • The S/ES form is used only in the present simple with he, she, it or a singular noun.
  • Use the "only one S" check: singular subject, verb takes -s; plural subject, noun takes -s.
  • Check irregular verbs in the Complete Verb Forms List.
For teachers and parents

Activity: write ten sentences on the board, each containing an infinitive. Ask students to cross out the infinitive and underline the finite main verb. Then ask them to turn each verb into all six forms.

Please note: this guide uses British spelling (travelled, practise). American English spells some forms differently, so students should follow the spelling system their school uses.
