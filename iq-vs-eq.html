IQ vs EQ: Differences, Myths and Facts (What the Evidence Really Says)

Which matters more, IQ or EQ? See how they differ, how they overlap, what each predicts, why the "80 per cent EQ" claim misleads, and what else matters for success and wellbeing.

Last updated: 12 October 2026. Written by the EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team. This article is for general education and does not replace advice from a qualified professional.

"Which is more important, IQ or EQ?" It may be the most common question about intelligence, and it is usually asked with a strong expectation that the answer is one or the other. Popular books, motivational posts and job-interview advice have repeated that EQ is "80 per cent of success" or that "IQ gets you hired, EQ gets you promoted". In this final article of our series, we put those claims to the test. We compare what each concept is, how they are related, what each of them predicts according to large meta-analyses, where the famous 80 per cent figure came from, and what else matters. We also give you a map of the whole series and a practical plan for building both. All main numbers come from published studies, which are listed at the end.

Key Takeaways

IQ and EQ are different but related. The best-fitting scientific models treat emotional intelligence as a group of abilities that sits within general intelligence, and tests of emotion understanding overlap with knowledge. IQ is a strong predictor of education (r about .56), a moderate predictor of job performance (about .31 in the latest large revision) and a modest predictor of income (about .20 to .23). EQ shows modest but real links with grades (about .20), well-being (about .32) and some work outcomes, and it adds only a small amount beyond IQ and personality. The claim that EQ is "80%" of success is a misreading of a statistic. Self-discipline, conscientiousness, relationships, health, opportunity and good teaching matter too. The sensible answer to "which matters more?" is: it depends on the outcome, the situation and the person, and you can improve both.

1.Why "Which Matters More?" Is a Tricky Question

Before comparing IQ and EQ, we need three ideas that decide how any comparison should be read.

  • It depends on the outcome. Success in an exam, a friendship, a career, a marriage and good health are different outcomes, and each has different ingredients.
  • It depends on the measure. There are ability tests of emotional intelligence, self-report questionnaires and "mixed" models. As we saw earlier in the series, these give quite different results.
  • It depends on the statistic. A correlation (r) shows how strongly two things go together, from 0 (none) to 1 (perfect). Squaring it (r²) gives the share of the differences between people that one factor "explains" statistically. This is easy to misread.
A correlation of .45 "explains" about 20% of the differences between people, not because the rest is one other factor, but because many things influence outcomes.

Notice that even a "strong" correlation of .45 leaves about 80 per cent of the variation unexplained by that one factor. That is the key to understanding the "20/80" claim that we examine in Section 7. Also remember that group statistics describe averages across many people. They cannot predict what any one person will do.

3.Are IQ and EQ Related? Not Opposites

A common belief says that "clever people have low EQ" or that "emotionally skilled people are not academic". The evidence points in the opposite direction: the two are positively and modestly related. A study of 688 students (MacCann, Joseph, Newman and Roberts, 2014) tested several models of how emotional abilities fit with cognitive abilities. The best-fitting model treated emotional intelligence as a second-level group of abilities within general intelligence (with a strong link of about .80 to the general factor g). A later study (Evans and colleagues, 2020) replicated the result. In other words, ability-based EI behaves like "intelligence applied to emotional information", rather than like an unrelated trait.

A separate line of meta-analytic evidence found weak to modest relationships between the four emotional ability branches and fluid and crystallised intelligence. The strongest links were between emotion understanding and crystallised knowledge (which makes sense, since understanding emotions depends on vocabulary and life knowledge), and the weakest was for emotion regulation.

IQ and EQ are related but not the same. Tests of emotion understanding overlap most with knowledge-type intelligence.

A caution: this applies to ability EI. The popular "EQ" of books and workplace questionnaires is usually the mixed type, which, as we showed in our article on Goleman's five components, overlaps strongly with personality traits such as emotional stability and conscientiousness. So when someone says "high EQ", it is worth asking which kind they mean.

This is a solid and consistent record. It also shows two things that are often forgotten: IQ's predictive power is strongest for education and much more modest for income and job performance, and other factors such as family background are comparably important.

Academic performanceOverall correlation of about .20 (ability EI .24; mixed EI .19; self-rated EI .12); EI was the third most important predictor after intelligence and conscientiousness; stronger in humanities than scienceMacCann and colleagues (2020), 158 citations, 42,529 students

In short, EQ has real but modest links with several outcomes, and the strongest evidence is for school performance and well-being. The evidence that high EQ "beats" IQ in career success is not there.

6.Side by Side: A Careful Look

Published correlations for IQ and for EQ with different outcomes. They come from different studies, so they show the pattern, not a contest.

Seeing the numbers next to each other may tempt you to conclude "IQ wins". Please be careful, for four reasons:

  • The studies measure different outcomes (education versus well-being), so the bars are not directly comparable.
  • IQ tests are more standardised than EQ measures, so IQ results tend to be cleaner.
  • Both have modest correlations with real-world success compared with the popular hype.
  • Correlations are group averages, and many other things matter for each person.

A fair summary is that IQ is a stronger predictor of learning and schooling, while emotional skills are especially relevant for well-being, relationships and the way people handle their emotions at school and work.

7.The "20/80" Claim: Where It Came From, and What It Really Means

In his 1995 book Emotional Intelligence, Daniel Goleman wrote that IQ contributes, at best, about 20 per cent to the factors that determine life success, which leaves 80 per cent to other forces. This sentence has been quoted thousands of times, often turned into "IQ is 20% and EQ is 80%". Researchers who studied the claim have noted several problems:

A statistic about how much IQ "explains" does not tell us what fills the rest. The 80% is not a measurement of EQ.
  1. The 20% is a variance statement. It corresponds to a correlation of about .45 (since .45 squared is roughly .20) and is a statistical statement about differences between people, not a measure of how much of a person's success "comes from" IQ.
  2. The remaining 80% is everything else. It includes family background, health, quality of schooling, personality, effort, opportunity, chance events, relationships and many more. Nothing in the statistic says that this 80% is "EQ".
  3. The numbers depend on the outcome. As we saw, correlations between IQ and success vary from about .20 (income) to .56 (education), so there is no single "20%".
  4. EQ research does not show an 80% share. In the best large studies, ability EI adds only a couple of percentage points of explained variance beyond IQ and personality.
  5. Even the originators objected. The scientists who first described emotional intelligence, Mayer, Salovey and Caruso, questioned the grandiose claims made in the popular book.
Worked Example: Reading a Statistic

Imagine a study finds that height explains 20% of the differences in basketball skill among a group of adults. Would you conclude that the other 80% is "shoe size"? Of course not. It is practice, coaching, health, motivation, teamwork and more. In the same way, if IQ "explains" about 20% of differences in some measure of success, the rest is a long list of things, of which emotional skills are only one.

8.What Else Matters: Beyond IQ and EQ

Focusing on the two famous letters can hide other important ingredients.

FactorEvidence
Self-discipline and conscientiousnessIn two studies of eighth graders (Duckworth and Seligman, 2005; 140 students, and 164 in a replication), self-discipline measured in the autumn accounted for more than twice as much variance as IQ in final grades the following spring, and still predicted grades after controlling for IQ. IQ remained important for scores on standardised achievement tests. In personnel selection, conscientiousness had a revised validity of about .19 (Sackett and colleagues, 2022)
Parental backgroundStrenze (2007) found parental socioeconomic status to be a meaningful predictor of later success, and intelligence was "not overwhelmingly better"
Quality of teaching and relationshipsWarm teacher-student relationships were linked with engagement and achievement (Roorda and colleagues, 2011)
Structured methods in selectionStructured interviews (about .42) were among the best predictors of job performance, ahead of cognitive ability (about .31) in the 2022 revision

Opportunity and chanceGood schools, supportive people and fair opportunities shape which abilities can develop

These findings do not make IQ or EQ unimportant. They show that success and well-being come from a mix of abilities, habits, relationships and circumstances.

9.Smart Is Not the Same as Wise, and Skilled Is Not the Same as Good

A high score on an intelligence test does not guarantee good judgement. In two studies with university students (West, Meserve and Stanovich, 2012), people showed a bias blind spot: they believed that thinking biases affect other people more than themselves. This blind spot was not reduced by higher cognitive ability, and if anything, a larger blind spot was linked with higher cognitive ability. The researchers also found that many classic thinking biases were unrelated to cognitive ability, though in much of the wider literature, a number of biases do decrease with higher ability. The practical lesson: being intelligent does not automatically protect you from fooling yourself. Habits such as asking "What would change my mind?" matter.

Similarly, emotional skill is a tool and not a moral compass. Someone who reads people well may use that skill to help or to manipulate (see our article on EQ at school, home and work). Values such as honesty, fairness and compassion need to guide both kinds of ability.

12.Myths and Facts

MythFact
"EQ is 80% of success and IQ only 20%"A misreading of a variance statistic; the 80% is everything else, and no study shows an 80% share for EQ
"IQ is useless; only EQ matters"IQ is a solid predictor of education and a moderate predictor of job performance
"EQ is useless; it's just soft skills"EI is linked with grades, well-being and relationships, though its extra value beyond IQ and personality is modest
"Clever people have low EQ"They are positively related; ability EI fits within models of intelligence
"Your IQ and EQ are fixed"IQ rank is fairly stable, yet schooling matters; EI skills can be trained
"A high IQ guarantees good judgement"Higher cognitive ability did not reduce the bias blind spot
"EQ tests can pick the best person for a job"Tests overlap with personality, add little beyond other measures, and should be used with caution
"IQ gets you hired, EQ gets you promoted"A catchy saying, not a research finding
"You must choose which to develop"Both can be improved, and effort on one often helps the other

A Closing Thought

Over ten articles, we have seen that intelligence is not one thing, that it grows with schooling, health and practice, that games promise more than they deliver, and that emotional skills are real, learnable and valuable, though not magic. The question "IQ or EQ?" is like asking whether a bird needs the left wing or the right. People thrive when they can think clearly, feel wisely, relate kindly and keep learning. Choose one thinking habit and one emotional habit this week, practise them, and check the evidence before you believe the next big claim, including this one.
