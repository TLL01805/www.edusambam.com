Types of Intelligence: Fluid, Crystallised and Multiple Intelligences

Intelligence is not one thing. Learn how psychologists describe different kinds of thinking, which theories have good evidence, which popular ideas are myths, and how to study smarter.

Last updated: 12 October 2026. Written by the EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team. This article is for general education and does not replace advice from a qualified professional.

Think about three people you know. One solves puzzles faster than anyone. One knows an amazing amount about history, cooking or cricket. One can calm a crowd, or play a tune after hearing it once. Who is the most intelligent? The honest answer is that it depends on what we mean by the word. Psychologists have argued about this for more than a century, and they have proposed several theories. Some are supported by strong evidence, some are useful but unproven, and some popular ideas are simply myths. This guide takes you through the main theories, step by step, so that you can tell the difference.

Key Takeaways

Most psychologists agree that mental abilities are linked: people who do well on one kind of thinking task tend to do well on others, which is the idea of a general factor, g. Within that, two broad kinds are fluid intelligence (solving new problems) and crystallised intelligence (what you have learned). Gardner's eight intelligences are popular and useful for valuing talents, but have weak scientific support, and the idea of "learning styles" is a myth. You can improve how you learn by using proven methods.

1.What Is Intelligence?

There is no single, final definition. In 1994, a group of 52 researchers signed a statement describing intelligence as a very general mental capability that includes reasoning, planning, solving problems, thinking abstractly, understanding complex ideas, learning quickly and learning from experience. They added that it is not just book learning or test-taking skill, but a broad ability to understand our surroundings.

Two reasons explain why the topic is so debated. First, intelligence is a construct: an idea that we cannot see directly and so must measure indirectly, through tasks and tests. Second, different cultures value different skills. A sailor who can find an island using stars and waves, and a student who can solve algebra, both show real intelligence of different kinds.

Question psychologists askTwo sides of the debate
Is intelligence one ability or many?One general ability (g) behind all tasks, or several separate abilities
Is it fixed or changeable?Mostly inherited, or strongly shaped by learning and environment (the real answer involves both)
Is it the same in every culture?A universal ability, or something that depends on what each culture values
Does a test capture all of it?Tests measure important parts, but not everything that makes people capable

2.Spearman and the General Factor (g)

In 1904 the English psychologist Charles Spearman noticed something interesting. Pupils who did well in one school subject usually did fairly well in the others, even in subjects that seemed very different, such as French, mathematics and music. He used statistics to show that the scores were linked, and he proposed that a single general factor, which he called g, lies behind performance on many mental tasks. He also said that each task needs a task-specific skill, called s.

Example

Imagine ten students who take tests in vocabulary, number puzzles and pattern matching. If the student who is best at vocabulary is also usually among the best at number puzzles and patterns, the scores are said to be positively correlated. Spearman argued that g is the reason for this overlap.

The general factor is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. It does not mean that people are equally good at everything, and it does not mean that g is a "thing" located in one part of the brain. It is a statistical summary of the fact that mental abilities tend to go together.

In the 1930s, Louis Thurstone challenged the idea. He argued that intelligence is made of about seven separate primary mental abilities, such as verbal comprehension, number facility, spatial visualisation and memory. When his data were studied again, however, the abilities were still linked to each other, which supported a general factor sitting above them. Today, most researchers use a model that includes both the general factor and the specific abilities.

3.Fluid and Crystallised Intelligence

The psychologist Raymond Cattell, later joined by John Horn, divided general ability into two broad types.

Fluid intelligence (Gf)Crystallised intelligence (Gc)
MeaningThe ability to reason and solve new problems without relying on what you already knowKnowledge, vocabulary and skills gained through learning and experience
Everyday exampleWorking out how an unfamiliar machine or game worksExplaining the causes of a historical event
Typical taskFinding the rule in a pattern of shapesDefining words; answering general knowledge questions
Depends onWorking memory, attention, speed of processingEducation, reading, culture and practice
Across lifeRises in childhood, tends to peak in early adulthood, then slowly declinesKeeps growing for decades, often into the sixties or later
A schematic of how the two kinds of intelligence tend to change across a lifetime. Individual people vary a lot.

The two work together all the time. When you read a new maths problem, fluid intelligence helps you work out what to do, and crystallised intelligence provides the formulas, vocabulary and methods you have learned. The more you know, the less new thinking each problem needs, which is why experts seem to solve hard problems so easily. This is also why an older person can often judge a situation more wisely than a young person even if the younger one is faster at a puzzle.

Real-World Example

An experienced chess master looks at a board and instantly sees good moves, using thousands of stored patterns (crystallised). A beginner who has never seen the opening must reason out each move from scratch (fluid), and finds it far more tiring.

4.The CHC Model: The Map Most Researchers Use

In 1993 John Carroll published a huge analysis of hundreds of datasets, building on the work of Cattell and Horn. The result, now called the Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory, is a hierarchy with three levels, or strata. At the top is the general factor. Below it are several broad abilities. Below those are dozens of narrow abilities. Many modern IQ tests are built on this model.

A simplified three-level model of intelligence: one general factor, several broad abilities, and many narrow skills.
Broad abilityShort codeWhat it means
Fluid reasoningGfSolving new problems; finding rules and patterns
Comprehension-knowledgeGcWord knowledge and general information
Quantitative knowledgeGqUnderstanding numbers and mathematical ideas
Reading and writingGrwReading accurately and fluently; writing clearly
Short-term (working) memoryGwmHolding and using information for a few seconds
Long-term storage and retrievalGlrStoring information and finding it again later
Visual processingGvThinking with images, shapes and space
Auditory processingGaTelling sounds apart and working with sound patterns
Processing speedGsDoing simple tasks quickly and accurately

Notice what is not in this list: creativity, motivation, leadership and emotional skills. The CHC model describes cognitive (thinking) abilities. Other theories try to cover the wider picture.

5.Gardner's Multiple Intelligences

In 1983, the Harvard psychologist Howard Gardner published the book Frames of Mind, in which he argued that intelligence is not one ability but several independent "intelligences". He originally listed seven and later added an eighth, the naturalist intelligence. He also considered a possible ninth, existential intelligence (thinking about deep questions of life), but did not fully add it.

Howard Gardner's eight proposed intelligences. They are a useful way to value different talents, but they are not accepted as separate, proven abilities.
IntelligenceStrength in...Example careers or activities
LinguisticWords, reading, writing, speakingWriter, lawyer, teacher
Logical-mathematicalNumbers, logic, scientific reasoningScientist, accountant, programmer
SpatialImages, maps, design, picturing objectsArchitect, pilot, graphic designer
Bodily-kinestheticSkilled movement and handling objectsAthlete, surgeon, dancer
MusicalRhythm, pitch and melodyMusician, composer, sound engineer
InterpersonalUnderstanding and working with othersCounsellor, manager, nurse
IntrapersonalUnderstanding yourselfResearcher, writer, entrepreneur
NaturalistRecognising living things and patterns in natureBiologist, farmer, ecologist

Why teachers like it

The theory sends a positive message: everyone has strengths, and school should value more than words and numbers. It encouraged teachers to offer projects, music, drama, movement and group work, and many students found those activities more engaging.

Why most psychologists are cautious

  • Weak evidence: Gardner built his list from reasoning and case studies, not from tests that separate the abilities. When abilities are measured, they tend to be linked, which supports g rather than eight independent intelligences.
  • Some are talents, not intelligences: critics say that musical or bodily skill is better called a talent, since we do not usually call a gifted athlete "intelligent" in the same sense as a gifted reasoner.
  • Interpersonal and intrapersonal overlap with emotional intelligence, a topic with its own research.
  • No agreed tests, so the theory is hard to check.

A balanced view is to treat Gardner's list as a helpful way to appreciate human variety, not as proven science, and not as a way to label children ("you are a musical learner").

6.Sternberg's Triarchic Theory

In 1985, Robert Sternberg proposed that successful intelligence has three parts, which is why the theory is called triarchic.

PartMeaningExample
AnalyticalAnalysing, comparing and evaluating; the kind measured by school testsFinding the flaw in an argument
CreativeGenerating new ideas and dealing with new situationsInventing a new game from simple materials
PracticalApplying ideas in real life; adapting to your environmentFinding a quick solution when a plan fails

Sternberg's main point is that school tests capture the analytical part well but miss the creative and practical parts, which matter in life. His theory has also been criticised for needing more evidence, but it draws attention to useful, real-world skills.

Real-World Example

Two students get equal marks in a business exam. One can write a perfect analysis of a case study (analytical). The other, running a small school stall, notices that a rival's price is lower, invents a bundle offer and doubles sales in a week (creative and practical). Both are showing real intelligence of different kinds.

7.Creativity, Wisdom and Emotional Intelligence

Several other human strengths overlap with intelligence without being the same thing.

  • Creativity: producing ideas that are both new and useful. In 1950, J. P. Guilford separated convergent thinking (finding the single right answer) from divergent thinking (finding many possible answers). IQ tests mostly measure the first; creativity tests mostly measure the second. Studies show that a certain level of reasoning ability helps creativity, but above that level, other things such as curiosity, knowledge and persistence matter more.
  • Wisdom: good judgement about important and uncertain life matters, which combines knowledge, experience, humility and concern for others.
  • Emotional intelligence: noticing, understanding and managing feelings. You can read more in our article IQ and EQ Explained.

9.Myths About Intelligence and the Brain

MythWhat the evidence says
People are either "left-brained" (logical) or "right-brained" (creative)The two sides of the brain differ in some functions, but they work together. A brain-imaging study of over a thousand people found no sign that people mainly use one side
We use only 10% of our brainsBrain scans show activity throughout the whole brain over a day, and damage to almost any area causes some loss
Everyone has a fixed "learning style" (visual, auditory, kinesthetic) and learns best when taught in that styleReviews of the research found no good evidence that matching teaching to a preferred style improves learning
Listening to Mozart makes children smarterA 1993 study found a small, short-lived effect on a spatial task in adults. It did not show a lasting rise in intelligence
"Brain-training" apps make you smarter in generalPeople get better at the trained game, but evidence of wide improvements in everyday thinking is weak
Intelligence is only about being good at schoolSchool tests capture some abilities well, but life uses many more

It is true that people have preferences. You may enjoy diagrams more than lectures. But what matters for learning is matching the method to the material, not to your label. A map is best learned with a picture, whether you call yourself visual or not, and a poem is best learned by hearing it.

10.Study Methods That Really Work

Instead of trying to find your "type", use methods that research supports for almost everyone.

MethodWhat to doWhy it works
Retrieval practiceClose the book and write down or say everything you remember, then checkPulling information out of memory strengthens it more than re-reading
Spaced practiceStudy a topic for short sessions on different days, not one long sessionForgetting a little and then recalling makes memories last longer
InterleavingMix different kinds of problems in one sessionYou learn to choose the right method, not just repeat one
ElaborationAsk "why" and "how" and explain the idea in your own wordsConnecting ideas to what you know builds crystallised knowledge
Dual codingCombine words with a diagram or sketch of the same ideaTwo connected representations give more routes to recall
Worked Example

You are learning the types of intelligence. Day 1: read this article and draw the hierarchy from memory. Day 3: without notes, list Gardner's eight intelligences, then check. Day 7: mix in questions from an earlier topic and explain the difference between fluid and crystallised intelligence to a friend. This three-step plan uses retrieval, spacing, interleaving and elaboration together.

11.Discover Your Strengths: A Reflection Activity

This is not a test, and it cannot give you a score. It is a way to notice what you enjoy and where you already show skill. Write a short answer to each question, and ask a friend or teacher to add what they notice about you.

Question to ask yourselfSkill it points to
Which tasks make me lose track of time?Interest and flow
When did I last solve a problem nobody told me how to solve?Fluid reasoning, creativity
Which topic do I know more about than most people my age?Crystallised knowledge
What do friends ask me for help with?Practical and interpersonal skill
What did I improve at by practising?Growth, persistence

Your answers are not a fixed label. They are clues about where to put effort, and they will change as you learn.

12.Seven Common Mistakes When Thinking About Intelligence

  1. Treating one test score as the full measure of a person.
  2. Believing that abilities are completely fixed.
  3. Believing that Gardner's list is proven science.
  4. Labelling yourself or others as a "type" of learner.
  5. Ignoring knowledge: crystallised intelligence is built by reading and study, and it matters a great deal.
  6. Thinking that creativity and intelligence are opposites.
  7. Forgetting that effort, sleep, health and good teaching also shape how well people think.

A Closing Thought

Intelligence is best seen as a family of related abilities that can grow with knowledge, practice and good habits. Be curious about theories, ask what evidence supports them, and be careful with labels. Most of all, remember that the useful question is not "How intelligent am I?" but "What can I learn next, and what is the best way to learn it?"
