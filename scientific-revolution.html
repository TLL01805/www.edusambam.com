The Scientific Revolution: How Modern Science Began

For most of history, questions about the natural world were settled by authority and tradition, not evidence. Over about 200 years, that changed completely — and every idea in this series traces back to that shift.

Every habit of modern science, such as testing an idea, measuring carefully and sharing results so others can check them, feels obvious today. It was not obvious for most of history. For well over a thousand years, many questions about nature were settled by quoting respected ancient writers. Between about 1543 and 1687, a group of thinkers changed that. This article explains who they were, what they discovered, how they worked and why it still matters.

In this article

The old picture of the world · why change became possible · astronomy · motion and gravity · the body and matter · new methods and institutions · four worked examples · key terms · common mistakes · practice questions and a quiz.

1.What Was the Scientific Revolution?

The Scientific Revolution is the name historians give to a period, roughly from the middle of the 1500s to the end of the 1600s, when European scholars developed new ways of studying nature and replaced many older ideas. A revolution here means a deep change in thinking, not a sudden event. It took about 150 years, and it built on work from many earlier cultures.

It is worth being honest about one point: historians do not all agree on the exact start and end dates, or even on whether "revolution" is the best word. Some prefer to describe a long, gradual change. For O-Level study, the dates 1543 and 1687 are a useful frame, because they mark two landmark books: Copernicus's book on the Sun-centred universe and Newton's Principia.

The most important change was not one discovery. It was a change in what counts as a good reason to believe something. Before, "an ancient authority said so" was often enough. Afterwards, a claim was expected to survive observation, measurement and experiment.

2.The Old Picture: Knowledge by Authority

To see how big the change was, start with what most educated people believed in the 1400s. This picture came mainly from three ancient sources.

  • Aristotle (4th century BCE, Greece) taught that the Earth sits still at the centre of the universe, and that heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones.
  • Ptolemy (2nd century CE, Alexandria) wrote the Almagest, a detailed mathematical model in which the Sun, Moon and planets circle a motionless Earth. To match what astronomers saw, the model used extra small circles called epicycles.
  • Galen (2nd century CE, a Greek physician in the Roman world) wrote much of what doctors studied about the human body. Much of his anatomy was based on animals rather than on human bodies.

These were serious, clever works, and for centuries they were useful. The problem was not that earlier scholars were foolish. The problem was that their conclusions were often treated as finished, so people checked books against other books more often than against nature.

Important roots from other cultures. The revolution did not appear from nowhere. Ibn al-Haytham (born about 965 CE, working in Cairo) wrote the Book of Optics, which studied light and vision using carefully designed experiments, and Latin translations later reached European scholars. The number system we use today (the Hindu-Arabic numerals) and the word "algebra" also reached Europe through the Arabic-language scholarship of the Middle Ages. Learning how to test ideas was a global inheritance.

3.Why Change Became Possible

Several developments helped new ideas spread and be checked.

  • The printing press. Around 1450, Gutenberg's printing in Europe made books cheaper. Diagrams and tables could be copied exactly, so many readers could examine the same evidence.
  • Better instruments. Telescopes and microscopes, and improved clocks, scales and pumps, let people see and measure things that no ancient author had seen.
  • Navigation and trade. Long sea voyages needed better maps, star tables and timekeeping, which pushed astronomy and mathematics forward.
  • Mathematics. Describing nature with numbers and equations made predictions precise enough to be tested.

4.A New Map of the Sky

Astronomy was the first big battleground. The question was simple: does the Sun move around the Earth, or the Earth around the Sun?

Figure 1. The Earth-centred and Sun-centred models side by side. Watch how the orbiting bodies move.

Copernicus (1473–1543) was a Polish scholar who proposed in his 1543 book that the Sun, not the Earth, is the centre, and that the Earth is a planet that also spins. A model with the Sun at the centre is called heliocentric; one with the Earth at the centre is geocentric. Copernicus kept the old idea that orbits are perfect circles, so his model still needed small extra circles and was not much more accurate than Ptolemy's at first. Its power was that it explained the planets' wandering paths more simply.

Tycho Brahe (1546–1601), a Danish astronomer, did not accept the Sun-centred model, but he made the most precise naked-eye measurements of planet positions then available. Careful data turned out to matter more than his own theory.

Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), who worked with Tycho's records, found that the data would not fit circles. Planets move on ellipses (stretched circles). He published three laws:

  • Each planet orbits the Sun on an ellipse, with the Sun at one special point called a focus.
  • A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so a planet moves faster when it is closer to the Sun.
  • The square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun.
Figure 2. Kepler's second law: a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times, so it moves fastest when closest to the Sun.

Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) did not invent the telescope; spectacle makers in the Netherlands built early versions around 1608. But in 1609–1610 he improved one and used it to study the sky. He reported mountains on the Moon, many more stars than the eye can see, and four moons circling Jupiter. The moons of Jupiter showed that not everything in the sky circles the Earth. Later in 1610 he saw that Venus shows phases like the Moon, which fitted the Sun-centred picture and caused serious trouble for the simple Ptolemaic model.

Worked example 1: Kepler's third law

Question: Mars is about 1.52 times farther from the Sun than Earth is. Estimate how long Mars takes to orbit the Sun.

Step 1. Use distances in astronomical units (AU), where Earth's average distance is 1 AU, and periods in Earth years. Then the law is T² = a³.

Step 2. For Mars, a = 1.52, so a³ = 1.52 × 1.52 × 1.52 ≈ 3.51.

Step 3. T = √3.51 ≈ 1.87 years.

Check: Mars's actual period is about 1.88 Earth years, so the law works very well.

6.Motion, Force and Gravity

Aristotle taught that heavy things fall faster than light things. Galileo argued, using careful reasoning and measurements, that if air resistance is removed, objects fall at the same rate. A popular story says he proved this by dropping balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa, but historians doubt it ever happened that way. His real measurements used balls rolling down gentle ramps, which slowed the motion enough to time it. In 1971 an Apollo 15 astronaut dropped a hammer and a feather together on the Moon, where there is almost no air, and they landed at the same time.

Worked example 2: Galileo's ramp

Finding: A ball starting from rest and rolling down a ramp covers a distance proportional to the square of the time.

Step 1. Let the distance after 1 time unit be 1 unit.

Step 2. After 2 time units the distance is 2² = 4 units; after 3 units it is 3² = 9 units.

Step 3. The distance covered during each separate time unit is 1, then 4 − 1 = 3, then 9 − 4 = 5. These are the odd numbers 1, 3, 5, … The ball is speeding up steadily.

Galileo also studied the pendulum. For small swings, the time of one swing depends mainly on the pendulum's length and hardly at all on how wide the swing is or how heavy the weight is. Pendulums later became the basis of accurate clocks.

Figure 4. A pendulum. Try this at home with string and a small weight: count 10 swings, change only the length, and compare.

Isaac Newton (1642–1727) brought these ideas together in his 1687 book Principia Mathematica. He described three laws of motion:

  • An object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, unless a force acts on it.
  • The force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration (F = m × a).
  • When one object pushes on another, the second pushes back equally in the opposite direction.

He also proposed universal gravitation: every object with mass attracts every other object, and the pull gets weaker as the distance grows. The same law explained why things fall on Earth and why the Moon and planets stay in orbit. For the first time, the heavens and the Earth followed the same rules. (Newton's name is linked to a falling apple, but the story matters less than the idea.)

Worked example 3: Newton's second law

Question: A trolley of mass 2 kg speeds up at 3 m/s². What net force acts on it?

Step 1. Write the law: F = m × a.

Step 2. Substitute: F = 2 × 3 = 6.

Answer: 6 newtons (N). Doubling the mass would need double the force for the same acceleration.

7.The Human Body and the Nature of Matter

Andreas Vesalius (1514–1564) published a richly illustrated book on human anatomy in 1543. He based it on dissecting human bodies himself and corrected several mistakes in Galen's teaching. William Harvey (1578–1657) studied the heart and published in 1628 that the heart pumps blood in a circuit around the body. This also builds on earlier descriptions of blood moving through the lungs, such as that of Ibn al-Nafis in the 13th century.

Robert Boyle (1627–1691) used an air pump to study gases. He found that, for a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, pressure and volume change in opposite directions. Microscopes opened a further world: Robert Hooke published detailed drawings in 1665, and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek described tiny living things in water in the 1670s.

Worked example 4: Boyle's law

Question: A gas at 100 kPa fills a 6 L container. It is squeezed to 3 L at the same temperature. What is the new pressure?

Step 1. Boyle's law: P₁ × V₁ = P₂ × V₂.

Step 2. 100 × 6 = P₂ × 3, so 600 = 3 × P₂.

Step 3. P₂ = 600 ÷ 3 = 200 kPa.

Check: The volume halved, so the pressure doubled, as the law predicts.

8.New Methods of Finding Out

Two thinkers argued about how knowledge should be built. Francis Bacon (1561–1626), in Novum Organum (1620), argued that knowledge should be built up from careful, systematic observation and experiment instead of from old authority. René Descartes (1596–1650), in his Discourse on Method (1637), stressed doubting everything that can be doubted and using clear reasoning and mathematics. Bacon's side is often called empiricism (knowledge from experience) and Descartes's rationalism (knowledge from reason). Real scientists, including Galileo and Newton, used both: mathematics to make predictions and experiments to test them.

9.New Institutions: Science Becomes a Shared Effort

Ideas spread faster once there were places to share them. The Royal Society in London began in 1660 as a group that met to watch experiments and discuss results. In 1665 it began publishing Philosophical Transactions, one of the first scientific journals. Other countries formed similar societies. Because results were written down and published, other people could repeat an experiment and check it. This habit of public checking is the ancestor of today's peer review, although the formal modern system came much later.

11.Common Mistakes and Myths

  • "Galileo invented the telescope." No. He improved it and was among the first to use it for astronomy.
  • "Educated people once thought the Earth was flat." Not so; scholars in Europe and the Islamic world knew the Earth was round long before this period. The dispute was about whether the Earth moves.
  • "Copernicus's model instantly beat Ptolemy's." Not at first. Kepler's ellipses were needed to make it truly accurate.
  • "The revolution happened overnight." It took about 150 years, with many people contributing, and several of them were churchmen or religious believers.
  • "A theory is just a guess." In science a theory is a strongly tested explanation.
  • "The scientific method is one fixed list of steps." It is better seen as a loop of observing, testing and revising.
Real-World Example

Galileo's support for the Sun-centred model brought him into conflict with powerful religious and academic authorities of his time, who held the older, Earth-centred view. In 1633 he was tried, forced to state that he abandoned the idea, and kept under house arrest for the rest of his life. His telescope observations were later confirmed to be correct. His case shows how hard it can be for new evidence to overturn established authority, and why it matters to build institutions around open, evidence-based testing.

12.Why It Still Matters

The methods developed in this period lie behind modern physics, medicine and engineering: measuring carefully, writing results down clearly, and letting evidence correct us. When you check a claim against evidence, or accept that a favourite idea might be wrong, you are using a way of thinking that took centuries to become normal.

13.Summary

  • The Scientific Revolution (about 1543–1687) was a long change in how people studied nature.
  • The old picture came from Aristotle, Ptolemy and Galen; new evidence and instruments challenged it.
  • Copernicus, Kepler and Galileo reshaped astronomy; Newton united motion and gravity.
  • Vesalius, Harvey, Boyle and the microscope users changed the study of life and matter.
  • Bacon and Descartes argued about methods; societies and journals let results be shared and checked.
  • The deepest change was in what counts as a good reason to believe a claim.

14.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. Name two sources of the old picture of nature before the revolution.

Any two of: Aristotle (physics and an Earth-centred universe), Ptolemy (astronomy), Galen (medicine and anatomy).

2. What is the difference between geocentric and heliocentric?

Geocentric means Earth-centred; heliocentric means Sun-centred.

3. Why did Kepler's work improve on Copernicus's model?

He showed that planetary orbits are ellipses, not circles, which fitted Tycho's measurements without needing extra epicycles.

4. A planet has an average distance of 4 AU from the Sun. Using T² = a³, what is its orbital period?

a³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64, so T = √64 = 8 years.

5. A gas at 50 kPa is compressed from 8 L to 2 L at constant temperature. What is the new pressure?

P₂ = (50 × 8) ÷ 2 = 200 kPa.

6. Why was the Royal Society's publishing of experiments important?

It let other people read, repeat and check the experiments, so results could be tested instead of simply trusted.

For Teachers and Parents

Suggested use: read sections 1–3 for context, then try the pendulum activity (string, a small weight, a stopwatch). Ask students to change only one variable at a time and to predict the result before testing. Discussion prompts: What kind of evidence would change your mind about a belief? Why do scientists publish their methods? The material is general education for students up to age 18; dates and attributions follow widely accepted history, and historians' disagreements are noted where they matter.
