Adapting Writing to Different Forms, Audiences and Contexts

A comprehensive Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 unit on transforming information and ideas so that purpose, audience, form, register, tone and context work together without losing meaning.

Adapting writing means changing the way information is communicated so that it works for a particular purpose, audience, form and context. The facts may remain substantially the same, but the vocabulary, tone, structure, level of explanation, examples and sentence patterns may need to change. This is a central practical skill in directed writing because a successful response is not simply a copied source passage placed under a new heading.

1. Unit Purpose

This unit teaches you how to take an idea, set of facts, source passage or planned response and reshape it for a different communicative situation. You will learn to preserve essential meaning while deliberately changing the features that the new audience, purpose, form and context require.

EDUSAMBAM principle

PRESERVE THE MESSAGE → CHANGE THE COMMUNICATION
Keep essential information accurate, but reshape its selection, order, wording, tone and presentation for the new task.

2. What Does Adaptation Mean?

Adaptation is the deliberate modification of a piece of communication so that it fits a new situation. A writer may need to change the same basic information into a speech, article, report, letter or email. Adaptation is therefore more than replacing a few words. It involves making a series of connected decisions about what the new reader needs and how the new form normally communicates.

ElementQuestion
ContentWhich information must be retained?
PurposeWhat must the new text achieve?
AudienceWho will read or hear it?
FormWhat kind of text is required?
RegisterHow formal should the language be?
ToneWhat attitude or emotional quality is appropriate?
ContextWhat situation surrounds the communication?

3. Why Adaptation Is Difficult

A source passage may have been written for one situation while the examination task requires another. A factual source may use neutral explanatory language, whereas the response may need to persuade a school audience, advise a community, or inform a group through a speech. The writer must therefore make changes without inventing unsupported facts or accidentally changing the original meaning.

  • The original text may contain information that is unnecessary for the new audience.
  • The new form may require a different structure.
  • The relationship between writer and reader may require a different register.
  • Some facts may need explanation while others can be assumed.
  • The new purpose may require emphasis on particular details.

4. The Four Core Decisions: Purpose, Audience, Form and Context

Before adapting anything, identify four variables. Purpose answers why; audience answers for whom; form answers what kind of text; context answers what situation. These variables interact. A speech to younger students about online safety and a formal report to a school principal may use some of the same facts, but they should not sound alike.

Quick test

If you can describe the task as “I am writing a ___ for ___ so that ___ in the context of ___,” you have a useful adaptation starting point.

5. Identifying the Target Audience

Audience is not simply a label such as “students” or “the public”. Consider age, knowledge, relationship with the writer, expectations, interests and likely concerns. A younger reader may need technical vocabulary explained; a specialist reader may not. A principal may expect concise recommendations, while a group of students may respond better to direct examples and engaging questions.

  • Age and educational level
  • Existing knowledge
  • Relationship to the writer
  • Interests and concerns
  • Expectations of the text type
  • Likely reaction to the message

6. Audience Changes What You Explain

The same fact can require different amounts of explanation. If an audience already understands a concept, excessive explanation may slow the text. If the audience is unfamiliar with it, a bare technical statement may be confusing. Effective adaptation therefore changes not only vocabulary but also the depth and sequence of explanation.

Compare

For specialists: “The system uses two-factor authentication.”
For younger learners: “Two-factor authentication adds a second security check, such as a code sent to your phone, after you enter your password.”

7. Purpose Controls Selection

A source may contain ten useful facts, but the new task may require only five. Selecting everything can produce an unfocused response. Selecting nothing is equally problematic. Ask what information directly helps achieve the new purpose.

PurposeLikely emphasis
InformRelevant facts, clear explanation, accurate detail
PersuadeEvidence, benefits, consequences, compelling reasoning
AdvisePractical steps, risks, solutions and clarity
ReportFindings, evidence, organisation and recommendations
Speak to an audienceKey messages, accessibility, engagement and memorable points

8. Form Is More Than a Label

Calling something a “speech” or “report” does not make it one. The form affects structure, opening, closing, paragraphing, headings, address to the audience, level of directness and language. A report may use headings and recommendations; a speech may address listeners directly and use rhetorical techniques; a formal letter normally establishes the recipient relationship and reason for writing early.

9. Register: Matching the Social Situation

Register is the level and style of language appropriate to a particular relationship and situation. Formal register often uses careful wording and avoids casual expressions. Informal register may use contractions and conversational phrasing where appropriate. Neutral register sits between these extremes. The important principle is appropriateness, not the belief that formal language is always better.

Too informal in a formal contextMore suitable alternative
“Loads of people are fed up.”“Many people are dissatisfied.”
“Kids need to chill out online.”“Young people need to behave responsibly online.”
“It’s a really bad idea.”“It is unlikely to be an effective approach.”

10. Tone: The Attitude the Reader Hears

Tone is the attitude conveyed by the writing. It may be concerned, respectful, enthusiastic, critical, encouraging, urgent, sympathetic or cautiously balanced. Tone is created through vocabulary, sentence patterns, punctuation, examples and what the writer chooses to emphasise. A formal text can still be warm; an informal text can still be serious.

11. Register and Tone Are Not the Same

Register describes the level and style appropriate to the situation, whereas tone describes the writer’s attitude. A formal letter can have a respectful and firm tone. A speech can have an informal register while maintaining a serious tone. Keeping these concepts separate helps prevent mechanical writing.

Remember

Register = how the relationship and situation shape language.
Tone = what attitude the language communicates.

12. Preserving Meaning During Adaptation

The most important safeguard is to identify the core meaning before rewriting. Separate essential facts from optional wording. A writer may change the order, vocabulary and sentence structure, but must not silently turn a possibility into a certainty, change a number, reverse a cause and effect relationship, or add a claim that the source does not support.

  • Mark essential facts.
  • Circle relationships such as cause, contrast and consequence.
  • Record important quantities, names, dates or conditions accurately.
  • Identify opinions separately from factual statements.
  • Check the adapted text against the source before finishing.

13. Source Facts, Inferences and New Ideas

When adapting source material, distinguish between what the source states, what can reasonably be inferred, and what you personally want to add. An inference may be acceptable when the task allows interpretation, but it should not be presented as an explicit source fact. Unsupported inventions weaken accuracy.

14. Selecting Information from a Source

Use a relevance filter. For each piece of information ask: Does it directly help the new purpose? Does the audience need it? Does it support a required point? Does it provide evidence or explanation? If not, it may be unnecessary.

Selection grid

KEEP: information that fulfils the task.
ADAPT: information that is useful but needs explanation or reordering.
LEAVE OUT: accurate information that is irrelevant to the new purpose.

15. Avoiding Source Dumping

Source dumping occurs when a writer transfers a large block of information with little selection or transformation. Even accurate content can fail to communicate effectively if the writer has not decided what matters most. A strong adaptation integrates selected information into a new structure.

16. Paraphrasing Without Distorting Meaning

Paraphrasing means expressing information in your own wording while preserving the original meaning. Change structure as well as vocabulary. Simply replacing a few words with synonyms often produces awkward writing and can still remain too close to the source.

Weak

“The project was extremely beneficial to the local population.” → “The project was extremely advantageous to the local population.”

Better

“The project brought clear benefits to local residents, particularly by improving access to essential services.”

17. Changing Structure, Not Just Words

Real adaptation often requires moving information. A source might begin with background history, while a speech may need to begin with a problem that immediately matters to listeners. A report may organise the same information under findings and recommendations. Structural change is often more important than synonym substitution.

18. Formal to Informal Adaptation

When moving from formal to informal writing, the aim is not to make every sentence slangy. Use natural conversational vocabulary, appropriate contractions and direct engagement while preserving clarity and respect.

Transformation

Formal: “Students are advised to maintain a consistent revision schedule.”
Informal: “Try to keep a regular revision routine so you do not have to panic at the last minute.”

19. Informal to Formal Adaptation

Formal adaptation requires more than removing slang. Replace conversational expressions with precise wording, remove unnecessary personal fillers, control contractions where appropriate, and make relationships between ideas explicit.

Transformation

Informal: “A lot of people think the plan is a bad idea because it will cost loads.”
Formal: “Many people consider the proposal unsuitable because it would involve substantial costs.”

20. Neutral to Persuasive Adaptation

A neutral text presents information without strongly directing the reader. A persuasive version selects evidence, emphasises consequences, anticipates concerns and makes a clear case. Persuasion must still be reasoned rather than exaggerated.

Transformation

Neutral: “The school could introduce a later start time.”
Persuasive: “Introducing a slightly later start time could help students arrive more alert and prepared to learn, making it a practical change worth considering.”

21. Adapting for a Speech

A speech is written to be heard. The writer should therefore make the message accessible in real time. Direct address, rhetorical questions, repetition for emphasis, memorable phrasing and clear signposting can help. Sentences should generally be easy to follow when spoken.

  • Address listeners appropriately.
  • Make the central message clear early.
  • Use spoken-friendly sentence lengths.
  • Use rhetorical questions or repetition deliberately.
  • Signal movement between points.
  • End with a memorable final message or call to action.

22. Adapting for an Article

An article generally needs an engaging opening, clear development and a conclusion that gives the reader a sense of completion. Depending on the task, it may combine information with explanation, examples, commentary or persuasion. The writing should remain focused rather than becoming a collection of unrelated facts.

23. Adapting for a Report

A report is usually functional and information-led. The reader should be able to locate the main findings, problems and recommendations efficiently. Headings may be appropriate when the task or form expects them. Personal storytelling and decorative language are usually less important than clarity, evidence and useful conclusions.

Report route

Purpose → Findings → Evidence/Explanation → Problems or implications → Recommendations

24. Adapting for a Formal Letter or Email

A formal letter or email must establish the relationship with the recipient and the reason for communication. The writer should state the issue clearly, provide relevant details, explain what is being requested or proposed, and close appropriately. Politeness does not mean vague writing; a respectful request can still be precise.

25. Adapting for a Personal or Informal Message

An informal message may use a more conversational register and personal voice. However, informality should still serve the purpose. If the writer needs to explain several points, clear paragraphing remains useful. “Friendly” does not mean careless or confusing.

26. Changing the Opening

Openings are strongly shaped by form and audience. A report may identify its purpose immediately. A speech may greet and engage listeners. An article may begin with a striking question, observation or situation. A formal letter may state its reason for writing. The same source information can therefore begin in very different ways.

FormPossible opening function
SpeechGreet, engage, establish the issue
ArticleCreate interest and introduce the subject
ReportState purpose or situation clearly
Formal letter/emailState reason for writing
Advice pieceIdentify the problem and promise useful guidance

27. Changing the Conclusion

The ending should match the purpose. A persuasive speech may call listeners to action. A report may state recommendations. An article may leave the reader with a considered final thought. A formal request may identify the action expected from the recipient. Simply writing “In conclusion” does not create an effective ending.

28. Adapting Level of Vocabulary

Vocabulary should be precise but accessible. Specialist terminology can be retained when the audience needs it, but it may need explanation for a general audience. Conversely, replacing every technical term with a vague everyday word can reduce accuracy. Choose the most precise word that the intended audience can understand.

29. Adapting Sentence Structures

Sentence structure can alter pace, emphasis and accessibility. A report may favour concise factual sentences. A speech may combine short emphatic statements with longer explanatory sentences. An article may vary structures to create interest. Adaptation should change structure deliberately rather than mechanically shortening every sentence.

30. Adapting Examples and Illustrations

Examples should be recognisable and useful to the target audience. An example for primary-aged learners may use school or family situations. An O Level audience may respond to study, technology, social issues or everyday responsibilities. The example must remain relevant to the actual point.

31. Adapting Emotional Distance

Some forms require a more personal relationship than others. A formal report normally maintains professional distance. A speech may speak directly to listeners. A personal letter may openly express feelings. Emotional distance should be controlled rather than accidental.

32. Changing Viewpoint and Voice Carefully

A task may require first-person or third-person writing, but changing viewpoint can also change responsibility and certainty. If the source says that “researchers found”, do not rewrite it as “I proved” unless the task legitimately gives the writer that role. Voice must be adapted without manufacturing authority.

33. Maintaining Factual Accuracy After Transformation

Adaptation creates opportunities for accidental errors. When sentences are reorganised, numbers can be misplaced, conditions can disappear and relationships can be reversed. After drafting, compare the adapted response with the source using a fact-check pass separate from the style check.

34. A Four-Column Adaptation Method

A practical planning method is to divide the task into four columns: source information, audience need, new form and language choice. This forces the writer to make adaptation decisions before drafting.

Source informationAudience needForm requirementLanguage choice
Key factUnderstand why it mattersSpeech pointDirect explanation
ProblemSee its consequenceReport findingPrecise factual wording
RecommendationKnow what to doLetter requestRespectful imperative/request
EvidenceTrust the claimArticle supportClear attribution

35. Worked Example: Source to Speech

Source idea: “Regular sleep supports concentration and memory, while insufficient sleep can reduce alertness.”

Adapted speech: “Think about how difficult it is to concentrate when you have barely slept. Regular sleep gives your brain the opportunity to recover, which can help you stay alert and remember what you study. So if you want to improve your revision, do not treat sleep as wasted time.”

36. Worked Example: Source to Report

Source idea: “The school library is heavily used during examination periods, but students report difficulty finding quiet study spaces.”

Adapted report wording: “Library use increases substantially during examination periods. However, student feedback indicates that the existing space does not always provide sufficient quiet study areas. The school could therefore consider extending supervised study hours or creating an additional designated quiet zone.”

37. Worked Example: Source to Formal Letter

Source idea: “The local park has damaged lighting and litter has increased.”

Adapted letter wording: “I am writing to request attention to two problems affecting the local park: damaged lighting and increasing litter. These issues are reducing the usefulness of the area and may create safety concerns for residents, particularly in the evening.”

38. Worked Example: Source to Article

Source idea: “Walking to school can provide physical activity and may reduce traffic around school entrances.”

Adapted article opening: “What if part of the solution to the morning traffic problem began before students even reached the school gate? For pupils who live nearby, walking to school can provide useful daily exercise while also reducing the number of cars arriving at the same time.”

39. Comparing Successful and Unsuccessful Adaptation

A successful adaptation changes what needs to change and protects what needs to stay. An unsuccessful version may copy the source, use the wrong register, ignore the new audience, omit required content or invent information.

UnsuccessfulSuccessful
Copies source structure blindlyReorganises information for the new purpose
Changes words but not formChanges structure and language together
Uses one register everywhereMatches register to relationship and context
Includes every source detailSelects relevant information
Adds unsupported claimsKeeps factual boundaries clear

40. Common Mistake: Synonym Swapping

Replacing individual words with synonyms is not enough. It can also produce unnatural collocations or change meaning. For example, “strong evidence” should not automatically become “powerful proof” merely because the words appear similar. Adapt the whole sentence and its communicative function.

41. Common Mistake: Making Everything More Formal

Students sometimes assume that sophisticated writing is always better. It is not. A speech to young people can become less effective if every sentence sounds like a legal document. The correct question is not “Can I make this more formal?” but “What language best suits this audience and purpose?”

42. Common Mistake: Making Everything Conversational

The opposite mistake is treating all writing as conversation. A formal report, complaint or professional email can lose credibility if it contains slang, casual fillers or exaggerated emotional language. Adaptation requires control rather than a permanent move towards informality.

43. Common Mistake: Changing the Meaning

A writer may accidentally strengthen a source statement. “May improve” becomes “will improve”; “some people” becomes “everyone”; “is associated with” becomes “causes”. These are not stylistic changes. They change the claim. Preserve degrees of certainty and logical relationships.

44. Common Mistake: Ignoring the Relationship with the Reader

A request to a friend and a request to a head teacher can concern the same subject but require different language. The writer should consider authority, familiarity, politeness, expected response and social distance.

45. Common Mistake: Forgetting the Form

A response can contain excellent English and still be poorly adapted if it does not behave like the required form. A report that reads like a personal story, or a speech that never addresses listeners, may not meet the communicative demands of the task.

46. The Adaptation Editing Sequence

Edit an adapted response in a deliberate order. First check task coverage and factual accuracy. Then check audience and purpose. Next examine form, structure and register. Only after these larger decisions should you polish vocabulary, sentence variety, grammar, spelling and punctuation.

EDUSAMBAM editing order

CONTENT → PURPOSE → AUDIENCE → FORM → STRUCTURE → REGISTER/TONE → LANGUAGE → ACCURACY

47. A Rapid Examination Method

Under time pressure, use a compact four-stage method. Spend a short period identifying the task variables; extract and classify the source information; create a form-specific paragraph route; then draft while continuously checking the audience and purpose.

  • 1. Box the purpose.
  • 2. Underline the audience.
  • 3. Circle the required form.
  • 4. Mark essential source points.
  • 5. Decide the paragraph sequence.
  • 6. Draft with the new audience in mind.
  • 7. Run a final adaptation check.

48. The 30-Second Adaptation Question Set

If time is very limited, ask: Who am I writing to? Why am I writing? What form am I using? What must I include? What should this text sound like? These five questions can prevent major mismatches before drafting begins.

49. Integrated Practice: One Message, Four Forms

Imagine the central message is that students should use school technology responsibly. A speech might open by addressing students and asking them to consider consequences. An article might explore benefits and risks. A report might present observed problems and recommendations. A formal letter might request a policy change. The core issue remains, but the communication route changes.

50. How to Check Whether an Adaptation Works

Read the response as the intended reader. Does the opening make sense immediately? Are unfamiliar ideas explained? Does the language sound natural for the situation? Can the reader identify what they are being asked to understand, believe or do? If the response sounds like the source rather than like the required form, adaptation is incomplete.

51. The Audience Removal Test

Temporarily hide the task and ask a classmate or yourself to identify the intended audience and purpose from the writing alone. If the answer is impossible, the response may not contain enough audience-sensitive or purpose-sensitive signals. This is a useful diagnostic, not a requirement that every text explicitly name its audience.

52. The Source Removal Test

After drafting, remove the source from view and read your response. Does it function as an independent piece of writing? If it still reads like notes copied from another text, restructure it. The goal is to create a new, coherent communication rather than a disguised source summary.

53. The Adaptation Balance

Strong adaptation balances four things: faithfulness to source information, fitness for purpose, accessibility for the audience and authenticity of the chosen form. Overemphasising one can damage another. Total source fidelity without adaptation produces a poor response; extreme creativity without factual control produces distortion.

DimensionToo littleBalancedToo much
Source fidelityInvented or distortedAccurateCopied mechanically
Audience focusInaccessibleAppropriateOver-simplified
FormGenericRecognisable and purposefulArtificially formulaic
VoiceFlat or unsuitableControlledOver-performed

54. EDUSAMBAM ADAPT Framework

Remember ADAPT

A — Analyse the task
Identify purpose, audience, form and context.

D — Decide what matters
Select essential source information.

A — Adjust the structure
Reorder information for the new form.

P — Personalise the language
Adapt register, tone, vocabulary and sentence patterns.

T — Test the result
Check accuracy, relevance, form and audience fit.

55. Cambridge O Level Connection

This unit brings together the practical writing demands developed across the preceding units: organising ideas effectively, choosing vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context, maintaining register, and controlling grammar, spelling and punctuation. Directed writing especially requires the writer to use source information selectively and shape it for a specified audience, purpose and form.

56. Mastery Checklist

  • I can identify the purpose of a writing task.
  • I can identify the intended audience and their likely needs.
  • I can recognise what the required form expects.
  • I can select relevant source information.
  • I can distinguish source facts from my own additions.
  • I can paraphrase without changing meaning.
  • I can reorganise source material instead of copying its structure.
  • I can adapt register appropriately.
  • I can control tone deliberately.
  • I can change vocabulary for different audiences.
  • I can change sentence structure for accessibility and effect.
  • I can write openings and conclusions appropriate to form.
  • I can adapt a message into a speech, article, report or letter/email.
  • I can preserve important facts, conditions and degrees of certainty.
  • I can edit for content, purpose, audience, form, structure, language and accuracy.

57. Final Self-Assessment

Give yourself a score from 1 to 5 for each area: source selection, audience awareness, purpose, form, register, tone, paraphrasing, structure, vocabulary and accuracy. Any score of 1 or 2 identifies a skill to practise. Improvement becomes easier when you diagnose the exact adaptation decision that is weak rather than simply deciding that the whole piece is “bad”.

58. Final Takeaway

EDUSAMBAM takeaway

Good adaptation does not change everything. It changes the right things.

Keep the essential message accurate. Change the structure, language, tone, explanation and emphasis so that the new text genuinely belongs to its new audience, purpose, form and context.
