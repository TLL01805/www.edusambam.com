Flags of the World: Reading the Symbols

How to read a flag: its parts, colours and symbols, flag families, the flags of Pakistan and South Asia, and how and why flags change.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 20 min read | General Knowledge |Last updated: October 2026

A national flag has to be recognised in a second, from a distance, often while moving in the wind. That is why almost every colour, shape and symbol on a flag was chosen on purpose. This article teaches you how to "read" a flag the way a vexillologist (a person who studies flags) does: the parts of a flag, what colours and symbols usually mean, the families that flags belong to, how flags are designed and how they change, with Pakistan, India and the rest of South Asia as close-up case studies. Countries, capitals and currencies are covered in a separate article; here the focus is only on the flags themselves.

In this article

The parts of a flag · colours and symbols · flag families · the Pakistan and India flags · South Asia at a glance · history and change · good flag design · flag etiquette · four worked examples · key terms · common mistakes · practice questions and a quiz.

1.The Parts of a Flag

Flags have their own vocabulary. Knowing it lets you describe any flag exactly.

Figure 1. The parts of a flag. The flag rises up the pole in the animation. (This is a generic flag, not a national one.)
  • Hoist: the edge nearest the pole. Fly: the edge farthest from the pole.
  • Field: the background colour or pattern.
  • Canton: the top corner nearest the pole, often holding a smaller design (as on Australia's and New Zealand's flags).
  • Charge: a symbol placed on the field, such as a star, a wheel or an animal.

Most national flags are rectangles. Proportions (the ratio of length to width) differ: many flags are 2:3, some are 1:2, and Switzerland and the Vatican City have square flags.

The crescent and star are older than Islam as symbols: the crescent was used in the ancient world and in the Byzantine city of Constantinople. The Ottoman Empire adopted and popularised the crescent and star over centuries, and from there it became widely linked to Muslim-majority countries. The colours and layout of each flag still differ greatly.

4.Pakistan's Flag: A Case Study

Pakistan's national flag, with its dark green field, white vertical bar on the hoist side, and white crescent and star on the green, was adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 11 August 1947, three days before independence. It is based on the flag of the All-India Muslim League and was designed by Syed Amir-ud-Din Kedwai. The ratio is 3:2.

Figure 3. The proportions of Pakistan's flag (simplified drawing). The white bar is one quarter of the length.
ElementOfficial meaning
Dark green fieldThe Muslim majority
White vertical barReligious minorities
CrescentProgress
Five-pointed starLight and knowledge
Worked example 1: Measuring the flag

Question: A Pakistani flag is 90 cm long. Find its width, and the widths of the white bar and the green field.

Step 1. The ratio length : width is 3 : 2, so width = 90 × 2 ÷ 3 = 60 cm.

Step 2. The white bar is one quarter of the length: 90 ÷ 4 = 22.5 cm.

Step 3. The green field is the rest: 90 − 22.5 = 67.5 cm.

Answer: 60 cm wide; a 22.5 cm white bar and a 67.5 cm green field.

5.India's Flag: A Case Study

India's flag, the Tiranga (tricolour), was adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 22 July 1947. The design is based on one by Pingali Venkayya, with three equal horizontal bands of saffron, white and green and a navy-blue 24-spoke wheel, the Ashoka Chakra, in the centre of the white band. The ratio is 3:2.

ElementCommonly explained as
Saffron (top)Courage and sacrifice
White (middle)Truth and peace
Green (bottom)Growth and the fertility of the land
Ashoka ChakraThe wheel of law (dharma) and constant movement; its 24 spokes are often explained as the 24 hours of the day

7.Flag Styles Around the World

Tricolours

A tricolour is a flag built from three bands of colour. The Dutch flag is one of the oldest tricolours, and the French tricolour, adopted in 1794 after the Revolution, helped spread the style across the world.

Nordic crosses

The Nordic cross, with the upright part of the cross shifted towards the hoist, appears on the flags of Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland and Iceland. Denmark's flag, the Dannebrog, is considered the oldest national flag still in continuous use, and tradition links it to the year 1219.

8.How Flags Change

Flags feel permanent, but they change when a country's government, borders or identity change.

Figure 4. Some milestones in the history of flags. The marker moves down the timeline.
  • The United States adopted the Stars and Stripes in 1777, and has had 50 stars since 4 July 1960, after Hawaii became a state.
  • Canada adopted its maple leaf flag in 1965, replacing the earlier flag with a Union Jack.
  • South Africa introduced a new flag in 1994, at the end of apartheid.
  • Libya had a plain green flag from 1977 to 2011, the only national flag at the time with a single colour and no design, and restored an older design in 2011.
  • New Zealand held a national referendum in 2016 on a new flag and voted to keep its present one.
  • Afghanistan has changed its flag many times since the early 1900s. In 2021, after the Taliban took control of the government, a white flag bearing the Islamic declaration of faith began to be flown by the authorities, while other states' recognition of the government has varied.
"Every flag looks completely different from every other flag"

Some flags are so similar that even adults mix them up.

The fix: Several pairs of flags are almost identical. Indonesia and Monaco are both a plain red-over-white pair of bands, differing mainly in proportions (Indonesia is 2:3 and Monaco is 4:5). Romania and Chad are both blue-yellow-red vertical tricolours in nearly identical shades. Ireland and Côte d'Ivoire use the same three colours, green, white and orange, in exactly reversed order. When flags look alike, the exact shade and order of the colours is the only reliable way to tell them apart.

"A country's flag never changes once it is chosen"

This is a very common assumption.

The fix: Flags change whenever a country's political situation changes significantly. South Africa (1994), Libya (2011), Canada (1965) and Afghanistan (several times, most recently 2021) all show that a flag is a snapshot of a country's identity at a moment in time.

"A colour always means the same thing on every flag"

Colour guides can make it sound that way.

The fix: Meanings are chosen by each country. Green stands for Islam on Pakistan's flag, for fertility on India's, and for the land on Bangladesh's. Always look for the country's own explanation.

Worked example 2: Spot the difference

Question: Indonesia's flag is 2:3 and Monaco's is 4:5. If both are 10 cm tall, how wide is each?

Step 1. Ratio 2:3 means width = 3 ÷ 2 × height. Indonesia: 10 × 3 ÷ 2 = 15 cm.

Step 2. Ratio 4:5: Monaco: 10 × 5 ÷ 4 = 12.5 cm.

Answer: 15 cm and 12.5 cm. The Indonesian flag is a little wider, which is the main visible difference.

Worked example 3: Years of a flag design

Question: The 50-star US flag has been used since 1960. How many years is that in 2026?

Step 1. Subtract: 2026 − 1960 = 66.

Answer: 66 years, making it the longest-serving design of the US flag so far.

9.What Makes a Good Flag?

Vexillologists have written guidance on flag design. A well-known guide published in 2001 by the North American Vexillological Association (NAVA) lists five principles:

  1. Keep it simple. A child should be able to draw it from memory.
  2. Use meaningful symbolism. Every element should relate to what the flag stands for.
  3. Use two or three basic colours.
  4. No lettering or seals. Writing is hard to read from a distance.
  5. Be distinctive or be related. Avoid copying other flags, unless you want to show a connection.
Worked example 4: Decode a flag

Question: A flag has a white field with a red disc in the centre. What can you say about it?

Step 1. Colours: white and red, two basic colours.

Step 2. Symbol: a disc usually stands for the sun.

Step 3. Layout: a single central charge on a plain field.

Answer: It follows the rule of simplicity, and matches Japan's flag, whose red disc stands for the sun.

10.Flag Etiquette

Countries have their own rules, but several customs are common. The flag is raised briskly and lowered slowly and respectfully. It should never touch the ground, and it should not be used as clothing or decoration. A flag flown at half-mast (partway down the pole) is a sign of mourning. Check your own country's flag code for the exact rules.

Real-World Example

The next time you watch an international cricket match, a football tournament or the Olympics, try naming each flag before the on-screen graphic does. Using Section 7's families (tricolours, crosses, crescents, cantons), you can often narrow down a country even from a flag you have never seen before.

Did You Know?

The study of flags is called vexillology, from the Latin vexillum, a small flag or banner used by Roman military units. The word was coined in 1957 by the American Whitney Smith.

12.Common Mistakes

  • Assuming a colour always has the same meaning in every country.
  • Confusing look-alike flags: Indonesia and Monaco, Romania and Chad, Ireland and Côte d'Ivoire.
  • Thinking that the crescent and star belong only to the Ottoman Empire or only to Islam. The crescent is older than both.
  • Saying that all flags are rectangles. Nepal's is not, and Switzerland's is square.
  • Assuming flags never change.

13.Summary

  • A flag has parts (hoist, fly, field, canton, charge) and a ratio.
  • Colours and symbols usually have official meanings that vary from country to country.
  • Flags fall into families: tricolours, Nordic crosses, Union Jack cantons, crescent-and-star flags and the Pan-African, Pan-Arab and Pan-Slavic colour sets.
  • Pakistan's flag was adopted on 11 August 1947 and India's on 22 July 1947.
  • Nepal's flag is the only non-rectangular national flag.
  • Flags change when countries change; the Dannebrog is the oldest still in use.
