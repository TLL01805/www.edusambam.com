Hieroglyphs, Pictograms and Emojis: The Language of Images

From carved Egyptian signs to the emoji on your phone, people have always used pictures to share ideas. Learn how picture writing works, and where it can mislead.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 15 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | General Knowledge

Long before anyone wrote an alphabet, people drew pictures to say what they meant. Egyptian hieroglyphs, road signs and the emoji on your phone are all part of one long story: the human habit of using images to communicate.

Quick facts
  • The word hieroglyph comes from Greek words meaning "sacred carving".
  • Egyptian hieroglyphs mix picture signs and sound signs. They are a real writing system, not just drawings.
  • The Rosetta Stone (196 BCE) carries one text in three scripts. It helped scholars read hieroglyphs again in the 1820s.
  • The word emoji is Japanese: e means "picture" and moji means "character".
  • The official Unicode list now has close to 4,000 emoji.

1.Pictures Before Letters

The oldest human messages were not words. They were pictures: handprints and animals painted on cave walls, marks scratched on bone, shapes pressed into clay. A picture has a big advantage. You do not need to know a language to see that a drawing shows a bird or a river.

Over time, communities began to use fixed pictures for fixed meanings. Then something clever happened. People began to use pictures not only for things but also for sounds. That step turned simple drawings into true writing. In this article we follow that story from Ancient Egypt to the smartphone.

2.What Are Hieroglyphs?

Egyptian hieroglyphs are the picture-based writing of Ancient Egypt. The earliest examples date from about 3200 BCE, and the script stayed in use for more than 3,000 years. Hieroglyphs were carved on temple walls, tombs, statues and obelisks, and painted on papyrus and coffins. Egyptians called them "the words of the gods", and used them mainly for religious and royal writing.

Hieroglyphs look like small, neat drawings of birds, people, animals, plants and tools. But they are not just a "picture code". A scribe could write any word of the Egyptian language with them, including names, prayers, laws and letters.

3.How Hieroglyphs Work: Three Kinds of Signs

Hieroglyphic writing uses three kinds of signs, often side by side in one word. This mix is the key to understanding the script.

Kind of signWhat it doesExample
Word sign (logogram)One picture stands for a whole word.A drawing of a house plan means "house". A sun disc means "sun".
Sound sign (phonogram)A picture stands for one or more sounds, not for the object.An owl stands for the sound "m". A water ripple stands for "n". A mouth stands for "r".
Silent meaning sign (determinative)Placed at the end of a word to show its category. It is not pronounced.A pair of walking legs shows that a word is about movement. A seated person shows a word about people.

Notice that the sound signs use the rebus principle: a picture is borrowed for its sound. Imagine writing the English word "belief" with a picture of a bee and a picture of a leaf. The pictures are not about insects or plants at all. They are used for their sounds. Egyptian scribes used this trick thousands of years ago.

Egyptian writing also had no spaces, no punctuation and, for the most part, no written vowels. A reader had to know the language well to fill the gaps. When modern scholars say the word "nefer" (beautiful), they add the vowel "e" only to make it easy to say. The Egyptian spelling used the consonants n-f-r.

4.How to Read a Line of Hieroglyphs

  • Direction can change. Hieroglyphs could be written in rows or in columns, and from right to left or left to right.
  • Follow the faces. Signs of people and animals face the beginning of the line. So if the birds look to the left, you read from the left.
  • Groups, not lines. Scribes arranged signs in neat blocks, so a small sign could sit above or below a tall one. The block is read top to bottom.
  • Cartouches. A royal name is written inside an oval ring called a cartouche. The ring marks the name of a king or queen.

5.More Than One Egyptian Script

Hieroglyphs were beautiful, but slow to draw. For everyday work, such as accounts, letters and records, scribes used faster, simpler scripts:

ScriptWhat it was
HieroglyphicThe formal picture script, used on monuments, temples and tombs.
HieraticA flowing, handwritten form of hieroglyphs, used mostly on papyrus with ink.
DemoticAn even more compact everyday script that developed later.
CopticThe late form of the Egyptian language, written with the Greek alphabet plus a few extra letters.

Reading and writing were skills of a small minority. Trained scribes studied for years and were respected members of society. The last known hieroglyphic inscription, at the temple of Philae, dates to 394 CE. After that, nobody could read the signs for about 1,400 years.

6.The Rosetta Stone: The Key That Unlocked Hieroglyphs

In 1799, soldiers of Napoleon's army working near the town of Rosetta (Rashid) in Egypt found a damaged slab of dark stone. It carried a decree from 196 BCE in honour of King Ptolemy V. The same text was written three times: in hieroglyphs, in Demotic and in Ancient Greek. Scholars could read Greek, so the Greek part gave them a way into the other two. The stone has been in the British Museum in London since 1802.

YearEvent
About 3200 BCEEarliest known hieroglyphic writing.
196 BCEThe decree on the Rosetta Stone is carved.
394 CELast known hieroglyphic inscription, at Philae.
1799The Rosetta Stone is found.
1814Thomas Young shows that hieroglyphs also spell foreign names by sound.
1822Jean-François Champollion announces his breakthrough in Paris.

How the code was cracked

Here is the idea behind Champollion's method, in simple steps.

1Use the Greek. The Greek text of the Rosetta Stone includes the royal name Ptolemy, and it is readable.
2Find the cartouches. In the hieroglyphic part, names of kings sit inside ovals. One of them must be "Ptolemy".
3Compare two names. The name of Queen Cleopatra was found on another monument. Signs for sounds that appear in both names, such as P, T and L, matched.
4Build a sound list. Each new royal name gave more sound signs.
5Use Coptic. Champollion knew Coptic, the later form of the Egyptian language. It showed him that the same sound signs were also used to spell ordinary Egyptian words, not just foreign names.

Today, many people in the world can read the writing of Ancient Egypt because of this chain of discoveries.

7.Other Writing That Began as Pictures

Egypt was not alone. Several writing systems around the world began with pictures, then slowly became more abstract.

SystemWhereRough startNotes
CuneiformMesopotamia (present-day Iraq)About 3400–3200 BCEBegan as pictures on clay. Later became wedge-shaped marks pressed with a reed.
Egyptian hieroglyphsEgyptAbout 3200 BCEWord signs, sound signs and silent meaning signs.
Chinese charactersChinaAbout 1200 BCE (oldest well-known examples)Many characters began as pictures. Still in daily use today.
Maya glyphsCentral AmericaOver 2,000 years agoCombine word signs and syllable signs.
Indus scriptSouth AsiaAbout 2600–1900 BCEStill not deciphered.

Chinese is the great survivor of this group. A few characters still look like what they mean: 日 (sun), 月 (moon), 山 (mountain) and 木 (tree). But most characters today combine a meaning part and a sound part, and few are simple pictures. This is the same mix of meaning and sound that we saw in hieroglyphs.

8.Pictograms Around You

A pictogram is a simple picture that stands for an object or an action. You see them every day: the symbol for a toilet, a no-smoking sign, a wheelchair sign for accessible entrances, or an arrow for an exit. Airports, hospitals, stations and road signs use them because they work across languages.

In the 1920s and 1930s, Otto Neurath and his team in Vienna developed Isotype, a system that used simple repeated pictures to show facts and statistics. Today, international standards (such as ISO 7001 for public information symbols) help make common symbols look similar in many countries.

Good pictograms follow a few rules: keep them simple, make them recognisable at a small size, and test them with real people. A picture that one designer finds obvious may confuse someone from another culture.

9.From Emoticons to Emoji

Early internet users added tiny faces made from keyboard characters, called emoticons. In 1982, computer scientist Scott Fahlman suggested using :-) to mark jokes and :-( for serious posts on a university message board. The idea spread widely.

In 1999, a designer named Shigetaka Kurita created the first set of 176 emoji for the mobile internet service of the Japanese company NTT DoCoMo. Each emoji was drawn on a tiny grid of 12 by 12 pixels. The set included weather symbols, hearts and facial expressions. It was so influential that New York's Museum of Modern Art added the original set to its collection in 2016.

YearMilestone
1982The first suggested text smiley :-) is posted online.
1999Kurita's original 176 emoji are released in Japan.
2010Emoji are added to the Unicode standard (Unicode 6.0).
2011Apple adds an emoji keyboard to the iPhone, and emoji spread worldwide.
2015Skin-tone options arrive. Oxford Dictionaries names the "face with tears of joy" emoji its Word of the Year.
2026Emoji 18.0 takes the official Unicode total to 3,972.

Do not confuse the word emoji with emotion. They look alike in English, but the Japanese word means "picture character". The word emoticon is the one that comes from "emotion" and "icon".

10.How Emoji Work on Your Phone

Every emoji is a character with its own number in Unicode, the worldwide standard that lets all phones and computers share text. The Unicode Consortium reviews new emoji proposals and approves a new set once a year. Fun fact: Unicode also includes over a thousand Egyptian hieroglyphs. So the oldest picture writing and the newest are stored in the same system.

Unicode only decides the code and the description. Each company, such as Apple, Google or Samsung, draws its own artwork. This is why the same emoji can look a little different on different phones. Some emoji are also built by joining several together. For example, the "woman astronaut" is a woman emoji joined to a rocket emoji by a hidden connector.

11.Are Emojis a Language?

Emoji are popular, but linguists generally do not call them a full language. A language has a large vocabulary, grammar rules that most speakers share, and the power to say almost anything. Emoji on their own have no fixed word order, no verb endings and no rules for building sentences. Most of the time they work alongside words, like a gesture or a tone of voice.

Egyptian hieroglyphsEmoji

One useful comparison is the silent meaning sign in hieroglyphs. It helps the reader understand the word. In the same way, a smiling or a crying emoji at the end of a message helps the reader understand the tone.

12.Same Picture, Different Meaning

Pictures seem universal, but their meanings depend on culture, age and context. That is the main weakness of picture communication.

PicturePossible meanings
🙏Please or thank you in Japan. Prayer, hope or thanks in many other places.
👍"Good" or "OK" in most places. In a few countries the hand gesture is rude, so the emoji can offend.
💀Death in older uses. Many young people now use it to mean "I am laughing so hard".

Hieroglyphs had the same problem. Scholars still debate the exact meaning of some signs, because the ancient readers who knew the full context are gone.

13.Why Pictures Work So Well

Psychologists have found that people often remember pictures better than words. This is called the picture superiority effect. A picture also needs no translation, can be understood quickly and works for people who cannot read well, such as young children.

But pictures have limits. They are good for simple, concrete ideas such as "water", "danger" or "happy". They are poor at abstract ideas, exact numbers, "if" and "because", and anything that needs precise detail. That is why even picture-based writing systems added sound signs, and why a legal contract is never written in emoji.

Real-World Example

Airports around the world use the same simple pictures for baggage, toilets and exits. A traveller who cannot read the local language can still find the way. But when the message is complex, such as "your flight is delayed because of fog and will leave at 6:40 pm", airports use words, because pictures alone cannot carry that much detail.

14.Worked Example: Reading an Emoji Message

What does this message mean?

A friend sends: 🏫 ❌ 🌧️ 🎉 📅

1Find the "word signs". School, a cross, rain, a party, a calendar.
2Look for relationships. The cross could mean "no", "closed" or "cancelled". It sits next to the school, so the first idea is "no school".
3Use the context. If your class group chat sent this on a stormy morning, it probably means: "School is closed because of the rain, so the party is moved to another day."
4Check the tone. If the friend added a sad face, they are probably disappointed. If they added a happy face, they are probably glad.

Without context, the same message could also mean "It rained at the school party". Emoji do not carry enough grammar to remove the doubt, so context does the job that grammar does in a full language.

Try a rebus

Each puzzle uses pictures for their sounds, the same trick that Egyptian scribes used.

1. An eye + a tin can = ?

2. A bee + a leaf = ?

Answers: "I can", and "belief".

15.Common Myths and Facts

MythHieroglyphs are just a picture code with no sounds.
FactThey use word signs, sound signs and silent meaning signs to write the Egyptian language.
MythAncient Egyptians only ever used hieroglyphs.
FactThey also used hieratic and Demotic for everyday writing, and Coptic later.
MythThe word "emoji" comes from "emotion".
FactIt is Japanese for "picture character".
MythEmoji are a universal language that everyone understands the same way.
FactMeanings change with culture, age, platform and context.

16.Use Pictures Wisely

  • Add tone, not content. Use emoji to show how you feel, and use words for the message itself.
  • Know your audience. A friendly emoji is fine with friends, but avoid it in a formal letter, an application or an exam answer.
  • Keep it short. Screen readers read each emoji aloud by name, so a long string of emoji is hard to listen to.
  • Check for another meaning. If an emoji could offend someone, choose words instead.
Key points to remember
  • Egyptian hieroglyphs combine word signs, sound signs and silent meaning signs.
  • The Rosetta Stone (196 BCE) carries one text in three scripts. Champollion announced his breakthrough in 1822.
  • Many early writing systems, such as cuneiform and Chinese, began as pictures.
  • Emoji began in Japan in 1999 and are now part of Unicode.
  • Emoji add tone to a message, but they are not a complete language, and meanings can differ between people.
For teachers and parents

Activity 1: let learners write their own name by sound, using the sign chart in this article (skip silent letters and most vowels). Then draw an oval around it, like a cartouche.

Activity 2: ask learners to design three pictograms for their school (for example, library, canteen and quiet zone). Test them on another class and see which ones people understand without words.

Discussion: when is a picture clearer than a sentence, and when is it not?
