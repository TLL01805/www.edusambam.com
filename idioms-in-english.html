Idioms in English: Meanings, Examples and Origins

Raining cats and dogs, a piece of cake, Achilles' heel. Learn what common idioms mean, where they come from and how to use them with confidence.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 24 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Vocabulary

"It's raining cats and dogs." "She let the cat out of the bag." "He kicked the bucket." If you take idioms literally, they make no sense, yet native speakers use them every day. Idioms add colour, humour and force to language, and they appear in speeches, novels, news reports and examination papers.

This guide explains what idioms are, how they differ from proverbs and phrasal verbs, where some famous ones come from, and how to use them correctly. Its main part gives 124 common idioms with their meanings and example sentences, grouped by theme, followed by a table of classical and literary origins, common mistakes, key points, ten practice questions and a teacher activity.

It pairs with Proverbs in English and Phrasal Verbs, and connects with Formal vs. Informal English and Common Errors in English. Spelling in this guide follows British English.

1.What Is an Idiom?

An idiom is a fixed phrase whose meaning cannot be worked out from the meanings of its separate words. Under the weather has nothing to do with the sky: it means "slightly ill". Because of this, idioms must be learned as whole units, as you learn single words.

  • Figurative meaning. The real meaning is different from the literal meaning.
  • Fixed wording. You say spill the beans, not spill the peas.
  • Cultural roots. Many come from history, farming, sailing, sport, religion and literature.
  • Informal tone. Many idioms suit speech and stories better than formal reports.

2.Idioms, Proverbs, Phrasal Verbs and Similes

TypeWhat it isExample
IdiomA fixed phrase with a figurative meaning; it is often only part of a sentenceto spill the beans
ProverbA complete sentence of traditional advice or truthA stitch in time saves nine.
Phrasal verbA verb with a particle that forms a new meaninggive up, look after
SimileA comparison using as or like; some are fixed idiomsas cool as a cucumber
MetaphorA direct comparison without as or likeTime is money.
ClichéAn expression that is overusedat the end of the day

3.How to Use Idioms Well

  • Learn the whole phrase with its exact words and the situation in which it is used.
  • Match the register. Use idioms freely in stories, speeches and speaking tests; use them carefully in formal reports.
  • Use one at a time. A single apt idiom adds force; a string of idioms sounds forced.
  • Do not translate idioms word by word from another language. An idiom in one language rarely matches in another.
  • Check the grammar. Some idioms change tense (he spilled the beans) but not their words.
  • Avoid tired idioms that are clichés in your writing.

13.Where Some Famous Idioms Come From

Many difficult idioms come from Greek and Roman history, myths, fables and the works of Shakespeare. Knowing the story makes them easier to remember.

IdiomOrigin
Achilles' heelGreek myth: the hero Achilles could be wounded only on his heel.
Pandora's boxGreek myth: Pandora opened a jar that released troubles into the world.
the Midas touchGreek myth: King Midas turned everything he touched into gold.
cross the RubiconIn 49 BC, Julius Caesar led his army across the Rubicon river, starting a civil war that could not be undone.
sour grapesAesop's fable of the fox who called the grapes sour because he could not reach them.
a Trojan horseThe wooden horse in the Greek story of the Trojan War.
a wild goose chaseUsed in Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet; it first described a kind of horse race.
a pound of fleshFrom Shakespeare's The Merchant of Venice, where a moneylender demands a pound of flesh.
the green-eyed monsterShakespeare's Othello calls jealousy "the green-eyed monster".
Catch-22From Joseph Heller's 1961 novel Catch-22.
the Sword of DamoclesAn ancient Greek story, retold by the Roman writer Cicero, of a courtier who sat beneath a sword hung by a single hair.

14.Common Mistakes with Idioms

Some idioms are often misheard and misspelled. These mistaken forms are sometimes called eggcorns.

MistakeCorrectWhy
for all intensive purposesfor all intents and purposesThe phrase means "in every important way".
case and pointcase in pointA "case in point" is a relevant example.
nip it in the buttnip it in the budA bud is the early stage of growth.
a mute pointa moot pointMoot means open to debate.
spill the peasspill the beansThe wording is fixed.
in the nick of the timein the nick of timeDo not add the.
on the tip of my headon the tip of my tongueThe idiom uses the tongue.
to be on the same boatto be in the same boatThe preposition is fixed.
cost a hand and a legcost an arm and a legUse the exact words.
bury the hatchbury the hatchetA hatchet is a small axe.
Key points to remember
  • An idiom is a fixed phrase whose meaning is figurative, not literal.
  • Idioms must be learned as whole units, with their exact words.
  • They differ from proverbs (full sentences of advice), phrasal verbs and similes.
  • Many difficult idioms come from Greek and Roman myths, fables and Shakespeare.
  • Use idioms to add colour in stories and speeches, but sparingly in formal writing.
  • Watch for common mistakes such as for all intents and purposes.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give each student three idioms and ask them to draw the literal meaning on one side of a card and write the real meaning on the other. Then ask them to write a short story that uses all three idioms. For older students, ask them to find the origin of one idiom in the origins table and present it to the class.

Please note: this guide uses British spelling. Some idioms differ between countries, and the origins of many everyday idioms are uncertain, so this guide gives only origins that are well documented.
