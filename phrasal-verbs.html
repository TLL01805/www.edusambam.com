Phrasal Verbs in English: A Complete Guide with Examples

Give up, look after, put off, come up with. Learn the verbs that make English sound natural, with clear meanings, examples and the one rule that decides where the object goes.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 18 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Vocabulary

Phrasal verbs are the small, everyday phrases that make English sound natural: give up, look after, put off, come up with. They are also one of the biggest challenges for learners, because the meaning is often different from the meaning of the words taken separately.

This guide explains what phrasal verbs are, the four types, and the one rule that decides where the object goes. It then gives 200 common phrasal verbs, grouped by particle with meanings and example sentences, followed by formal alternatives, common mistakes, key points, ten practice questions and a teacher activity.

It builds on Parts of Speech and Tenses & Verb Forms. Phrasal verbs change tense like any other verb: gives up, gave up, given up, giving up. Spelling in this guide follows British English.

1.What Is a Phrasal Verb?

A phrasal verb is a verb combined with a small word called a particle (an adverb or a preposition), such as up, out, off or in. Together they make a new meaning.

SentenceWhat it means
She looked up at the sky.She raised her eyes (literal meaning).
She looked up the word.She searched for the word in a dictionary (new meaning).
The plane took off.It left the ground.
He gave up smoking.He stopped smoking.

You cannot always guess the meaning from the parts, so phrasal verbs must be learned as whole units, in sentences.

2.The Four Types of Phrasal Verbs

TypeDoes it take an object?Example
IntransitiveNo objectThe plane took off. We set off early.
Transitive, separableObject can go in the middle or afterTurn off the light / Turn the light off / Turn it off.
Transitive, inseparableObject must come after the particleLook after the baby / Look after her.
Three-word (phrasal-prepositional)Verb + two particles + objectI look forward to the holidays. She came up with a plan.

3.The Separable Rule: Where Does the Object Go?

  • With a noun, separable verbs allow two positions: Pick up the book or Pick the book up.
  • With a pronoun (it, him, her, them, us), a separable verb must be split: Pick it up, never Pick up it.
  • Inseparable verbs are never split: Look after her, never Look her after.
  • Long objects usually go after the particle: Turn off all the lights in the building.

A good dictionary marks the type for you, for example with a sign such as [separable]. When unsure, check the entry.

17.Phrasal Verbs in Formal and Informal Writing

Phrasal verbs are very common in speech, emails and stories. In formal essays, reports and exams, a single-word verb often sounds more serious. Use the form that suits your reader.

put offpostpone

Neither style is wrong. See Formal vs. Informal English for more.

18.Common Mistakes with Phrasal Verbs

MistakeCorrectWhy
Turn off it.Turn it off.A pronoun goes between a separable verb and its particle.
Look it after.Look after it.Inseparable verbs are never split.
I look forward to meet you.I look forward to meeting you.To is a preposition, so use the -ing form.
He ran out the milk.He ran out of the milk.The three-word verb needs of.
I cannot put up the noise.I cannot put up with the noise.Do not drop the last particle.
She picked up it.She picked it up.Same rule as turn it off.
We came across to an old book.We came across an old book.Come across takes no extra to.

19.How to Learn Phrasal Verbs

  • Learn in sentences, not in lists. A sentence shows the meaning and the word order.
  • Group by particle or topic. Up often means increase or completion; out often means leaving or finishing; off often means starting a journey or stopping.
  • Learn five a week and use each in a sentence of your own.
  • Notice them in reading, news and films, and write down the whole phrase.
  • Check the type (separable or not) in a dictionary the first time you meet a new one.
Key points to remember
  • A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle, and the meaning is often new.
  • There are four types: intransitive, separable, inseparable and three-word.
  • With a separable verb, a pronoun object goes in the middle: turn it off.
  • Three-word phrasal verbs are inseparable: put up with it.
  • Phrasal verbs are natural in everyday English; use one-word verbs in very formal writing.
  • Learn them in sentences, in small groups, and check the dictionary for the type.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give each pair of students six cards with a verb on one set and a particle on another. They match them to make real phrasal verbs, then write one sentence for each. Finish by asking them to replace three phrasal verbs with a formal one-word verb.

Please note: this guide uses British spelling. Some phrasal verbs have more than one meaning, and only the most common meanings are shown here.
