What is Biology?

From the tiniest bacteria to the largest whales, biology is the science that tries to answer one huge question: what does it mean to be alive?

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 11 min read | Science

Look closely enough at anything alive — a blade of grass, a beetle, your own hand — and you find the same astonishing machinery at work: cells dividing, energy being captured and used, information being copied and passed on. Biology is the science devoted to understanding that machinery, in every living thing on Earth, and it is one of the broadest, most far-reaching subjects a person can study.

1.Defining Biology

Biology comes from the Greek words bios (life) and logos (study). It is the science of living organisms — how they are built, how they function, how they grow and reproduce, how they interact with their environment, and how they change over time. Biology asks questions at every possible scale, from a single molecule inside a cell to an entire rainforest ecosystem.

What makes biology unique among the sciences is its subject matter: life itself is messy, varied, and constantly changing. Unlike physics, where a falling object obeys the same predictable equation every time, living things adapt, evolve, and sometimes surprise even the scientists studying them. This is part of why biology is often described less as a fixed set of facts and more as an ongoing investigation — new species are still being discovered, new medicines still being developed, and new questions about the brain, genes, and ecosystems still being asked every single day.

Biologists don't just memorise facts about animals and plants. They ask why and how — why does a chameleon change colour, how does a seed know when to sprout, why do some diseases spread faster than others? Answering these questions requires observation, testing, and careful reasoning, which is why biology is built on the scientific method (more on that below).

2.The Characteristics of Living Things

Not everything that moves or changes is alive, and not everything alive moves or changes quickly. A rock can be worn smooth by a river, and a candle flame can flicker and "grow," yet neither is alive. Biologists generally agree that living things share a set of common characteristics, and something is only considered truly "alive" if it displays most or all of them together.

CharacteristicWhat It Means
OrganisationMade of one or more cells, organised into a structured body — even the simplest bacterium has internal order
MetabolismTakes in and uses energy to carry out life processes, such as breaking down food into usable fuel
GrowthIncreases in size or complexity over its lifetime, whether that's a seedling becoming a tree or a baby becoming an adult
ReproductionCan produce offspring, passing on genetic information so the species continues
ResponseReacts to changes in its environment (stimuli), like a plant bending toward light or an animal fleeing danger
HomeostasisMaintains stable internal conditions despite a changing outside world, such as keeping body temperature steady
AdaptationChanges across generations to suit its environment better, through the process of evolution

It's worth noting that viruses sit in a strange grey area here — they can reproduce and evolve, but only inside a host cell, and they show no metabolism of their own outside that host. This is exactly the kind of tricky, open question that keeps biology interesting: even something as fundamental as "what counts as alive" doesn't always have a clean, simple answer.

3.The Main Branches of Biology

Because "living things" covers such an enormous range — from bacteria too small to see, to blue whales longer than a bus — biology has split into many specialised branches, each focused on a different piece of the puzzle. A single biologist rarely studies everything at once; instead, they usually specialise, much like a doctor might specialise in cardiology or a teacher might specialise in mathematics.

Beyond these five, there are dozens more specialisms: anatomy studies body structure, physiology studies how those structures function, evolutionary biology studies how species change over time, marine biology focuses on ocean life, and biotechnology applies biological knowledge to create new medicines, crops, and materials. Many modern breakthroughs actually happen where two branches overlap — for example, "genomics" combines genetics with computer science to analyse enormous amounts of DNA data.

4.Levels of Biological Organisation

Biologists often organise living systems by scale, moving from the smallest building blocks up to entire ecosystems. This "zoom level" approach helps make sense of an overwhelming subject — a question about a single gene sits at the bottom of the scale, while a question about a whole rainforest sits near the top, and both are still biology.

Levels of biological organisation, from a single cell up to an entire ecosystem. Each level is built from the ones below it.

A single skin cell (Cell) combines with others to form skin tissue (Tissue). That tissue works with other tissue types to form an organ, like the heart (Organ). Organs work together as organ systems inside a whole living creature (Organism). Many organisms of the same species living in one place make up a Population, and all the populations interacting with their physical environment form an Ecosystem.

5.The Scientific Method in Biology

Biology isn't just a collection of facts to memorise — it's a way of asking and answering questions using evidence. Almost all biological knowledge, from the discovery of DNA's structure to the invention of new vaccines, has come from following a repeatable process called the scientific method.

The scientific method: a repeating cycle biologists use to build reliable knowledge about living things.

A biologist might observe that plants near a window grow faster than plants in a dark cupboard, question why that happens, form a testable hypothesis ("plants need light to grow well"), test it with a controlled experiment, and then conclude whether the evidence supports the hypothesis. Crucially, this isn't a one-way street — the results of one experiment almost always raise new questions, which is why the cycle loops back around rather than stopping.

6.Why Biology Matters

Example

Understanding how the influenza virus mutates each year — a question of microbiology and genetics — is exactly why scientists redesign the flu vaccine annually. Without that biological knowledge, the vaccine would quickly stop working.

Biology underpins medicine, agriculture, conservation, forensics, and technology. Doctors rely on it to treat disease, farmers rely on it to grow crops and raise healthy livestock, conservationists rely on it to protect endangered species, and engineers increasingly borrow ideas directly from nature — a field called biomimicry — to design new materials and machines, such as aeroplane wings inspired by bird flight or adhesives inspired by gecko feet.

Even careers that don't sound obviously "biological" often depend on it. Forensic scientists use DNA analysis to solve crimes. Food scientists use microbiology to keep food safe. Sports scientists use physiology to help athletes train more effectively. Wherever there is a living system involved, there is biology at work behind the scenes.

Real-World Example

A single question — "why are coral reefs dying?" — actually needs biology at every level: molecular biologists study heat-stress proteins in coral cells, physiologists study how coral polyps expel their algae, and ecologists study how reef collapse affects entire fish populations and coastal communities. No single branch of biology could answer the question alone.

A Closing Thought

Biology is less a single subject than a vast, connected web of questions — all circling back to the same idea: life, in all its forms, follows patterns we can observe, test, and understand. Every branch you'll explore in this section, from cells to ecosystems, is really just a different lens on that one big question — and as you move through the rest of this Biology section, you'll start to see how each new topic connects back to the ones before it.
