The Solar System: Planets, Moons & the Sun

Eight planets, well over 400 moons, and one star hold together a family of worlds bound by gravity. This article maps out the Sun, the planets, and the smaller bodies that share our cosmic neighbourhood.

Every planet you can name, every moon that circles one, and every comet that streaks across the night sky belongs to one family held together by gravity: the Solar System. At its centre is a single star whose gravity and light make everything else possible. This article explains how the Solar System formed, what the Sun is made of, how the eight planets compare, why Pluto is no longer a planet, how the Moon causes phases and eclipses, and how humans have explored it, with scale models, diagrams and worked examples.

In this article

What the Solar System is · how it formed · the Sun · the planets · a comparison table · the Moon, phases and eclipses · moons, dwarf planets and small bodies · exploration · four worked examples · key terms · common mistakes · practice questions and a quiz.

1.What Is the Solar System?

The Solar System is the Sun together with everything that orbits it because of its gravity: eight planets, their moons, five recognised dwarf planets, and countless asteroids, comets and smaller fragments of rock and ice. It formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a huge, slowly spinning cloud of gas and dust.

The leading explanation is the nebular hypothesis. The cloud collapsed under its own gravity and flattened into a spinning disc, with most of the material falling into the centre to form the Sun. Within the disc, dust grains stuck together, grew into larger bodies, and eventually became planets. This explains why almost everything orbits the Sun in nearly the same flat plane and in the same direction.

It also helps explain two families of planets. Close to the Sun it was too hot for ices to form, so only rock and metal could clump together: the small, dense rocky planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars). Farther out, beyond a distance called the frost line, ices could form too, and the growing planets became massive enough to hold huge amounts of gas: the giant planets (Jupiter and Saturn, the gas giants, and Uranus and Neptune, the ice giants).

2.The Sun: Our Local Star

The Sun is an ordinary, medium-sized star made mostly of hydrogen and helium. It holds about 99.8% of all the mass in the Solar System, which is why its gravity controls the orbits of everything else. It is not "burning" like a fire. Deep in its core, where the temperature is about 15 million °C, hydrogen nuclei are squeezed together in nuclear fusion to make helium, releasing enormous energy as light and heat.

Figure 1. Layers of the Sun (not to scale for the outer corona). Energy made in the core slowly works its way outwards.
  • Core: where fusion happens.
  • Radiative zone: energy passes outward as light, taking a very long time because the material is so dense.
  • Convective zone: hot gas rises, cools, and sinks again, like boiling water.
  • Photosphere: the visible surface, about 5,500 °C. Cooler dark patches on it are sunspots.
  • Corona: the Sun's thin outer atmosphere, visible during a total solar eclipse. It is surprisingly hotter than the surface, at over a million degrees, and scientists are still working out exactly why.

Sunspot numbers rise and fall in a cycle of about 11 years. At busy times, the Sun produces solar flares and coronal mass ejections, which send charged particles toward Earth. When these meet our magnetic field and atmosphere near the poles, they produce auroras, and strong events can disturb satellites and power grids, which is why space agencies watch the Sun every day. The Sun is about halfway through a life of roughly 10 billion years. In several billion years it will swell into a red giant.

Never look directly at the Sun, not even through a telescope, binoculars or ordinary sunglasses. It can permanently damage your eyes. Use certified eclipse glasses or project the image onto a screen.

3.The Planets: A Quick Tour

Figure 2. The four inner planets orbiting the Sun, with distances drawn to scale (1 AU = the Earth–Sun distance). The speeds follow the real ratios of their years: Mercury goes round about four times for every Earth year.

Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. With almost no atmosphere to hold heat, its surface swings from about 430 °C by day to about −180 °C at night. Venus is the hottest planet, about 465 °C, because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect, even though Mercury is closer to the Sun. Venus also spins backwards compared with most planets. Earth is the only known planet with liquid water on its surface and with life. Mars is the "Red Planet", coloured by iron oxide (rust) in its soil. It has the tallest known volcano in the Solar System, Olympus Mons, and evidence that water once flowed on its surface.

Jupiter is the largest planet; its Great Red Spot is a giant storm that has lasted for centuries and is about as wide as Earth or wider. Saturn is famous for its bright rings of ice and rock, thousands of kilometres wide yet very thin. Uranus rotates almost on its side, probably because of an ancient collision. Neptune is the farthest planet and has the fastest winds, over 2,000 km/h.

An astronomical unit (AU) is the average distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 150 million km. The figures are rounded, and a "day" here means one rotation of the planet.

Worked example 1: Build a scale model

Question: If the Sun is shrunk to a basketball 24 cm across, how far away is Earth, and how big is it?

Step 1. The real Sun is about 1,390,000 km wide, so the scale is 24 cm ÷ 1,390,000 km ≈ 0.0000173 cm per km, which is about 1 : 5.8 billion.

Step 2. Earth's distance is 150,000,000 km × 0.0000173 cm/km ≈ 2,600 cm.

Step 3. Earth's width is 12,742 km × 0.0000173 ≈ 0.22 cm.

Answer: About 26 m away and about 2 mm across, a tiny speck. Neptune (30 AU) would be about 780 m away. The Solar System is mostly empty space.

Worked example 2: Light travel time

Question: Light travels at about 300,000 km/s. How long does sunlight take to reach Earth, and how long to reach Neptune?

Step 1. Time = distance ÷ speed. Earth: 150,000,000 km ÷ 300,000 km/s = 500 s.

Step 2. 500 s ÷ 60 ≈ 8.3 minutes.

Step 3. Neptune: 4,500,000,000 km ÷ 300,000 km/s = 15,000 s, which is about 4.2 hours.

Answer: Sunlight reaches Earth in about 8 minutes and Neptune in about 4 hours.

Worked example 3: How long is Jupiter's year?

Question: Kepler's third law says T² = a³ (T in Earth years, a in AU). Jupiter is 5.2 AU from the Sun. Find its orbital period.

Step 1. a³ = 5.2 × 5.2 × 5.2 ≈ 140.6.

Step 2. T = √140.6 ≈ 11.9.

Answer: About 11.9 Earth years, matching the table. (The law is explained in our article on the Scientific Revolution.)

4.The Moon: Phases, Eclipses and Tides

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite, about 384,000 km away. The leading explanation of its origin is the giant-impact hypothesis: a Mars-sized body struck the young Earth, and the debris joined together to form the Moon. The Moon does not make light. It shines by reflecting sunlight, and always half of it is lit. We see different amounts of the lit half as the Moon orbits Earth about once every 27 days, so the cycle of phases takes about 29.5 days.

Eclipses. A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and its shadow falls on part of Earth; this can only happen at New Moon. A lunar eclipse happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and the Moon enters Earth's shadow; this happens only at Full Moon. We do not get an eclipse every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5 degrees, so the three bodies usually do not line up exactly. Tides are caused mainly by the Moon's gravity pulling on the oceans, with the Sun adding a smaller effect.

The Moon rotates once for each orbit, so the same side always faces Earth. Its far side is sometimes wrongly called the "dark side", but it receives just as much sunlight as the near side.

Worked example 4: Mass and weight

Question: A student has a mass of 50 kg. What is her weight on Earth and on Mars? (Surface gravity: Earth 9.8 m/s², Mars 3.7 m/s².)

Step 1. Weight = mass × gravity. On Earth: 50 × 9.8 = 490 N.

Step 2. On Mars: 50 × 3.7 = 185 N.

Answer: About 490 N on Earth and 185 N on Mars. Her mass is still 50 kg everywhere; only her weight changes.

5.Moons, Dwarf Planets and Smaller Bodies

A moon is any natural object that orbits a planet. Mercury and Venus have none, Earth has one, Mars has two small ones (Phobos and Deimos), and the giant planets have very many. The exact number of known moons keeps rising as new ones are discovered, and for Saturn and Jupiter it is already in the hundreds combined. Some are especially interesting:

  • Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto are Jupiter's four largest moons, discovered by Galileo in 1610. Io is the most volcanic body known, and Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System.
  • Titan, Saturn's largest moon, is the only moon with a thick atmosphere.
  • Europa is believed to hide a liquid water ocean under its icy crust, making it a prime target in the search for life beyond Earth.

Since 2006, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) has defined a planet as a body that (1) orbits the Sun, (2) has enough gravity to pull itself into a round shape, and (3) has cleared other debris from the neighbourhood of its orbit. A dwarf planet meets the first two but not the third. Five dwarf planets are recognised: Pluto, Eris, Haumea, Makemake and Ceres. Pluto was called the ninth planet from its discovery in 1930 until 2006. The reason for the change was not its small size alone, but the discovery of many similar objects in its region, which showed that it had not cleared its orbit.

  • Asteroids are rocky or metallic bodies, most of them in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. Ceres, a dwarf planet, lies there. Despite the name, the belt is mostly empty space, and spacecraft pass through safely.
  • Comets are icy bodies that grow a glowing head and a tail when they approach the Sun. The tail points away from the Sun because of sunlight and the solar wind.
  • The Kuiper Belt is a region of icy bodies beyond Neptune, where Pluto lives. The Oort Cloud is a theorised distant shell of icy bodies that has not been directly observed.
  • A meteoroid is a small piece of rock or metal in space. If it burns up in our atmosphere, the streak of light is a meteor (a "shooting star", which is not a star). If a piece reaches the ground, it is a meteorite.

6.Exploring the Solar System

  • 1957: The Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite.
  • 1961: Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space.
  • 1969: Apollo 11 landed Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin on the Moon.
  • 1977: Voyager 1 and 2 were launched; Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in 2012.
  • 2015: The New Horizons spacecraft flew past Pluto.
  • Today: Rovers explore Mars, and orbiters study Jupiter and other worlds.

Closer to home. Pakistan's space agency SUPARCO was founded in 1961, and Pakistan's first satellite, Badr-1, was launched in 1990. India landed the Chandrayaan-3 spacecraft near the Moon's south pole in August 2023.

8.Common Mistakes and Myths

  • "Mercury is the hottest planet because it is closest." Venus is hotter, because of its thick atmosphere.
  • "Pluto lost planet status because it is small." The key reason was that it has not cleared its orbit.
  • "Seasons come from distance from the Sun." They come from the tilt of Earth's axis (see our article on weather and climate).
  • "The Moon has a permanent dark side." Both sides get sunlight; the far side is just never visible from Earth.
  • "The Sun is on fire." It shines by nuclear fusion, not by burning.
  • "The asteroid belt is crowded with rocks." The rocks are very far apart.
  • "Shooting stars are stars." They are meteors, tiny bits of rock burning up in our atmosphere.

9.Summary

  • The Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a spinning cloud of gas and dust.
  • The Sun holds about 99.8% of its mass and shines by nuclear fusion.
  • Four small rocky planets lie near the Sun; four giant planets lie farther out.
  • Dwarf planets, asteroids, comets and the Kuiper Belt fill the rest of the system.
  • The Moon's phases and eclipses come from its orbit around Earth.
  • Distances in space are huge; light takes about 8 minutes to reach us from the Sun.

10.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. Which planet is the hottest, and why?

Venus, because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat.

2. What are the three conditions for a body to be a planet?

It orbits the Sun, it is round because of its own gravity, and it has cleared the neighbourhood of its orbit.

3. A planet is 4 AU from the Sun. Using T² = a³, find its year.

a³ = 64, so T = √64 = 8 Earth years.

4. Light takes 500 seconds to reach Earth from the Sun. About how many minutes is that?

500 ÷ 60 is about 8.3 minutes.

5. Why do we not see an eclipse every month?

The Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5 degrees, so the Sun, Earth and Moon usually do not line up exactly.

6. What is the difference between a meteor and a meteorite?

A meteor is the streak of light made by a space rock burning up in the atmosphere; a meteorite is a piece that reaches the ground.

For Teachers and Parents

Suggested activities: build the scale model from the worked example outside (the Sun as a basketball, Earth a pinhead about 26 metres away); keep a Moon diary for a month, drawing the shape each evening; use a torch, a ball and a smaller ball to model eclipses. Always supervise any Sun-watching and use only certified eclipse glasses. Planet data are rounded, and the numbers of moons change as new discoveries are made, so we do not give exact moon counts.
