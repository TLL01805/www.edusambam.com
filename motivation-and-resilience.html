Motivation and Resilience: How to Keep Going and Bounce Back

Why do we start and stop? How do people bounce back? See what research says about motivation, habits, procrastination, grit and resilience, with practical tools you can use.

Last updated: 12 October 2026. Written by the EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team. This article is for general education and does not replace advice from a qualified professional.

Every student knows the feeling: you decide to study, exercise or practise, and an hour later you are still scrolling. Every adult knows the other feeling too: a setback, a failure or a loss, and the question of how to get up again. Motivation (the energy to start and continue) and resilience (the ability to adapt and recover when things go wrong) are two of the most useful life skills, and they have been studied carefully. In this guide, we look at what scientists have actually found, including some findings that go against popular advice, and we show you tools that are backed by evidence. It builds on the motivation section of our article on Goleman's five components of EQ.

Key Takeaways

Wanting to change is not the same as changing: strong intentions turn into action much less reliably than people expect. If-then plans, a doable first step and a regular cue help. Rewards can undermine interest in activities you enjoy. Habits usually take weeks to months, not 21 days, and one missed day does not ruin them. Procrastination is more about managing unpleasant feelings than about laziness. "Grit" overlaps strongly with ordinary conscientiousness. Most people are more resilient than they think, resilience comes largely from relationships and ordinary skills, and treating yourself kindly after failure helps you improve. If low motivation or low mood lasts for weeks, ask for help.

1.What Is Motivation?

Motivation is the set of processes that start, direct and sustain behaviour towards a goal. Psychologists usually look at three parts: direction (what you aim at), intensity (how hard you try) and persistence (how long you keep going).

The intention-action gap

People often assume that if they really want to do something, they will do it. The evidence shows otherwise. A meta-analysis by Webb and Sheeran (2006) found that even a medium-to-large change in people's intentions produced only a small-to-medium change in their actual behaviour. Earlier work by Sheeran (2002) found that intentions explained, on average, only about 28% of the variation in whether people acted. In other words, there is a large gap between "I will" and "I did". Much of the science of motivation is about closing that gap.

2.Types of Motivation: Why You Do What You Do

Self-determination theory (Ryan and Deci, 2000) says that what matters is not only how much motivation you have but also what kind. Motivation lies on a continuum from controlled to self-chosen.

TypeMeaningExample in studying
AmotivationNo intention to act; feeling it is pointless"There is no point in studying this."
ExternalActing to gain a reward or avoid punishment"I study so that I won't be punished."
IntrojectedActing to avoid guilt or protect pride"I must study or I'll feel like a failure."
IdentifiedActing because you value the goal"I study because I want to become a doctor."
IntegratedThe goal fits who you are"Learning is part of who I am."
IntrinsicActing because the activity itself is interesting or enjoyable"I find this topic fascinating."

The more self-chosen the motivation, the better, on average, the persistence, learning quality and well-being. The theory proposes that people's motivation grows when three needs are met: autonomy (a sense of choice), competence (a feeling of growing skill) and relatedness (connection with others). Even a task that you did not choose can become more self-endorsed if you understand why it matters, are given some choices in how to do it, and feel supported.

3.Do Rewards Help? The Surprising Evidence

It seems obvious that rewards increase effort, and for boring tasks they often do. But for activities that people already enjoy, rewards can have a hidden cost. In a classic 1973 experiment, preschool children who liked drawing were promised a certificate for drawing. Later, when the reward was no longer available, these children spent less free time drawing than children who were not rewarded or who received an unexpected reward. This is called the overjustification effect: the reward changes the reason in your mind from "I like it" to "I do it for the prize".

A major meta-analysis by Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999) combined 128 experiments. It found that, overall, tangible rewards that were expected, and rewards given for just taking part, for finishing a task or for performing well, significantly reduced later free-choice intrinsic motivation (effect sizes of about minus 0.40, minus 0.36 and minus 0.28 for engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent rewards). In contrast, positive verbal feedback increased intrinsic motivation (an effect of about +0.33 for free-choice behaviour). Tangible rewards were more harmful to children than to college students.

A fair note: other researchers have argued that the harmful effects are limited to some situations and have disputed how the data should be interpreted. The most reasonable summary is that the effect of rewards depends on the type of reward, how it is given, and whether the person already enjoys the task.

SituationBetter approach
A child already loves readingAvoid paying for each book; discuss the stories, visit a library, praise effort and growth
A task that is boring or necessaryRewards are fine; connect the task to a meaningful goal as well
Giving praiseMake it specific, honest and about effort, strategy or progress, not only about ability
Rewarding yourselfUse small rewards after finishing, such as a break or a walk, not as the reason you do the activity
Teachers and parentsOffer choices, explain why tasks matter, and give informative (not controlling) feedback

4."Can I Do It?" and "Do I Want It?": Expectancy, Value and Delay

Expectancy-value theories (for example, Eccles and Wigfield, 2002) propose that motivation depends on two beliefs: expectancy ("Can I succeed at this?") and value ("Is it worth it to me?", including interest, usefulness and importance). If either one is zero, motivation collapses. Piers Steel and Cornelius König added two more ingredients in temporal motivation theory: delay (how long until the payoff) and impulsiveness (how sensitive you are to delay and distraction). The theory combines expectancy theory with the idea that people discount rewards that are far in the future.

A simple model of why we put things off: when a task feels doable and valuable, and the reward is near, we start. When the payoff is far away or we are easily distracted, we delay.

This simple model gives practical levers for any task you keep putting off:

LeverQuestion to askWhat to do
Raise expectancy"Do I believe I can do this?"Make the task smaller; get a model answer; ask for help; recall past successes
Raise value"Why does this matter to me?"Link it to a personal goal; find an interesting angle; study with a friend
Reduce delay"When do I get a payoff?"Set mini-deadlines and quick wins; track progress; reward each stage
Reduce impulsiveness"What distracts me?"Put the phone in another room; use a blocker; work in a quiet place; start with a cue

5.Self-Efficacy: Believing You Can

The psychologist Albert Bandura (1977, 1997) called the belief in your ability to carry out the actions needed for a specific goal self-efficacy. It is specific, not general: you can be confident in mathematics and doubtful about public speaking. Bandura described four sources of self-efficacy:

SourceMeaningHow to use it
Mastery experiencesSucceeding at the task, especially after effortBreak goals into steps you can complete; keep a "done" list
Vicarious experiencesSeeing similar people succeedStudy with peers; watch a classmate solve a problem; find role models
Verbal persuasionEncouragement from people you trustSeek honest, specific encouragement from a teacher or friend
Physical and emotional statesHow you interpret nerves, tiredness and moodSleep, move, breathe; read nerves as readiness (see our article on anger and stress)

Mastery experiences are the strongest source. This is why small wins matter: they are not just a trick but a way to build real evidence that you can do it.

6.If-Then Planning: A Well-Tested Tool

If intentions do not reliably become actions, what helps? One of the best-supported answers is implementation intentions, a term from the psychologist Peter Gollwitzer. Instead of just setting a goal ("I will study more"), you decide in advance when, where and how in an if-then form: "If situation X happens, then I will do Y."

A meta-analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) combined 94 independent tests and found a medium-to-large effect of if-then planning on goal achievement (d = 0.65). The plans helped people to start goals, to protect them from distractions, to drop failing strategies and to conserve effort. Later reviews with many more studies have continued to find positive effects, though they vary by goal and by how well the plan is made. If-then plans work best when you truly want the goal.

GoalVague planIf-then plan
Study maths"I'll study more.""If it is 5 p.m. and I have had a snack, then I will open my maths book at my desk for 25 minutes."
Exercise"I'll get fit.""If I wake up on a weekday, then I will put on my trainers and walk for 20 minutes before breakfast."
Avoid distraction"I won't use my phone.""If I start to study, then I will put my phone in another room."
Stay calm"I'll stop shouting.""If I feel my jaw tighten, then I will take one slow breath and count to five."
Obstacle plan"I hope nothing gets in the way.""If I feel tired at 5 p.m., then I will do only 10 minutes, but I will still start."

7.Habits: How Long Do They Really Take?

A habit is a behaviour triggered automatically by a cue. You may have heard that it takes 21 days to form a habit. That figure appears to come from an observation by a plastic surgeon in the 1960s about how long patients took to get used to a new appearance, and not from habit research. The best-known real-world study (Lally and colleagues, 2010) asked 96 volunteers to choose an eating, drinking or exercise behaviour and do it daily in the same context (for example, "after breakfast") for 12 weeks, while rating how automatic it felt.

Habits grow quickly at first, then level off. In one study of people repeating a daily behaviour, the time to reach near-automaticity varied from 18 to an estimated 254 days, with a median of 66 days.
  • Of the 82 people who provided enough data, the model fitted 62, and well for 39 of them. For these, the estimated time to reach near-automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days, with a median of 66 days. (Since the study lasted 84 days, the longer estimates were extrapolated.)
  • Missing a single opportunity did not materially affect habit formation, although people who were very inconsistent did not succeed.
  • Simple behaviours became automatic faster than complex ones, and exercise took longer than simple eating and drinking habits.

It was a small study of adults, so the numbers should not be taken as exact rules. The practical lessons are clear: expect weeks to months, keep the same cue, start with a tiny version, and do not give up after a slip.

Habit-building principleExample
Anchor to a stable cue"After I brush my teeth at night, I will read two pages."
Start tiny"Do one maths problem" before aiming for an hour
Make it easy and visibleLeave your book and pen on your desk the night before

8.Procrastination: It Is Not Just Laziness

A major meta-analysis by Piers Steel (2007), based on 691 correlations, found that the strongest and most consistent predictors of procrastination were task aversiveness (how unpleasant the task feels), task delay (how far away the deadline or reward is), low self-efficacy and impulsiveness, together with traits related to conscientiousness such as self-control and organisation. Traits such as neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation seeking showed only weak links. Procrastination also tends to decrease with age.

Researchers Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl (2013) suggest another way to understand it: procrastination is often short-term mood repair. We avoid a task that makes us feel anxious, bored or inadequate, and the avoidance gives us immediate relief, which makes us do it again, even though we pay for it later.

CauseWhat it feels likeWhat helps
The task feels unpleasant or boring"I can't face it."Make it more pleasant: change the place, study with others, add music if it helps you, use short blocks with breaks
Fear of failing"If I try and fail, it proves I'm not good."Aim for a "rough draft", ask for help, treat the first attempt as practice, use self-compassion
The goal is too big"Where do I even start?"Write the very next physical action: "open the document and type the title"
Distractions are too easy"Just five minutes of scrolling..."Remove them: phone away, blocker on, if-then plan
The reward is far away"The exam is months away."Set mini-deadlines this week, and a small reward after each
Unclear instructions"I'm not sure what is wanted."Ask your teacher for clarification or an example
Worked Example: The Ten-Minute Start

Nida has avoided her science project for a week. She makes an if-then plan: "If it is 4 p.m. and I am at my desk, then I will work for ten minutes on the first paragraph, and I am allowed to stop after that." She often continues beyond ten minutes, because starting is the hardest part. If she stops, she has still made progress and keeps her promise to herself, which builds self-efficacy.

9.Grit, Perseverance and Knowing When to Stop

The psychologist Angela Duckworth and colleagues (2007) defined grit as perseverance and passion for long-term goals, with two parts: perseverance of effort and consistency of interests. The idea was hugely popular. However, a large meta-analysis by Credé, Tynan and Harms (2017), covering 584 effect sizes from 88 samples and 66,807 people, found three things:

  • Grit was only moderately related to performance and retention (a correlation of about 0.18 with performance).
  • Grit overlapped very strongly with conscientiousness, an established personality trait (estimated at about 0.84).
  • The perseverance of effort part had stronger links with performance than the consistency of interests part, and it still explained some academic performance after accounting for conscientiousness.

The authors concluded that programmes designed to increase grit may have only weak effects. A sensible message is that sustained effort matters, but it is not a mysterious new trait, and it is shaped by interests, teaching and circumstances.

Persistence is not always the answer

Research on goal disengagement (for example, Wrosch and colleagues, 2003) suggests that people who can let go of truly unattainable goals, and redirect their effort to new, realistic ones, tend to have better well-being than people who cling to them. The skill is to tell the difference between a goal that needs a new strategy and a goal that needs to be replaced.

Ask yourselfIf yes
Is the goal still meaningful to me?Keep going, but review the strategy
Have I tried a different method or asked for help?Adjust before quitting
Is progress possible, even slowly?Persist with smaller steps
Is the goal harming my health, or truly out of reach despite repeated good attempts?Consider changing or replacing the goal, ideally after talking to someone you trust

10.What Is Resilience?

Resilience is the capacity to adapt well and keep or regain healthy functioning in the face of stress, adversity or trauma. It is not a fixed personality trait and not the same as being "tough" or never feeling pain. A resilient person may feel sad, anxious or angry, and still keep functioning and recover.

The psychologist George Bonanno (2004) challenged an old assumption that resilience is rare or that people who seem fine after a loss are hiding something. After reviewing the evidence, he argued that resilience is more common than is often believed, that it is a pattern distinct from slow recovery, and that there are multiple and sometimes unexpected pathways to it. People who show only minor and temporary disruption to their ability to function often still experience positive emotions as well as grief.

Four common patterns after a stressful or painful event (a schematic based on the trajectories described by Bonanno, 2004). Many people follow the resilience pattern more often than expected.

Please remember that none of these patterns is "right" or "wrong". People differ, and the same person can respond differently at different times. If someone is struggling, it is a sign to offer support, not a sign of weakness.

11.Where Does Resilience Come From? The Kauai Study

One of the most important studies of resilience followed all 698 children born in 1955 on the Hawaiian island of Kauai, from before birth to adulthood (Emmy Werner and Ruth Smith). About one third (201 children) were classed as high-risk because they faced four or more risk factors by age two, such as poverty, family conflict, parental mental illness or problems around birth. Many of them did go on to have problems. But one in three of the high-risk children (72) grew into competent, confident and caring adults.

What did these resilient young people have? Werner described three clusters of protective factors:

ClusterExamples found in resilient children
In the childAn easygoing, sociable temperament; good problem-solving and communication skills; a sense that they could influence their lives
In the familyA close bond with at least one competent, caring adult (often not a parent, such as a grandparent, aunt, older sibling or neighbour)
In the communitySupport and belonging through school, teachers, youth groups, faith communities and mentors

The developmental psychologist Ann Masten (2001) summed up this body of research as "ordinary magic": resilience usually comes from ordinary protective systems, such as relationships, thinking skills, self-regulation and community, and not from extraordinary traits. The practical message is hopeful: resilience can be supported by what the people around a person do, and by building skills.

Real-World Example

A teacher notices that a student who is going through a difficult time at home has stopped handing in work. Instead of a punishment, she arranges a quiet weekly check-in, helps him break assignments into steps, and links him with the school football club. None of these steps removes the hardship, but each one adds a protective factor: a caring adult, a feeling of competence and a place to belong.

Explanatory style: how you explain bad events

The psychologist Martin Seligman described a habit of thought called explanatory style: how you explain why bad things happen. A pessimistic style sees setbacks as permanent ("It will always be like this"), pervasive ("Everything is ruined") and personal ("It is all my fault"). An optimistic style sees them as more temporary, specific and balanced.

DimensionUnhelpful explanationMore balanced explanation
Permanence"I always fail exams.""I did not prepare well for this exam. I can prepare differently."
Pervasiveness"I'm bad at everything.""I struggled with this topic. I am doing well in others."
Personalisation"It's all my fault.""My choices played a part, and so did the difficulty of the paper and the time I had."

A note of balance: optimism is not always best. The psychologists Norem and Cantor (1986) described defensive pessimism, a strategy in which anxious people set low expectations and then carefully imagine what could go wrong in order to prepare for it. For some people this works well, so you do not need to force cheerfulness if careful planning suits you.

Do resilience programmes work?

The best-studied school programme is the Penn Resiliency Program, a group cognitive-behavioural programme. A meta-analysis of 17 studies with about 2,500 young people (Brunwasser, Gillham and Kim, 2009) found that participants had fewer depressive symptoms than comparison groups, for at least a year afterwards, but the effects were small (about 0.11 to 0.21). This is a realistic picture: resilience training can help, though it is not a cure-all, and the quality of relationships and environments matters as much as the lessons.

13.After Failure: The Case for Self-Compassion

Many people believe that being hard on themselves keeps them sharp. Research suggests the opposite. In four experiments (Breines and Chen, 2012), people who were guided to treat a failure or weakness with self-compassion (for example, remembering that setbacks are common and being kind to themselves) were more motivated to improve than people who received a self-esteem boost or no intervention. They were more willing to see a weakness as changeable, more motivated to make amends for a moral mistake and to avoid repeating it, and, after failing an initial difficult test, they spent more time studying for the next one.

Self-compassion is not lowering your standards or making excuses. It means acknowledging the mistake, accepting that imperfection is part of being human, and speaking to yourself as you would to a friend.

After a failure...Harsh self-criticismSelf-compassionate and responsible
Failed a test"I'm stupid. Why bother?""This went badly, and that hurts. Many people struggle with this topic. What can I change before the next test?"
Hurt a friend"I'm a terrible person.""I made a mistake and I care about the damage. I will apologise and do better."
Missed a goal"I always give up.""I slipped. What got in the way, and how do I restart with a smaller step?"

A simple review after any setback

  1. Facts: what exactly happened? (No labels.)
  2. Feelings: what am I feeling, and can I allow it without acting on it?
  3. Causes: which parts were in my control, and which were not?
  4. Lessons: what is one thing I would do differently?
  5. Next step: what is the smallest action I can take today?
  6. Support: who could help me?

14.When Motivation Drops: Burnout, Low Mood and Other Causes

Sometimes a lack of motivation is not a matter of technique. It may have a cause that needs attention.

Possible causeCluesWhat helps
Poor sleep, hunger or illnessTired, irritable, foggyRegular sleep, meals and a check-up with a doctor
Unclear or unrealistic goalsVague plans; feeling lostClarify the goal; break it into steps; ask for guidance
Too much pressure or too little controlFeeling trapped or resentfulFind choices, negotiate workloads, rest
BurnoutLong-term exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about the work, and reduced effectivenessRest, reduce demands, seek support; consider changes in the situation
Low mood or depressionLoss of interest or pleasure in most things, hopelessness, changes in sleep or appetite for weeksTalk to a doctor, counsellor or trusted adult; effective treatments exist

The World Health Organization's classification (ICD-11) describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with three dimensions: feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion, increased mental distance from one's job or feelings of negativism or cynicism about it, and reduced professional efficacy. It is not classed as a medical condition, but it is a real and serious state that needs a change in workload and support.

Important

If you have lost interest in almost everything for weeks, feel hopeless, or have thoughts of harming yourself, please speak to a doctor, counsellor or trusted adult soon. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, contact your local emergency number or a crisis helpline in your country. Asking for help is a strong and sensible step.

15.Growth From Hard Times: A Careful Word

Some people report positive changes after adversity, such as closer relationships, new priorities or a stronger sense of purpose. Researchers call this post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi and Calhoun, 1996). However, some scientists caution that self-reported growth does not always match measured change, and that nobody should feel pressured to find a "silver lining". It is perfectly healthy to simply say, "This was painful and I did not want it."

HabitA behaviour triggered automatically by a cue
Implementation intentionAn if-then plan that links a situation to an action
Intrinsic motivationDoing something for its own interest or enjoyment
Overjustification effectA decline in interest when an external reward replaces your own reason for doing something
ProcrastinationVoluntarily delaying something despite expecting to be worse off
ResilienceAdapting well and keeping or regaining healthy functioning under stress or adversity
Self-compassionTreating yourself with kindness and understanding after mistakes or hardship
Self-efficacyBelief in your ability to carry out the actions needed for a specific goal

A Closing Thought

Motivation is not a feeling you wait for. It is something you build, by making goals meaningful and doable, by planning when and where you will act, by removing small obstacles, and by treating yourself kindly when you slip. Resilience is not a superpower either. It grows from skills, from people who stand by us, and from the willingness to ask for help. You do not need to be perfect. You only need a small next step, and a person or two in your corner.
