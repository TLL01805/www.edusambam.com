The Grammar Trap: 10 Mistakes That Make Smart People Look Careless

Ten small slips that readers notice at once, each with a quick test you can use in five seconds, plus a few "rules" that are really myths.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 17 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Grammar
🔊 LISTEN TO THIS ARTICLE
SAVE YOUR EYES • IMPROVE YOUR LISTENING

Ready to read the article.

Grammar is not about being perfect, and it is not about obeying a rulebook written centuries ago. It is about clarity: making sure the thought in your head lands precisely in someone else's mind.

Some mistakes are so common, and so deeply rooted in everyday speech and typing, that millions of educated people make them without noticing. These are not the mistakes of careless people. They are the mistakes of people who were never shown a quick test that works. This guide explains ten of the most common, with a test for each, an honest note on which are true errors and which are matters of style, and the exceptions that most "top ten" lists leave out. It includes two worked examples, a checklist, common myths and ten practice questions.

For a wider reference, see Common Errors in English. This article also connects with Punctuation Rules, Sentence Structure and Editing Your Own Work. Spelling and style follow British English.

1.Error, Convention or Style? Sort Before You Fix

Not every item on a list of "grammar mistakes" is the same kind of problem. Sorting them saves you from "correcting" things that are not wrong and from ignoring things that are.

Three kinds of grammar problem
The ten mistakes in this guide, sorted.

Diagram 1. Mistake 3 (apostrophes) is a mixture: some uses are errors and some are style choices.

2.Mistake 1: "Your" and "You're"

The rule. Your shows possession (your book). You're is a contraction of you are.

Why it happens. The two words sound identical, and a spell-checker will not flag either one, because both are real words. A quick typist's fingers choose the sound, not the meaning.

The swap test

Replace the word with "you are". If the sentence still makes sense, write you're.

✅ You're going to love this. → You are going to love this. (makes sense)
❌ Your going to love this. → Your are going to love this. (nonsense)

3.Mistake 2: "Their", "There" and "They're"

Three words, one sound, three meanings.

WordMeaningMemory key
therea place, or "there is / there are"It contains here: both are about place.
theirbelonging to themIt contains heir: someone who inherits.
they'rethey areThe apostrophe replaces the missing a.
Examples

✅ The answer is over there.
✅ Their house is beautiful.
✅ They're coming tomorrow.

The swap tests in one place
Try the long form. If it fits, use the contraction.

Diagram 2. If the long form does not fit, use the other word (your, their or there, its, whose).

4.Mistake 3: The Apostrophe Catastrophe

Apostrophes cause more arguments than almost any other mark, mostly because they are put where they do not belong. Two uses are always standard.

  1. Missing letters (contractions): can't (cannot), it's (it is), I've (I have).
  2. Possession: the teacher's book (one teacher), the teachers' room (several), London's history, the children's toys.

The most common error is the greengrocer's apostrophe, used to make an ordinary plural: ❌ Apple's for sale, ✅ Apples for sale. An apostrophe never makes an ordinary word plural.

The third use: plurals of letters, numbers and abbreviations

This is where style guides differ, so it is a convention to check and not a simple rule.

  • Lowercase single letters: use an apostrophe: Mind your p's and q's.
  • Capital letters: style guides have moved. The AP Stylebook recommends the apostrophe (all A's). Older editions of the Chicago Manual of Style omitted it (all As), but its 18th edition (2024) brought the apostrophe back for capital letters. When the plural could be misread (As and as), keep the apostrophe or reword.
  • Decades and numbers: most major style manuals omit it: the 1990s.
  • Abbreviations: avoid it: two PhDs, three CDs. Using one (PhD's) is not wrong, but is considered inelegant and unnecessary in formal writing.
Its and it's

✅ It's a beautiful morning. (it is)
✅ The cat licked its paw. (belonging to it)

If in doubt, say "it is". If that fits, use the apostrophe.

5.Mistake 4: "Me and John" Instead of "John and I"

"Me and John went to the market." You have heard it, and you may have said it. It feels natural because in speech we often put ourselves first. The problem is case: I is the subject form, me the object form.

The remove-the-other-person test

Take out the other person and read it aloud. "Me went to the market" sounds wrong; "I went" sounds right. So: John and I went to the market.

  • As an object, use me: She gave the tickets to John and me. (She gave the tickets to me.)
  • After a preposition, use me: between you and me, not "between you and I".
  • Order is politeness, not grammar. Naming yourself last (John and I) is polite convention. "I and John" is grammatical but sounds self-centred.
  • Do not over-correct. Using I everywhere because it "sounds educated" (She invited John and I) is its own error, called hypercorrection. Also, myself is not a fancier me: write please contact Sara or me.

6.Mistake 5: "Less" Where You Mean "Fewer"

"10 items or less" appears on supermarket signs everywhere, and the traditional rule says it should be "10 items or fewer".

UseForExamples
fewerthings you can count one by onefewer students, fewer mistakes, fewer chairs
lessthings you cannot countless water, less time, less patience

The test: put a number in front. "Three students" works, so use fewer students. "Three patience" does not, so use less patience.

The exceptions that most lists forget. Time, money, distance and weight take less than because we treat the amount as one thing: It cost less than five pounds. He lives less than ten miles away. Dictionary writers also note that less is common in speech even with countable nouns, so it is more a convention of careful writing than an absolute rule. In exams and formal writing, follow the traditional rule.

7.Mistake 6: The Dangling Modifier

A dangling modifier is a describing phrase attached to the wrong subject. The result can be absurd.

Ask: who is doing the action?
The opening phrase must describe the subject that follows it.

Diagram 3. The modifier must sit next to the thing it describes.

Read your sentences aloud. If something sounds unintentionally funny, a dangling modifier is usually the cause. A related slip is the misplaced modifier: She almost drove the whole way (she did not quite drive) versus She drove almost the whole way.

8.Mistake 7: Overusing "Very" (Style, Not Error)

"Very" is not wrong, and it is useful. The weakness is that very + a plain adjective is often vaguer than one exact word. Compare:

PlainOften stronger
very tiredexhausted
very happydelighted
very coldfreezing
very angryfurious
very bigenormous

Use the stronger word only when it is more accurate. A person who is "very tired" is not always "exhausted". And in simple, clear writing, "very" is perfectly fine. The aim is precision, not a ban.

9.Mistake 8: "Affect" and "Effect"

Affect is usually a verb (to influence); effect is usually a noun (a result).

Examples

✅ The rain affected our plans. (verb)
✅ The rain had a strange effect on our mood. (noun)

Memory trick: RAVEN

Remember: Affect is a Verb, Effect is a Noun.

The exceptions. Effect can be a formal verb meaning "to bring about": to effect change. Affect is also used as a noun in psychology to mean an outward emotional expression. Both are rare, and your exam will almost certainly expect the usual pattern.

10.Mistake 9: Run-On Sentences and Comma Splices

A run-on sentence joins two complete sentences with no punctuation. A comma splice joins them with only a comma. Either way, the reader has to guess where one idea ends.

Four fixes

❌ I was tired, I went to bed early.
✅ A full stop: I was tired. I went to bed early.
✅ A semicolon: I was tired; I went to bed early.
✅ A joining word: I was tired, so I went to bed early.
✅ A dependent clause: Because I was tired, I went to bed early.

Short sentences are powerful, so do not be afraid of them. See Sentence Structure and Punctuation Rules for more.

11.Mistake 10: Passive Voice When Active Is Clearer (Style, Not Error)

Passive and active

Passive: "The letter was written by Sarah." (weaker, more indirect)
Active: "Sarah wrote the letter." (short, clear, direct)

The passive is not wrong. Scientists use it constantly because the process matters more than the person. It is also right when the doer is unknown (The window was broken) or unimportant. The risk is that it can hide who is responsible: Mistakes were made. Prefer the active voice when you want to be clear about who did what.

The zombie test

Add "by zombies" after the verb. If the sentence still makes sense, it is passive.
"The letter was written… by zombies" → passive.
"Sarah wrote the letter… by zombies" → makes no sense, so it is active.

The test works for most passives. Some passives, such as He was born in 1990, do not take a "by" phrase and need a different check: is the subject receiving the action?

12.What These Mistakes Have in Common

  • Speed. Most of the sound-alike slips happen when we type or write fast.
  • Sounds, not letters. Our ears cannot separate their from there, so we need a test, not a feeling.
  • Half-remembered rules. We remember "never say me first" and then use I everywhere.
  • Spell-check blindness. Every word in "Your going to love there house" is a real word, so spell-check misses all of the errors.

The cure is not memorising a rulebook. It is knowing your own three or four recurring slips and checking for them on purpose. See Editing Your Own Work.

13.How to Proofread for These Ten Mistakes

Most of these slips are made by people who know the rule. They happen when you write fast. A short proofreading routine catches them.

  • Read it aloud. Your ear catches run-ons and missing words that your eye skips.
  • Search for the trouble words. Use "find" for your, you're, their, there, they're, its, it's, affect, effect, less and check each one with its swap test.
  • Look at every sentence opener ending in -ing or -ed. Ask who is doing the action; that is how you spot a dangling modifier.
  • Check long sentences. If a sentence holds two complete ideas, it may need a full stop or a semicolon.
  • Do not trust the spellchecker alone. It accepts any real word, so "your going" and "their is" often pass without a warning.
  • Read it backwards, one sentence at a time. This breaks the flow of meaning so you see the surface mistakes.

Then leave the text for a while and read it again. For a longer routine, see Editing Your Own Work.

14.Worked Examples

Example A: Proofreading a paragraph with ten slips

Your going to be surprised by how their is fewer mistakes in this draft. Me and Sara checked it between you and I, and there were less errors then last time. Walking to the library, the exam stress felt very very big. The report was written quickly and it's effect was positive, we think.

SlipCorrection and test
Your goingYou're going (you are)
their isthere are (existence; plural "mistakes")
Me and Sara checkedSara and I checked (remove Sara: "I checked")
between you and Ibetween you and me (after a preposition)
less errorsfewer errors (countable)
then last timethan last time (comparison)
Walking to the library, the exam stress felt…As I walked to the library, the exam stress felt… (dangling modifier)
very very bigenormous (one precise word)
it's effectits effect (possession)

Corrected: You're going to be surprised by how there are fewer mistakes in this draft. Sara and I checked it between you and me, and there were fewer errors than last time. As I walked to the library, the exam stress felt enormous. The report was written quickly, and its effect was positive, we think.

Example B: Applying the tests

1"The weather ___ our trip." Is it a verb? Yes. affected.
2"There are ___ chairs than people." Can you count them? Yes. fewer.
3"___ the best player on the team?" Try "who is": Who's the best player.
4"The dog wagged ___ tail." Try "it is": "it is tail" fails, so its.
5"She sent Tom and ___ an invitation." Remove the other person: "She sent me an invitation." So "Tom and me".

Try it yourself. Correct: "Having finished the exam, the bell rang and there happy faces were everywhere."

Sample answer: "Having finished the exam, the students heard the bell ring, and happy faces were everywhere." The dangling modifier is fixed by naming who finished the exam. "There happy faces" (a slip for "their" or "there are") is removed.

16.Common Myths

MythThese mistakes show a person is not intelligent.
FactThey are common among educated people, mostly because of speed and sound-alike words.
MythThe passive voice is always wrong.
FactIt is useful when the doer is unknown or unimportant, and in much scientific writing.
MythYou must never use the word "very".
FactIt is not an error. A more exact word is often better, but not always.
MythSpell-check will catch these.
FactSpell-check finds non-words. It cannot see that a real word is in the wrong place.

17.Key Terms

Contraction: a shortened form with an apostrophe, such as you're.
Possessive: a form showing ownership, such as your or the dog's bone.
Subject / object pronoun: I and he / me and him.
Hypercorrection: using a "correct" form where it is not needed, such as between you and I.
Countable noun: a noun you can count one by one.
Dangling modifier: a phrase attached to the wrong subject.
Run-on / comma splice: two sentences joined without proper punctuation.
Active / passive voice: the subject does / receives the action.
Greengrocer's apostrophe: an apostrophe wrongly added to an ordinary plural.

18.A Final Word

Nobody becomes a good writer by memorising rules. Good writers read widely, write often and care about their words. These ten mistakes are not signs of poor intelligence. They are signs of a writer who has not yet been shown a quick test, and now you have ten. Choose the three that you make most, check for them every time, and the others will take care of themselves. Continue with Common Errors in English and Punctuation Rules.

Key points to remember
  • Sort problems into errors, conventions and style choices.
  • Use swap tests: "you are", "they are", "it is".
  • Apostrophes show missing letters or possession, never an ordinary plural.
  • Remove the other person to test I and me.
  • Fewer for counting, less for amounts, with exceptions for time and money.
  • Make sure every opening phrase describes the subject that follows.
  • Know your own recurring slips, and check for them deliberately.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students a paragraph with ten planted slips (one from each mistake) and ask them to find and fix each using the test, naming the test they used. Then ask each student to list their own three most frequent slips from their last essay.

Please note: guidance on apostrophes with plurals of letters and abbreviations differs between style guides, and usage of less and fewer varies in everyday speech. Students should follow the style required by their own syllabus and teacher. The examples are original.
