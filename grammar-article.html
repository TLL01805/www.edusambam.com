The Grammar Trap: 10 Mistakes That Make Smart People Look Careless

📋 EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team ⏱ 9 min read 📅 2026
🔊 LISTEN TO THIS ARTICLE
SAVE YOUR EYES • IMPROVE YOUR LISTENING
Listen to the article instead of relying only on continuous screen reading.
Ready to read the article.
Grammar is not about being perfect. It is not about following a rulebook written centuries ago. Grammar is about clarity — the art of making sure the thought in your head lands precisely in someone else's mind.

Some mistakes are so common, so deeply rooted in everyday speech and writing, that millions of educated people make them without even noticing. These are not the mistakes of careless people. These are the mistakes of people who were simply never shown a better way.

This article sets that right.
Mistake 1
Confusing "Your" and "You're"
What most websites say"Your" shows possession. "You're" means "you are."

The deeper explanation: The reason people confuse these is not ignorance — it is speed. When we type quickly, our fingers go on autopilot. The brain processes meaning, not spelling.

Every time you write you're, say the words "you are" in your head and replace it. If the sentence still makes sense, it is correct.

✅You're going to love this. → You are going to love this. Makes sense.
❌Your going to love this. → Your are going to love this. Sounds wrong immediately.

That tiny mental pause will save you from embarrassment every time.

Mistake 2
Writing "Their", "There" and "They're" Wrongly

Three words. Same sound. Three completely different meanings.

Memory Key

There contains the word here — both are about place.  ·  Their contains heir — both show belonging.  ·  They're has an apostrophe — always say "they are" and check.

✅"The answer is over there."
✅"Their house is beautiful."
✅"They're coming tomorrow."

Write these three keys on a sticky note. Within two weeks, you will never confuse them again.

Mistake 3
The Apostrophe Catastrophe

Apostrophes cause more arguments than almost any other punctuation mark. People put them everywhere — especially where they do not belong.

The Golden Rule — Apostrophes do three jobs 1. They show something is missing: can't (cannot), it's (it is), I've (I have)
2. They show possession: the teacher's book, London's history
3. They make special plurals of letters, numbers and abbreviations: A's, 9's, PhD's

This third use is one most grammar books forget to mention — but it matters greatly in academic and professional writing. When writing the plural of a single letter, number or abbreviation, the apostrophe prevents confusion:

✅"She got three A's in her exams." — Without the apostrophe, As looks like the word as
✅"He scored two 9's in the test." — 9s alone can look odd and unclear
✅"She holds two PhD's." — The apostrophe shows these are qualifications
✅"Mind your p's and q's." — A classic expression meaning behave properly

However, modern style guides are divided. Some now accept As, 9s, PhDs without apostrophes. The safest rule: when leaving out the apostrophe causes confusion, keep it in.

Apostrophes never — ever — make an ordinary word plural.

❌"Apple's for sale"
✅"Apples for sale"

The confusion with its and it's deserves special attention:

✅It's = it is → "It's a beautiful morning."
✅Its = belonging to it → "The cat licked its paw."

If in doubt, say "it is" out loud. If it sounds right, use the apostrophe. If it sounds wrong, leave it out.

Mistake 4
Starting a Sentence with "Me" Instead of "I"

"Me and John went to the market." — You have heard this. You may have said it. It feels natural because in conversation, we always mention ourselves first. But grammatically, it is incorrect.

The Simple Test

Remove the other person and read it aloud. "Me went to the market" sounds wrong. "I went to the market" sounds right. So it must be: "John and I went to the market."

In correct English, you mention yourself last — not first. It is considered both grammatically accurate and polite.

Mistake 5
Using "Less" When You Mean "Fewer"

"10 items or less" — this is on supermarket signs everywhere. And every time, it should say "10 items or fewer."

The Rule Fewer — for things you can count: fewer students, fewer mistakes, fewer chairs
Less — for things you cannot count: less water, less time, less patience

If you can put a number in front of it, use fewer. You can say "three students" — so it is "fewer students." You cannot say "three patience" — so it is "less patience."

Mistake 6
The Dangling Modifier — The Funniest Grammar Mistake

A dangling modifier is a describing phrase attached to the wrong subject. The result can be absurd.

❌"Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful." — Were the trees walking?
❌"Having eaten the whole cake, the plate was empty." — Did the plate eat the cake?
✅"Walking down the street, I noticed the trees were beautiful."
✅"Having eaten the whole cake, she left an empty plate."

Read your sentences aloud. If something sounds unintentionally funny, a dangling modifier is usually the culprit.

Mistake 7
Overusing the Word "Very"

"Very" is a lazy word. It tells the reader something is more than it seems — but does not show them. In good writing, showing always beats telling.

WeakStrong
Very tiredExhausted
Very happyDelighted
Very coldFreezing
Very angryFurious
Very bigEnormous

Find the stronger, more precise word. The writing will immediately feel more confident and more intelligent.

Mistake 8
Confusing "Affect" and "Effect"
The Difference Affect is almost always a verb (an action): "The rain affected our plans."
Effect is almost always a noun (a thing): "The rain had a strange effect on our mood."
Memory Trick — RAVEN

Remember — Affect is a Verb, Effect is a Noun. Say it three times. RAVEN. RAVEN. RAVEN.

Mistake 9
Writing Run-On Sentences

A run-on sentence happens when two complete sentences are joined with no proper punctuation — just a comma or nothing at all — the result is a breathless, confusing rush of words that exhausts the reader and loses the meaning somewhere along the way.

Did you notice what just happened? That paragraph above was a run-on sentence.

Three Fixes 1. Use a full stop: "I was tired. I went to bed early."
2. Use a semicolon: "I was tired; I went to bed early."
3. Use a joining word: "I was tired, so I went to bed early."

Short sentences are powerful. Do not be afraid of them.

Mistake 10
Passive Voice When Active Voice Is Better
❌"The letter was written by Sarah." — Passive voice. Weak and indirect.
✅"Sarah wrote the letter." — Active voice. Short, clear, direct.

Passive voice is not wrong — scientists use it constantly. But in everyday writing, active voice makes your words come alive.

The Zombie Test

If you can add "by zombies" to the end of your sentence and it makes sense, you have written a passive sentence. "The letter was written... by zombies" ✅ Passive. "Sarah wrote the letter... by zombies" ❌ Active.

A Final Word

Nobody becomes a good writer by memorising rules. Good writers read widely, write often, and care about their words.

These ten mistakes are not signs of poor intelligence. They are signs of a writer who has not yet been shown a better way. That changes today.

The next step is yours.
