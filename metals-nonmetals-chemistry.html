Chemistry of Metals and Non-Metals: A Tale of Two Families

Iron bridges, copper wires, oxygen in the air and sulfur in volcanoes belong to two families with very different behaviour. Learn how to tell them apart and predict their reactions.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 17 min read | Updated 21 October 2026

Of the roughly 118 known elements, most are metals. Metals build our bridges, wires and vehicles; non-metals make up most of the air we breathe, our water and our bodies. Understanding why these two families behave so differently, and how to compare the reactivity of different metals, is a core part of chemistry.

1.Where Metals and Non-Metals Are Found

On the periodic table, metals occupy the left and the centre, and non-metals are in the upper right (plus hydrogen, which sits at the top left but behaves as a non-metal). A staircase line separates them. Elements next to this line, such as silicon and germanium, have properties between the two and are called metalloids or semi-metals; they are important in electronics because they are semiconductors.

Most of the elements in the periodic table are metals. The most abundant metal in the Earth’s crust is aluminium, followed by iron. Iron is the most widely used metal in the world, mainly as steel.

2.Physical Properties Compared

PropertyMetals (typical)Non-metals (typical)
AppearanceShiny (lustrous) when cleanDull
State at room temperatureSolid (except mercury)Solids, liquids (bromine) or gases
Strength and shapeStrong; malleable (hammered) and ductile (drawn into wires)Brittle if solid; shatter when hit
Electrical conductivityGood conductorsPoor conductors (insulators), except graphite
Thermal conductivityGoodPoor
Melting and boiling pointsUsually highUsually low
DensityUsually highUsually low
Sound when struckSonorous (ring)Dull thud

These are general patterns, and there are exceptions. Mercury is a liquid metal. Sodium and potassium are soft enough to cut with a knife and have low melting points. Graphite, a form of carbon, conducts electricity, while diamond, also pure carbon, is extremely hard and does not conduct. Iodine is a shiny non-metal solid.

3.Metallic Bonding and Why It Matters

In a metal, the atoms lose their outer electrons and become positive ions arranged in a regular lattice. The outer electrons are delocalised: they are not attached to any one atom but move freely through the whole structure. The strong attraction between the positive ions and the sea of electrons is called metallic bonding.

Figure 1. Metallic bonding. Positive ions (red) sit in a lattice while delocalised electrons (gold) move freely between them.
  • Conduct electricity: the free electrons carry charge when a voltage is applied.
  • Conduct heat: moving electrons pass energy quickly through the metal.
  • Malleable and ductile: layers of ions can slide over each other while the electron sea keeps them bonded, so the metal bends instead of shattering.
  • High melting points: a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong metallic bonds (though this varies from metal to metal).

Alloys

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements. In an alloy, atoms of different sizes disturb the regular layers, so the layers slide less easily and the alloy is usually harder and stronger than the pure metal. Examples: steel (iron with a little carbon), brass (copper and zinc), bronze (copper and tin) and solder (tin and lead, or lead-free versions). Pure iron is too soft for most uses, so we nearly always use steel.

4.Chemical Properties

Metals tend to lose electrons to form positive ions (cations), such as Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Al³⁺. Non-metals tend to gain electrons to form negative ions (anions), such as Cl⁻ and O²⁻, or to share electrons in covalent bonds.

OxidesMetal oxidesNon-metal oxides
Usual natureBasicAcidic (or neutral)
With waterSoluble ones give alkalis: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOHSoluble ones give acids: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃

ExamplesMgO, CuO, CaOCO₂, SO₂, NO₂, P₄O₁₀; water and CO are neutral

Some oxides, such as aluminium oxide and zinc oxide, are amphoteric: they react with both acids and strong alkalis. Acidic oxides are the cause of acid rain, which is why sulfur dioxide from burning fuels is a pollution problem.

5.The Reactivity Series

The reactivity series arranges metals in order of how readily they react, that is, how easily they lose electrons. Carbon and hydrogen are added for comparison because they help predict extraction and reactions with acids.

Figure 2. The reactivity series, with the usual method of extraction for each group. The red arrow pulses to show that reactivity increases going up.
MetalWith cold waterWith dilute acidWith air / oxygen
Potassium, sodiumViolent reaction; hydrogen and an alkali formExplosive: not safe to tryTarnish quickly; burn readily
CalciumSteady fizzing; calcium hydroxide and hydrogenVigorous reactionBurns
MagnesiumVery slow with cold water; reacts with steam to give MgO + H₂Fast reactionBurns with a bright white flame
Zinc, ironNo reaction with cold water; react with steamModerate (zinc), slow (iron)Slowly form oxide; iron rusts
Copper, silver, goldNo reactionNo reactionCopper forms black CuO on heating; silver and gold are unreactive

Examples: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ and Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂. Aluminium is high in the series, but it appears unreactive because a thin, tough layer of aluminium oxide forms on its surface and protects the metal underneath. This is why aluminium is used for drinks cans, window frames and aircraft.

The reactivity series also predicts the thermal decomposition of metal carbonates: carbonates of very reactive metals such as sodium and potassium do not decompose in a Bunsen flame, but those of less reactive metals do. For example, green copper(II) carbonate decomposes when heated: CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂ (it turns black).

Safety note

Sodium, potassium and calcium react violently with water and must be handled only by teachers, with eye protection and safety screens. Alkali metals are stored under oil. Never touch them with your hands. Look away from the bright flame of burning magnesium.

6.Displacement Reactions

A more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt. The more reactive metal loses electrons (is oxidised) and the less reactive metal ions gain them (are reduced).

Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu

In the ionic form: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. When an iron nail is placed in blue copper(II) sulfate solution, a pink-brown coating of copper forms on the nail and the blue colour fades to pale green as iron(II) sulfate forms.

Worked example 1: will a reaction happen?

Question: Predict whether each reaction occurs, and write a word equation where it does: (a) magnesium + zinc sulfate; (b) copper + zinc sulfate.

  1. Compare positions in the reactivity series. Magnesium is above zinc, so (a) occurs: magnesium + zinc sulfate → magnesium sulfate + zinc.
  2. Copper is below zinc, so (b) does not occur: no reaction.
  3. Ionic equation for (a): Mg + Zn²⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Zn.

Rule: a metal displaces any metal lower than itself in the series.

7.Extracting Metals from Ores

An ore is a rock that contains enough of a metal compound to make it worth extracting. The method depends on the metal’s position in the reactivity series: the more reactive the metal, the more energy is needed to extract it.

  • Very reactive metals (potassium to aluminium) are extracted by electrolysis of their molten compounds, which needs a lot of electricity. See the Electrochemistry article for aluminium.
  • Moderately reactive metals (zinc, iron, and so on) are extracted by heating their oxides with carbon or carbon monoxide, which removes the oxygen (reduction).
  • Unreactive metals such as gold are found uncombined (native) in the ground and only need to be separated from rock.

Iron in the blast furnace

Iron is extracted from the ore haematite (mainly iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃) in a blast furnace. The raw materials are iron ore, coke (nearly pure carbon), limestone and hot air.

Figure 3. The blast furnace. Gold dots show hot gases rising through the furnace. The main reducing agent is carbon monoxide.
  1. Coke burns in the hot air blast: C + O₂ → CO₂, releasing heat.
  2. Carbon dioxide reacts with more coke to form carbon monoxide: CO₂ + C → 2CO.
  3. Carbon monoxide reduces the iron(III) oxide to molten iron: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.
  4. Limestone decomposes: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. The calcium oxide reacts with sand (silicon dioxide, an impurity) to form calcium silicate, called slag: CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃. The slag floats on the molten iron and is tapped off separately.
  5. Molten iron is run off at the bottom. It contains about 4% carbon, which makes it brittle, so it is further processed into steel.
Worked example 2: how much iron?

Question: What mass of iron can be made from 80 kg of iron(III) oxide, assuming complete reaction? (Ar: Fe = 56, O = 16)

  1. Equation: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.
  2. Mr of Fe₂O₃ = 2 × 56 + 3 × 16 = 160.
  3. Moles of Fe₂O₃ = 80 000 g ÷ 160 g/mol = 500 mol.
  4. Ratio 1 : 2, so moles of Fe = 1000 mol.
  5. Mass of Fe = 1000 × 56 = 56 000 g = 56 kg.

Answer: 56 kg of iron. (Real furnaces give slightly less because of impurities and heat losses.)

The halogens: a non-metal family

The halogens (Group 17) are fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine. At room temperature chlorine is a green-yellow gas, bromine a red-brown liquid and iodine a grey-black solid that gives a purple vapour. Unlike metals, reactivity decreases down the group. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution:

Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂

Chlorine displaces bromine (the solution turns orange-brown); bromine displaces iodine, but iodine cannot displace bromine or chlorine.

Safety note

Chlorine, bromine and iodine vapours are harmful to breathe and irritate the eyes and lungs. Halogen reactions are teacher demonstrations in a fume cupboard.

9.Using Metals Wisely

Metals are a finite resource, and mining and extracting them affect the environment through habitat loss, energy use and waste. Recycling metals such as aluminium, iron and copper saves ores and energy, and reduces waste. Preventing corrosion, for example by painting, galvanising and sacrificial protection, makes metal objects last longer. Choosing the right material for each job, based on properties such as strength, density, conductivity and cost, is a key part of engineering.

10.Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Saying all metals are hard and heavy. Sodium is soft and light, and mercury is a liquid.
  • Forgetting graphite. It is a non-metal that conducts electricity.
  • Mixing up the trends. Metal reactivity in Group 1 increases down the group; halogen reactivity decreases down the group.
  • Thinking aluminium is unreactive. It is high in the series but protected by its oxide layer.
  • Saying “the metal wants to lose electrons”. Explain with energy and structure instead: it loses electrons easily.
  • Saying carbon reduces all the ore directly. In the blast furnace most of the reduction is done by carbon monoxide.

11.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. Explain why metals conduct electricity.

Answer: Delocalised electrons are free to move through the metal and carry charge.

2. Why is steel used rather than pure iron?

Answer: Steel is harder and stronger because the added carbon atoms disrupt the layers of iron atoms.

3. Will zinc displace copper from copper(II) sulfate? Explain.

Answer: Yes, because zinc is more reactive than copper and so takes its place.

4. Write the word equation for sodium reacting with water.

Answer: Sodium + water → sodium hydroxide + hydrogen.

5. Why is limestone added to the blast furnace?

Answer: It decomposes to calcium oxide, which reacts with silica (sand) impurities to form slag.

6. Which gas does copper(II) carbonate give off when heated, and what colour change occurs?

Answer: Carbon dioxide; green to black.

7. Which is more reactive, chlorine or iodine?

Answer: Chlorine, because halogen reactivity decreases down Group 17.

12.Summary

  • Metals are on the left and centre of the periodic table; non-metals are on the upper right.
  • Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile and conduct heat and electricity because of delocalised electrons; most non-metals are dull, brittle and insulating.
  • Metals form positive ions and basic oxides; non-metals form negative ions or covalent bonds and acidic oxides.
  • The reactivity series predicts reactions with water, acid and salt solutions, and the method of extraction.
  • Iron is extracted in the blast furnace using carbon monoxide; reactive metals use electrolysis.
  • Halogens are reactive non-metals and the reactivity decreases down the group.
  • Recycling and corrosion prevention save resources.
For teachers and parents

Suggested use: have students test samples of several metals and non-metals for conductivity with a simple circuit and sort them, then compare magnesium, zinc and copper with dilute acid and record the speed of bubbling. Reactions of alkali metals and halogens are teacher demonstrations only. Parents can use household objects (foil, cables, pans, coins, pencil lead) to discuss properties and uses. Please follow your own syllabus for the required details of extraction and trends.
