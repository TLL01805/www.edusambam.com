Acids, Bases and Salts: The Chemistry of Sour, Bitter and Neutral

Lemon juice, soap, baking soda and stomach acid are all described by one idea: how many hydrogen ions are present. Learn the reactions, tests and calculations.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 17 min read | Updated 21 October 2026

Sour lemons, bitter soap, fizzing antacids and the colour change of litmus paper all trace back to acids and bases. These are among the most useful ideas in chemistry, because they explain digestion, cleaning, farming and the manufacture of countless materials. In this article you will learn how to recognise acids and bases, predict their reactions, make salts and calculate concentrations by titration.

1.What Are Acids and Bases?

An acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions, H⁺, when dissolved in water. Common examples are hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), nitric acid (HNO₃), ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH, in vinegar) and citric acid (in citrus fruits). Acids taste sour and turn blue litmus red, and they have a pH below 7.

A base is a substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water. Most bases are metal oxides or metal hydroxides, such as copper(II) oxide (CuO) and magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)₂). A base that dissolves in water is called an alkali, and it produces hydroxide ions, OH⁻. Common alkalis are sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), calcium hydroxide (limewater) and ammonia solution. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis, because many bases do not dissolve in water.

Alkalis feel slippery, turn red litmus blue and have a pH above 7. A solution that is neither acidic nor alkaline is neutral and has a pH of 7; pure water is neutral.

Safety note

Never taste or touch laboratory acids and alkalis. Concentrated acids and alkalis are corrosive and can burn the skin and eyes. Wear eye protection, and if a chemical gets on your skin or in your eyes, rinse with plenty of running water and tell a teacher at once. When diluting an acid, always add the acid slowly to the water, never the other way round.

2.Indicators and the pH Scale

An indicator is a substance that changes colour depending on whether it is in acid or alkali. The pH scale runs from 0 to 14 and measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is. Lower pH values mean a higher concentration of H⁺ ions: pH 1 is much more acidic than pH 6. Each step on the scale is a tenfold change in hydrogen-ion concentration.

IndicatorIn acidIn neutralIn alkali

Universal indicator is a mixture that gives a range of colours, so it can show an approximate pH. A pH meter gives a more precise reading. Remember that the exact colours at each pH can vary slightly between brands.

3.Strong and Weak Acids and Alkalis

“Strong” and “weak” describe how much an acid or alkali ionises (splits into ions) in water. This is different from “concentrated” and “dilute”, which describe how much substance is dissolved.

Figure 1. A strong acid such as hydrochloric acid is almost fully ionised in water, while a weak acid such as ethanoic acid is mostly whole molecules with only a few ions.
TypeExamples
Strong acids (fully ionised)Hydrochloric, sulfuric, nitric
Weak acids (partly ionised)Ethanoic, citric, carbonic
Strong alkalis (fully ionised)Sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide
Weak alkali (partly ionised)Ammonia solution

Equal concentrations of a strong and a weak acid have different pH values: the strong acid has the lower pH because it produces more H⁺ ions. A very dilute strong acid can still have a higher pH than a concentrated weak acid, which shows why strength and concentration are separate ideas.

4.Reactions of Acids

Acids have four reactions you must know. In each, a salt is formed. A salt is a compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal (or ammonium) ion. The first part of the salt’s name comes from the metal or base, and the second part from the acid: hydrochloric acid gives chlorides, sulfuric acid gives sulfates, nitric acid gives nitrates, and ethanoic acid gives ethanoates.

Acid reacts with…ProductsExample equationObservation / test
A reactive metalSalt + hydrogenMg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂Fizzing; hydrogen gives a squeaky pop with a lighted splint
A base (oxide or hydroxide)Salt + waterCuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂OBlack solid disappears; blue solution forms
An alkaliSalt + waterNaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂OTemperature rises; indicator changes colour
A carbonateSalt + water + carbon dioxideCaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂Fizzing; CO₂ turns limewater milky

Metals low in the reactivity series, such as copper and silver, do not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid. Very reactive metals such as sodium and potassium react far too violently to be used in this way.

Worked example 1: writing and balancing

Question: Write a balanced equation for zinc reacting with dilute sulfuric acid, and name the salt.

  1. Reactants: Zn and H₂SO₄. Products: a sulfate salt and hydrogen.
  2. Zinc forms Zn²⁺ and sulfate is SO₄²⁻, so the salt is ZnSO₄ (zinc sulfate).
  3. Write: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂.
  4. Check: Zn 1 = 1; H 2 = 2; S 1 = 1; O 4 = 4. Already balanced.

Answer: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂; the salt is zinc sulfate.

5.Neutralisation: What Really Happens

Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and a base (or alkali) to form a salt and water. In terms of ions, every neutralisation in water is the same reaction between hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)
Figure 2. Neutralisation at the ion level. Hydrogen ions (red) from the acid and hydroxide ions (blue) from the alkali move together and join to form water. The other ions stay in solution and form the salt.

Neutralisation is exothermic, so the temperature rises. It is used in many places: antacid tablets neutralise excess stomach acid; farmers add lime to neutralise acidic soil; factories neutralise acidic waste before releasing it; and a bee sting (acidic) or wasp sting (alkaline) can be soothed with a mild base or acid, though medical advice is better for severe stings.

6.Making Salts

The method depends on whether the salt dissolves in water. First, know the solubility rules for common salts:

  • All nitrates are soluble.
  • All salts of sodium, potassium and ammonium are soluble.
  • Chlorides are soluble, except silver chloride and lead(II) chloride.
  • Sulfates are soluble, except barium sulfate and lead(II) sulfate (calcium sulfate is only slightly soluble).
  • Carbonates and hydroxides are insoluble, except those of sodium, potassium and ammonium (calcium hydroxide is slightly soluble).
Figure 3. Choosing a method for making a salt. First ask whether the salt is soluble, then whether the starting base or metal is a solid that does not dissolve.

Method A: insoluble base, metal or carbonate plus an acid

  1. Warm the dilute acid in a beaker.
  2. Add the solid (for example copper(II) oxide) a little at a time with stirring until some is left over, which shows that all the acid has reacted.
  3. Filter to remove the excess solid.
  4. Heat the filtrate gently to evaporate some water until it is saturated, then leave to crystallise.
  5. Filter off the crystals, rinse with a little cold distilled water, and dry them between filter paper.

This makes copper(II) sulfate from copper(II) oxide: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O. Using an excess of the solid makes sure no acid is left in the product.

Method B: titration (soluble alkali plus an acid)

Sodium, potassium and ammonium salts cannot be made by Method A because the alkali dissolves, so you cannot see when it has all reacted or filter out an excess. Instead, a measured volume of alkali is neutralised exactly with acid using an indicator. The experiment is then repeated without the indicator, using the same volumes, and the solution is crystallised.

Method C: precipitation (insoluble salts)

Mix solutions of two soluble salts that contain the ions of the insoluble salt. For example, barium sulfate forms when barium chloride and sodium sulfate solutions are mixed: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s). The solid precipitate is filtered off, washed with distilled water and dried.

7.Titration Calculations

A titration finds the concentration of an unknown acid or alkali. A burette delivers acid into a measured volume of alkali in a conical flask, with an indicator, until the colour changes at the end-point. Phenolphthalein or methyl orange are the usual indicators. The steps for the calculation are always the same:

  1. Write the balanced equation.
  2. Find the amount (moles) of the solution you know: n = concentration (mol/dm³) × volume (dm³).
  3. Use the equation’s ratio to find moles of the unknown.
  4. Find the concentration: c = n ÷ V (in dm³).
Worked example 2: a 1 : 1 reaction

Question: 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution is exactly neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid. Find the concentration of the sodium hydroxide.

  1. Equation: NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O (ratio 1 : 1).
  2. Moles of HCl = 0.100 × 0.0200 = 0.00200 mol.
  3. Moles of NaOH = 0.00200 mol (same ratio).
  4. Concentration of NaOH = 0.00200 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0800 mol/dm³.

Answer: 0.0800 mol/dm³.

Worked example 3: a 1 : 2 reaction

Question: What volume of 0.0500 mol/dm³ sulfuric acid is needed to neutralise 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide?

  1. Equation: H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O (1 acid : 2 alkali).
  2. Moles of NaOH = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol.
  3. Moles of H₂SO₄ = 0.00250 ÷ 2 = 0.00125 mol.
  4. Volume = 0.00125 ÷ 0.0500 = 0.0250 dm³ = 25.0 cm³.

Answer: 25.0 cm³. Always check the mole ratio from the balanced equation.

8.Acids, Bases and Salts in Everyday Life

WhereChemistry
Stomach and antacidsStomach acid is hydrochloric acid; antacids such as magnesium hydroxide or calcium carbonate neutralise excess acid.
FarmingLime (calcium oxide or hydroxide) raises the pH of acidic soil; ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulfate are fertilisers made by neutralising ammonia with acids.
FoodVinegar (ethanoic acid) and citric acid are used for flavour and preservation; baking soda reacts with acid to release carbon dioxide.
IndustrySulfuric acid is one of the most widely produced industrial chemicals, used for fertilisers, detergents, batteries and many other products.
EnvironmentAcid rain from sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides damages buildings, soil and lakes; limestone and lime are used to reduce acidity.
Dental careBacteria turn sugar into acids that dissolve tooth enamel; toothpaste is mildly alkaline.

This article is general education and not medical advice. Do not treat burns or poisoning at home; seek medical help.

9.Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing strong with concentrated. Strength is about ionisation; concentration is about how much is dissolved.
  • Saying all bases are alkalis. Only soluble bases are alkalis.
  • Forgetting the mole ratio in titrations. H₂SO₄ reacts 1 : 2 with NaOH.
  • Mixing up the observation for carbonates. The gas is carbon dioxide (limewater turns milky); hydrogen is for metals (squeaky pop).
  • Using the wrong method for a salt. You cannot filter off “excess” sodium hydroxide, because it dissolves.
  • Adding water to concentrated acid. Always add acid to water.

10.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. What ion do all acids produce in water, and all alkalis?

Answer: Acids produce H⁺; alkalis produce OH⁻.

2. Name the salt and the gas formed when magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid.

Answer: Magnesium chloride and hydrogen.

3. Write the ionic equation for neutralisation.

Answer: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

4. What is the colour of phenolphthalein in alkali?

Answer: Pink.

5. Name the type of reaction and give the products when calcium carbonate reacts with nitric acid.

Answer: A reaction of an acid with a carbonate gives calcium nitrate, water and carbon dioxide.

6. Which method makes barium sulfate, and why?

Answer: Precipitation, because barium sulfate is insoluble: mix a soluble barium salt with a soluble sulfate and filter.

7. 25.0 cm³ of KOH is neutralised by 10.0 cm³ of 0.250 mol/dm³ HCl. What is the concentration of KOH?

Answer: Moles HCl = 0.250 × 0.0100 = 0.00250 mol; KOH = 0.00250 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.100 mol/dm³.

11.Summary

  • Acids produce H⁺ ions; alkalis (soluble bases) produce OH⁻ ions; pH 7 is neutral.
  • Indicators such as litmus, methyl orange, phenolphthalein and universal indicator show acidity or alkalinity.
  • Strong acids and alkalis are fully ionised; weak ones are only partly ionised. This is different from concentration.
  • Acids react with metals, bases, alkalis and carbonates to form salts.
  • Neutralisation is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
  • Soluble salts are made by excess solid, or titration; insoluble salts by precipitation.
  • Titration calculations use n = c × V and the mole ratio from the balanced equation.
For teachers and parents

Suggested use: red-cabbage indicator is a safe way to test household liquids before moving to universal indicator. Titration practice needs burettes, eye protection and careful reading of the meniscus; repeat until two results agree within 0.10 cm³. Parents can connect the topic to antacids, vinegar and soil. Please follow your own syllabus for the required indicator colours and solubility rules.
