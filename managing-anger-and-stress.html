Self-Awareness and Self-Regulation: How to Manage Anger and Stress

What happens in your body and mind when you are angry or stressed, what research says works and what does not, and a practical toolkit you can start using today.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 23 min read | IQ & EQ

Last updated: 12 October 2026. Written by the EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team. This article is for general education and does not replace advice from a qualified professional.

Your heart pounds, your face feels hot, and words you will regret are already forming in your mouth. Or it is the night before an exam, your mind is racing, and you cannot sleep. Anger and stress are among the most common difficult experiences in human life, and they are not signs that something is wrong with you. They are built-in responses. What matters is what happens next. This guide combines two of Goleman's emotional skills, self-awareness (noticing what is happening) and self-regulation (choosing how to respond), and shows what scientific research says about managing anger and stress, including some popular advice that does not work. This article is for learning and general wellbeing and is not a substitute for professional help.

Key Takeaways

Stress and anger are normal alarm systems, and they become a problem when they are too frequent, too intense or badly handled. Noticing and precisely naming a feeling comes first. Venting anger by hitting things or shouting does not reduce it and can make it worse, while rethinking the situation, slow breathing with long exhales, regular exercise, enough sleep, social support and learned cognitive-behavioural skills have good evidence behind them. If anger or stress stays intense for weeks, damages your relationships or daily life, or leads you to think about harming yourself or someone else, speak to a doctor or counsellor, or contact local emergency services.

1.Stress: What Happens in Your Body

When your brain detects a threat or a big demand, it prepares the body to act. The physiologist Walter Cannon called this the fight-or-flight response. The sympathetic nervous system releases adrenaline, which raises heart rate and blood pressure and sends more blood to the muscles. A slower hormone system, the HPA axis (hypothalamus, pituitary and adrenal glands), releases cortisol, which keeps energy available. This is useful when you must run from danger or perform in a big match. It is less helpful when the "threat" is an exam, an argument or a stream of notifications.

Three stages of a long stress response

In 1936 the endocrinologist Hans Selye described the general adaptation syndrome, which has three stages: alarm (the body mobilises), resistance (it keeps coping, often above its normal level) and exhaustion (resources run low if the stress never lets up).

Hans Selye's general adaptation syndrome (1936) in a simplified, schematic form: the body copes well in short bursts, but long, unrelieved stress can wear down its resources.

Later research by Bruce McEwen introduced the idea of allostatic load, the wear and tear that builds up when the body's stress systems are activated again and again without enough recovery. Short bursts of stress that are followed by rest are normal. Stress that never switches off is what causes the greatest concern for health.

How stress shows up

AreaCommon signs
BodyFast heartbeat, tight muscles, headaches, stomach upset, tiredness, trouble sleeping
EmotionsWorry, irritability, feeling overwhelmed, restlessness, low mood
ThinkingRacing thoughts, trouble concentrating, forgetfulness, expecting the worst
BehaviourSnapping at people, avoiding tasks, withdrawing from friends, changes in eating or sleeping, relying on caffeine, alcohol or other substances to cope

These signs can also have medical causes, so if they are severe or persistent, a doctor should check them.

2.It Is Not Only the Event: The Role of Appraisal

Two students face the same difficult exam. One feels sick with dread; the other feels nervous but ready. Why? The psychologists Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman (1984) proposed that stress depends on appraisal: first, we judge whether an event is a threat, a harm or a challenge (primary appraisal), and second, we judge whether we have the resources to cope with it (secondary appraisal). Stress is greatest when we think the demands are higher than our resources.

Threat appraisalChallenge appraisal
The thought"This is too much and I cannot cope.""This is hard, but I can prepare and I will learn from it."
FeelingFear, dread, shameNervous excitement, determination
Typical actionAvoid, freeze, give upPlan, practise, ask for help

Can changing your view of stress really help?

Research suggests that it can in some situations. In a study by Jamieson and colleagues (2010), students preparing for the Graduate Record Examination were told that the nervous feelings of a racing heart can actually help performance. Those students did better on a practice test and, in a follow-up, on the quantitative section of the real test than a comparison group. In a laboratory study with 50 university students (Jamieson and colleagues, 2012), people who were taught to see their stress arousal as helpful showed more efficient cardiovascular responses during a stressful speaking task than people who were told to ignore it or given no instructions.

These are encouraging but limited findings: they involve short-term performance situations in healthy students. They do not mean that long-lasting or severe stress is harmless, or that you should tell anyone in difficulty simply to "think positive". The message is more modest: during a performance moment, telling yourself "this feeling is my body getting ready" can be more useful than "I must calm down at once".

3.Anger: What It Is and What It Is For

Anger is a normal emotion that signals that something seems unfair, blocked or threatening. It can be useful: it can motivate you to stand up for yourself or for others and to fix a problem. The trouble starts when anger is too intense, too frequent, or leads to behaviour that hurts you or other people. It is important to separate three things.

TermMeaningExample
AngerAn emotion, with feelings and body changesFeeling hot and tense when someone cuts in line
HostilityA lasting attitude of ill will or suspicion toward othersAlways assuming that people mean to insult you
AggressionBehaviour intended to harm, in words or actionsShouting insults, pushing, throwing things

Having the emotion of anger is not the problem. Aggression is a choice about behaviour, and it can be changed. Anger management is therefore not about never feeling angry. It is about how you think, what you do with the energy, and how you repair things afterwards.

Common anger triggers

Trigger typeExamples
UnfairnessBeing blamed for something you did not do; unequal rules
DisrespectBeing ignored, mocked or interrupted
Blocked goalsTraffic when you are late; a device that fails before a deadline
Feeling unsafe or hurtCriticism that touches a sore spot; being excluded
Body stateBeing tired, hungry, in pain or already stressed

Anger often travels with other feelings underneath it, such as hurt, fear, embarrassment or disappointment. This is a common observation in counselling rather than a rule for every situation, but it is worth asking yourself: "What else am I feeling?"

A simple model of how anger builds. Each link is a chance to step in.

4.Step One: Self-Awareness

You cannot change a pattern that you do not notice. Good self-regulation begins with three kinds of noticing.

  • Body signals: clenched jaw, tight fists, hot face, fast breathing, a sinking stomach. These usually appear before you speak or act, and they are your early warning.
  • Thoughts: the quick sentence in your mind, such as "He did that on purpose" or "I always fail".
  • Feelings, named precisely: in the previous article we explained that putting a feeling into words reduces the response of the amygdala (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007). Researchers also find that people who can tell their emotions apart in detail, for example separating "irritated", "humiliated", "disappointed" and "worried", have an advantage. In three daily-diary studies of 628 people (Pond and colleagues, 2012), those with higher emotion differentiation reported less aggressive behaviour when they felt angry. A review (Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight, 2015) found that fine-grained emotion words were linked with less use of harmful coping behaviours such as aggression and binge drinking.

Tool: the 0 to 10 feeling scale

Rate your anger or stress from 0 (calm) to 10 (the strongest you have felt). Learn your own levels, for example: 3 = annoyed, 5 = tense, 7 = hard to think clearly, 9 = about to lose control. The aim is to use your tools at 3 to 5, not at 9, because at high levels the thinking part of the brain is not working well.

Tool: the trigger log

What happened?Body signalsThoughtPrecise feelingLevel (0-10)What I didWhat I could try next time
Brother took my chargerHot face, clenched jaw"He never respects my things"Irritated and disrespected6ShoutedBreathe, then ask for it back calmly

After one to two weeks of notes, look for patterns: times of day, places, people, tiredness, and the thoughts that repeat.

5.What Does NOT Work

Venting and "letting it out"

Many people believe that expressing anger physically, by shouting, hitting a pillow or punching a bag, "gets it out of the system". This is the catharsis idea, and research does not support it. In a well-known experiment (Bushman, 2002), about 600 students who were angered by a critical evaluation were assigned to hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who annoyed them (rumination group), to hit the bag while thinking about fitness (distraction group), or to do nothing. The rumination group felt the angriest and then behaved most aggressively in a later task, followed by the distraction group, while the group that did nothing was the least aggressive. The author's conclusion was that venting does not reduce anger and can feed it.

Note the difference between venting and calmly expressing a feeling. Saying "I felt hurt when you said that" in a respectful way is healthy and effective. Shouting or hitting while replaying the offence is not.

Rumination

Rumination means repeatedly going over a problem and its causes without moving towards a solution. A major review (Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky, 2008) found that rumination prolongs and deepens low mood and anxiety. It also keeps anger alive. If you notice yourself replaying a scene for the tenth time, that is a signal to change the channel by using a coping tool, talking to someone or taking a problem-solving step.

Suppression and avoidance

Hiding your feelings, or avoiding everything that upsets you, can bring short-term relief, but as the earlier article showed, habitual suppression is linked with worse well-being and relationships than reappraisal (Gross and John, 2003). Avoidance also keeps you from learning that you can cope.

Using alcohol, drugs or aggression to cope

These may numb feelings briefly but create bigger problems, and they make self-control harder. If you rely on substances to get through the day, it is a good reason to seek support.

6.What Works: A Toolkit

Time frameToolHow it helps

Slow breathing

Breathing is the only part of the stress response that you can control directly. Slow breathing, especially with a longer exhale, tends to calm the body. In a month-long randomised study of 108 healthy adults (Balban and colleagues, 2023), people who practised five minutes a day of an exhale-focused pattern called cyclic sighing reported greater improvement in mood and a larger drop in breathing rate than people who did mindfulness meditation for the same time. All groups, including meditation, improved on mood and anxiety. The study was small, mostly involved university students and was done remotely, so the results are promising and not final.

Cyclic sighing, the exhale-focused breathing pattern tested by Balban and colleagues (2023): the exhale is longer than the inhale.

How to try it: sit comfortably. Breathe in slowly through your nose, then take a second, short sip of air to fill the lungs fully. Breathe out through your mouth slowly, for longer than the inhale. Repeat for a few minutes. If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe normally. People with lung or heart conditions, or who are pregnant, should check with a doctor before trying new breathing exercises.

Rethinking the situation (reappraisal)

In anger, our first thought is often an assumption about the other person's intentions. Reappraisal means generating other possible explanations. Try these questions:

  • What are three other reasons that might explain what happened?
  • Will this matter in a week? In a year?
  • What would I say to a friend in this situation?
  • What part of this can I influence, and what part can I let go?

A related technique is self-distancing: looking at the event from the viewpoint of an observer, as if watching a film of it. Experiments by Bushman and others have found that distancing and calm distraction help reduce anger more than rumination.

7.Cognitive Skills: Catching Thought Traps

Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) is built on the finding that the way we interpret events shapes our emotions and actions. Several thinking habits tend to inflame anger and stress.

Thought trapExampleA more balanced thought
"Should" and "must" demands"People must always treat me fairly.""I would like to be treated fairly, and I can't control others."
Mind reading"She ignored me on purpose.""She may not have seen me. I can ask."
Catastrophising"If I fail this test, my life is ruined.""It would be disappointing, but I would have options."
Labelling"He is an idiot.""He made a poor decision."
All-or-nothing thinking"I lost my temper, so I'm a terrible person.""I handled that badly. I can apologise and do better."
Personalising"They are in a bad mood because of me.""There may be other reasons for their mood."

Evidence supports this approach. A meta-analysis of 40 studies of CBT for anger problems in children and adolescents, covering 1,953 young people (Sukhodolsky, Kassinove and Gorman, 2004), found a medium-sized overall benefit, and that programmes that used more practice, modelling and feedback, and that gave homework, worked better. Earlier reviews of anger treatment in adults (such as Beck and Fernandez, 1998) also reported moderate benefits of CBT. This is why practising the skills between sessions, and not only learning about them, is important.

8.Stress-Management Tools With Evidence

ToolWhat research suggestsNotes and limits
Physical activityA large umbrella review of 97 reviews, 1,039 trials and 128,119 participants (Singh and colleagues, 2023) found medium effects of physical activity on depression, anxiety and psychological distress compared with usual careMost evidence is about mild to moderate symptoms; the review found that higher-intensity activity had larger effects
SleepIn a study of 26 healthy adults, amygdala reactivity to negative images was over 60% greater after a night of sleep deprivation, along with weaker links to the prefrontal cortex (Yoo and colleagues, 2007)Small laboratory study, but consistent with everyday experience that tiredness makes us more reactive
Mindfulness meditationA review of 47 trials with 3,515 people (Goyal and colleagues, 2014) found moderate evidence of small to moderate improvements in anxiety (effect about 0.38 at 8 weeks), depression (about 0.30) and pain, and low evidence for general stressIt was not shown to be better than other active treatments, such as exercise or other behavioural therapies. It is a good option, not a miracle
Social supportResearch on the "buffering" effect (Cohen and Wills, 1985) finds that support from others can protect people from the harm of stressQuality matters more than numbers; one trusted person can help
Problem-focused copingBreaking a problem into steps and acting on what you can controlBest when the stressor can be changed
Emotion-focused copingCalming, reappraising, talking and relaxingBest when the stressor cannot be changed right now

Lazarus and Folkman's distinction between problem-focused and emotion-focused coping is practical. Ask yourself: "Can I do something about the cause?" If yes, make a plan. If not, work on how you respond.

10.A Simple Framework: PAUSE

This five-step memory aid is a teaching tool made for this article that combines the research above. It is not itself a scientifically tested programme.

LetterStepWhat to do
PPauseStop. Take one long exhale. Do not speak or send anything yet.
AAware of the bodyNotice jaw, hands, chest, breathing. Rate the level from 0 to 10.
UUnderstand the feelingName it precisely: "I feel disrespected and embarrassed."
SStep backAsk: What else could explain this? What do I want to happen? Take a time-out if the level is high.
EExpress or Engage wiselySpeak calmly with an "I" statement, make a request, solve the problem, or choose not to engage.
Worked Example 1: A Group Project

Rahim finds out that a teammate has not done his part, two days before the deadline. He feels his face burn (aware, level 7) and thinks, "He is lazy and selfish." He pauses and breathes out slowly, then names the feeling: "I am angry and worried about our grade." He steps back: "Maybe something happened. What do I want? A finished project." He walks for ten minutes, then says, "I'm worried we won't finish on time. Is something getting in the way? How can we split what is left?" The teammate explains that he had been ill and they redistribute the work.

Worked Example 2: Exam Night

Aisha has an exam tomorrow and her thoughts are racing: "I will fail and everyone will be disappointed." She notices that her chest is tight. She names the feeling as anxiety and rates it as 7. She does five minutes of slow breathing with long exhales, writes down the three topics she is most worried about, reviews only those for 30 minutes, then puts the books away and goes to bed on time, reminding herself, "This feeling shows that I care and that my body is getting ready."

Worked Example 3: Repairing After Losing Your Temper

Omar shouted at his sister over a broken headset. Later, when calm, he says: "I'm sorry I shouted. I was angry about the headset, but I should have asked you what happened. Can we talk about how to share things?" Repair matters: a sincere apology, with no excuses, plus a plan for the future, helps trust to recover.

11.Anger, Stress and Relationships

  • Agree on a time-out signal in advance. A pre-agreed word or hand sign lets either person pause without blame. Say when you will come back, such as "I need twenty minutes, then let's continue."
  • Criticise the behaviour, not the person. "The plates were left in the sink" works better than "You are so lazy".
  • Use "I" statements: "I feel [feeling] when [event] because [reason]. I would like [request]."
  • Listen to the other side and repeat it back before answering.
  • Apologise and repair. Name what you did, acknowledge the impact, and say what you will do differently.
  • Look after the basics when conflict is high: sleep, food and exercise.

12.Helping Children and Teenagers

AgeHelpful approaches
Young childrenStay calm yourself; name the feeling ("You look angry because the toy broke"); set a firm limit on behaviour ("It is okay to be angry, it is not okay to hit"); teach "smell the flower, blow out the candle" breathing; praise calm choices
Primary schoolUse a feelings chart; practise a calm-down routine at a quiet moment, not in the middle of a tantrum; read stories about feelings; problem-solve together after the storm
TeenagersRespect their need for independence; discuss triggers; share the PAUSE steps; encourage exercise and sleep; support them to speak to a counsellor if needed
TeachersPredictable routines; a calm corner or break pass; teach SEL skills explicitly; repair relationships after conflicts

Feelings are never "bad". Behaviour has limits, and children learn to manage feelings first by experiencing a calm adult doing it. Programmes that teach these skills in a structured way, with practice, work best, as the evidence on school social and emotional learning programmes also shows.

13.Myths and Facts

MythFact
"Letting it all out" removes angerExperiments found that venting, especially while ruminating, increased anger and aggression
Stress is always harmfulShort-term stress can sharpen focus and help performance; the harm comes from stress that is constant and unrelieved
Calm people do not feel angerCalm people feel it too but notice, name and manage it
You cannot change your temperStudies show that anger-management skills can be learned, with practice
Bottling feelings up is self-controlHabitual suppression is linked with worse well-being; expressing calmly and rethinking work better
Anger management is only for "angry people"Everyone benefits from these skills, like other life skills
Asking for help is a weaknessUsing a counsellor or doctor is a sensible, skilled step, as with any other health issue

14.When to Seek Professional Help

Everyone has hard days. Consider speaking to a doctor, school counsellor, psychologist or another qualified professional if:

  • anger or stress is intense most days and lasts for weeks;
  • it is damaging relationships, schooling or work;
  • you have hurt someone, or are afraid you might, when angry;
  • you cannot sleep, eat or concentrate for a long time;
  • you are using alcohol or drugs to cope;
  • you feel hopeless, or have thoughts of harming yourself.

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, contact your local emergency number or a crisis helpline in your country straight away. If you are a young person, talking to a parent, teacher or another trusted adult is a good first step. This article gives general information and cannot diagnose or treat any condition.

15.Your Four-Week Plan

WeekFocusDaily actions
1NoticeFill in the trigger log once a day; rate your feelings from 0 to 10; use three precise feeling words daily
2Calm the bodyFive minutes of slow breathing with long exhales; a 20 to 30 minute walk or other activity on most days; a regular bedtime
3RethinkEach day, catch one thought trap and write a more balanced version; use the reappraisal questions when upset
4Respond and repairUse PAUSE in at least one real situation; practise one "I" statement; if you lost your temper, apologise and repair; review your log and note progress

A Closing Thought

You will not stop feeling angry or stressed, and you do not need to. Those feelings carry information. What you can learn is to notice them early, name them precisely, calm your body, rethink the story, and then choose a response you will not regret. Each time you do this, even imperfectly, you are building a skill that lasts. Start small: one slow breath before your next reply.
