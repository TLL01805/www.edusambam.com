Effects of Substance Abuse: Smoking, Alcohol & Vaping

Understanding how substances like tobacco, alcohol, and vapes affect the body helps you make informed, confident decisions about your own health.

Substances such as tobacco, alcohol and vapes are easy to find, which can make them seem harmless. But decades of medical research show that they can seriously affect a young, developing body and brain. This article explains in a clear, factual way what these substances do, how addiction develops, how to say no, and where to find help. The aim is knowledge, not fear.

1.What Does "Substance Abuse" Mean?

A substance here means something that changes how the brain and body work, such as nicotine, alcohol, cannabis or certain medicines. Substance abuse means using these substances in a harmful way, for example using them when you are too young, using them in amounts or ways that damage health, or using medicines that were not prescribed for you. Addiction (also called dependence) is the stage where a person feels unable to stop even though the substance is causing harm.

Addiction is a health condition, not a weakness of character. People who struggle with it deserve understanding and medical support.

2.Why Do Some Young People Try Substances?

There is rarely one single reason. Common ones include curiosity, pressure from friends, a wish to fit in, stress, boredom, copying adults or older siblings, and marketing that makes substances look attractive. Some young people use substances to cope with sadness, worry or problems at home, but this usually adds new problems instead of solving the old ones.

The good news is that protective factors make a real difference. These include a supportive family, close friends who make healthy choices, interests and hobbies, regular sport or activity, good coping skills for stress, and an adult you can talk to. Building these protective factors is one of the best ways to lower the risk.

3.Why the Teenage Years Matter

The human brain keeps developing until the mid-20s, and the age of 25 is often used as a rough guide. The parts of the brain that handle planning, decision-making and impulse control, in the front of the brain, mature last. The brain's reward system, which makes new and exciting things feel appealing, is already very active in the teenage years. This mismatch is one reason teenagers are more likely to take risks. It also means that harmful substances can interfere with development at a sensitive time, in ways that can be harder to reverse later.

4.How Addiction Develops

Addictive substances cause a surge of dopamine, a brain chemical associated with pleasure and reward. Over time the brain adapts to expect this surge. It then needs more of the substance to feel the same effect, and this is the beginning of dependency. The steps below show how the cycle can build up.

First useA person tries a substance, often because of curiosity, stress or pressure from others.
↓
2

↓
3

↓
4

The earlier a person starts, the harder it generally becomes to stop later in life. That is why most health guidelines focus on prevention and delay during the teenage years, as well as treatment for people who already struggle.

5.Tobacco and Nicotine

EffectExplanation
AddictionNicotine is highly addictive and changes the brain's reward system quickly.
Lung damageTar and chemicals damage the lungs' ability to absorb oxygen efficiently.
Heart strainNicotine raises heart rate and blood pressure, straining the heart and blood vessels.
Cancer riskTobacco smoke contains thousands of chemicals, many toxic and dozens known to cause cancer.
Harm to othersSecond-hand smoke harms people nearby, especially children.

The World Health Organization estimates that tobacco use kills more than 8 million people every year, including people exposed to second-hand smoke. Smoking is not the only form of tobacco. Shisha (waterpipe) smoke still contains toxic chemicals, and a shisha session can expose a person to a lot of smoke. Smokeless tobacco, such as chewing tobacco, gutka and some kinds of paan with tobacco, contains nicotine and is linked to cancers of the mouth. None of these is a safe alternative.

6.Vaping: Understanding the Risks

E-cigarettes and vapes are sometimes sold as a "safer" option than smoking. For adults who already smoke, some health bodies see vapes as less harmful than cigarettes. But that does not mean vapes are safe, and they are certainly not safe for young people who have never smoked.

Example

Most vapes contain nicotine, the same highly addictive chemical found in cigarettes. A young person who starts vaping can develop the same nicotine dependency as a smoker, along with exposure to chemicals whose long-term effects are still being studied. Studies also find that young people who vape are more likely to start smoking later.

7.Alcohol and the Developing Body

Alcohol is a depressant, which means it slows the brain and body. It affects nearly every organ, but its effect on a young, developing brain is a special concern for researchers.

  • Brain development: early alcohol use is linked to problems with memory and learning later in life.
  • Liver damage: the liver processes alcohol, and repeated use can cause long-term liver damage.
  • Impaired judgement: alcohol reduces coordination and decision-making, which increases the risk of accidents, injuries and unsafe choices.
  • Dependency risk: starting alcohol use at a young age is associated with a higher risk of dependency later.

Laws about alcohol differ between countries, and in many places it is illegal for young people. Many families and faiths also advise against it altogether.

8.Other Substances

  • Cannabis: can affect attention, memory and learning, and regular use in the teenage years is linked to mental health problems. It is illegal in many countries.
  • Misusing medicines: taking painkillers, sedatives or other prescription medicines that were not prescribed for you, or in larger amounts than prescribed, is dangerous and can cause overdose.
  • Illegal and synthetic drugs: these can contain unknown ingredients, so their effects are unpredictable and can be deadly.

9.Effects Beyond the Body

Substance misuse can harm more than health. It can lower school performance, damage trust with family and friends, lead to money problems, increase the risk of accidents and violence, and cause legal trouble. These effects can change a person's whole future, which is another reason prevention matters.

Real-World Example

Public health data consistently shows that the earlier a person starts using tobacco, alcohol or vapes, the harder it typically becomes to stop, and the greater the long-term health risks. This is why national health guidelines focus heavily on prevention and delay during the teenage years, and not only on treatment after dependency has developed.

10.Worked Example: A Friend Offers You a Vape

What would you do?

At a gathering, a friend holds out a vape and says, "Just try it, everyone does."

1Pause. You do not have to answer straight away. Take a breath.
2Think. Most vapes contain nicotine, which is addictive. The idea that "everyone does it" is often not true.
3Choose a reply. A short, calm answer works best: "No thanks, I'm not into that." You do not need to give a long reason.
4Move on. Change the subject, suggest another activity, or walk away. Stay near friends who respect your choice.
5Tell someone. If the pressure continues, talk to a trusted adult. Asking for help is a strong decision.

11.Ways to Say No

  • Say it simply and firmly: "No thanks."
  • Use a reason that is true for you: "I'm in training," "I have a test tomorrow," "I don't want that."
  • Offer an alternative: "Let's play something instead."
  • Leave the situation, or call a trusted adult for a lift.
  • Choose friends who respect your decisions. Real friends will not keep pressuring you.

12.Warning Signs and How to Help a Friend

Signs that someone may be struggling with substances include sudden changes in mood or behaviour, losing interest in activities, falling grades, secrecy, new friends and money going missing. These signs can also have other causes, so do not jump to conclusions.

If you are worried about a friend, speak to them kindly and privately, listen without judging, and encourage them to talk to a trusted adult, a doctor or a school counsellor. Do not try to handle a serious situation alone.

Emergency: if someone cannot be woken, is breathing very slowly or not at all, or is having a seizure after taking a substance, call your local emergency number immediately and follow the steps in our First Aid and Basic Safety article.

13.Common Myths

MythVaping is just water vapour.
FactVape aerosol usually contains nicotine, flavourings and other chemicals, and is not just water vapour.
MythEveryone my age tries it.
FactMany young people do not use these substances. It often looks more common than it is.
MythI can stop whenever I want.
FactNicotine and other substances can cause dependence, and stopping is often harder than people expect.
MythAddiction is a weakness.
FactAddiction is a health condition that changes the brain, and it can be treated with support.

14.Where to Get Help

Help is available and it works. A trusted parent or relative, a family doctor, a school counsellor or teacher, or a local health service can all help. Many countries have confidential helplines and quit-smoking services. Search for the one in your area, and ask a trusted adult to help you if you prefer. Asking for help early makes recovery easier.

Key points to remember
  • The brain keeps developing into the mid-20s, so young people are especially vulnerable.
  • Addictive substances trigger dopamine, and the brain adapts, leading to tolerance and craving.
  • Vapes, shisha and smokeless tobacco are not safe alternatives.
  • Addiction is a health condition, and help is available.
  • Knowing the facts and having a few ready replies makes it easier to say no.
For teachers and parents

Activity: practise "saying no" through short role-plays in class. Discuss the myths and facts, and show students where local help services are found.

For parents: talk openly and calmly, listen more than you lecture, and remember that your own example matters.

Please note: this article is general education and not medical advice. A person who is struggling with substances should be supported by a doctor or qualified counsellor.
