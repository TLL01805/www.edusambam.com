Understanding Vaccines and Immunization

How vaccines build immune memory, the different vaccine types, herd immunity, efficacy, safety and how to judge claims.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|Biology|In-depth learning article|Last updated: October 2026

Vaccines are one of the most effective public-health tools ever developed. They work by letting the immune system practise against a harmless version of a germ so that it can respond fast if the real one arrives. This article explains how that practice works, the different kinds of vaccine, what terms such as herd immunity and efficacy really mean, and how to judge claims about vaccines.

Written for students up to O-Level (grades 8 to 10), and useful for parents and teachers. This is general education, not medical advice. For decisions about your own or your child's vaccinations, talk to a doctor or nurse and follow your national immunisation schedule.

1.What Is a Vaccine?

A vaccine is a medical product that exposes the immune system to an antigen (or the instructions for making one) from a pathogen, without causing the full disease. Vaccination is giving the vaccine; immunisation is the resulting protection. The idea is to build memory cells before you meet the real germ.

The word comes from vacca, Latin for cow: in 1796 Edward Jenner showed that deliberate exposure to cowpox could protect against smallpox. Smallpox was declared eradicated worldwide in 1980, the only human disease so far to be eliminated this way.

Figure 1. Original EDUSAMBAM diagram showing how a vaccine prepares the immune system. The gold dot is animated.

2.Memory in Brief

When an antigen meets matching B and T cells, they multiply and some become memory cells. On a second meeting, the response is faster, larger and longer lasting (the secondary response; see the immune system article for the graph). A vaccine is a safe way to cause that first meeting.

3.Types of Vaccine

TypeWhat it containsExamples (general)
Live attenuatedA living but weakened form of the germMeasles, mumps and rubella (MMR); BCG for tuberculosis; oral polio vaccine in some countries
Inactivated (killed)Germs that have been killedInjected polio vaccine; some influenza and hepatitis A vaccines
Subunit or conjugateOnly selected pieces of the germ, such as a protein or sugarHepatitis B; whooping cough component; some pneumococcal vaccines
ToxoidA harmless, treated form of a toxin made by the germTetanus and diphtheria
mRNAInstructions that let body cells briefly make one antigenSome COVID-19 vaccines
Viral vectorA harmless carrier virus delivering a gene for an antigenSome COVID-19 and Ebola vaccines

Different methods suit different germs. Live vaccines usually produce strong, long-lasting protection but are not suitable for everyone, for example people with seriously weakened immune systems. Vaccines made from pieces or inactivated germs cannot cause the disease they protect against, but may need extra doses or an adjuvant, a substance that helps the immune system respond.

4.Why Some Vaccines Need More Than One Dose

A single exposure may not build enough memory. A second or third dose acts like the secondary response, increasing antibody levels and the number of memory cells. Some protection also fades over time, so booster doses are recommended for certain diseases, such as tetanus. Some germs, such as influenza viruses, change from year to year, so the vaccine is updated regularly.

5.Herd (Community) Immunity

Vaccines protect the person vaccinated, and they can also protect others. If enough people in a community are immune, the germ struggles to find new people to infect, so outbreaks die out. This indirect protection is called herd immunity (or community immunity). It matters for babies who are too young to be vaccinated and for people who cannot be vaccinated for medical reasons.

Figure 2. A simple model: the infection (red) cannot cross the wall of vaccinated people (blue), so those behind them are shielded. Real spread is more complicated.

How many people must be immune depends on how contagious the germ is. This is measured by R0 (the basic reproduction number): the average number of people one case would infect in a population with no immunity. A simple estimate of the share that needs to be immune is:

threshold = 1 − 1 ÷ R0

Figure 3. Herd immunity threshold from the formula, for several R0 values. The more contagious the germ, the higher the threshold.
Worked example: calculate a threshold

A germ has R0 = 4 (a practice value). What share of the population must be immune to stop spread?

  1. Divide: 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25.
  2. Subtract from 1: 1 − 0.25 = 0.75.
  3. Convert to a percentage: 75%.

Check the logic: if each case infects 4 people, then 3 of every 4 contacts must be immune so that each case infects only 1 person on average, and the outbreak stops growing. Real germs have different R0 values in different places, and vaccines are not 100% effective, so real targets are set by health agencies.

6.Efficacy and Effectiveness

Efficacy is how well a vaccine works in a controlled clinical trial. Effectiveness is how well it works in everyday use across a whole population. They can differ because real life includes older people, people with other illnesses, missed doses and new variants.

Vaccine protection is rarely all-or-nothing. A vaccinated person may still catch the disease, but is often much less likely to become seriously ill. 'Vaccine efficacy 90%' does not mean 10% of vaccinated people will get the disease; it means the vaccinated group had 90% fewer cases than the unvaccinated group in the trial.

Worked example: efficacy from trial numbers

Practice values: in a trial, 1000 unvaccinated people had 50 cases, and 1000 vaccinated people had 5 cases.

  1. Risk without vaccine: 50 ÷ 1000 = 0.05 (5%).
  2. Risk with vaccine: 5 ÷ 1000 = 0.005 (0.5%).
  3. Efficacy = (0.05 − 0.005) ÷ 0.05 = 0.045 ÷ 0.05 = 0.9, or 90%.

So the vaccinated group had 90% fewer cases, even though some vaccinated people still became ill.

7.Safety Monitoring

New vaccines go through laboratory testing and several phases of clinical trials with increasing numbers of volunteers before approval. After approval, safety monitoring continues: health agencies collect reports of health events after vaccination and investigate whether a vaccine caused them, because some events happen by coincidence. Like all medicines, vaccines can cause side effects. Common ones are usually mild and short-lived, such as a sore arm or a mild fever. Serious reactions are rare, and a vaccine is recommended when its benefits clearly outweigh its risks. Anyone with a history of a severe allergic reaction should tell the vaccinator.

8.Common Misconceptions

ClaimWhat the evidence supports
Vaccines give you the diseaseInactivated, subunit, toxoid, mRNA and vector vaccines cannot cause the disease. Live attenuated vaccines can occasionally cause a very mild form.
Natural infection is always betterInfection can cause serious illness or death and complications that the vaccine does not. Immunity from some infections is also short-lived.
If vaccinated people still get ill, vaccines don't workProtection is rarely complete; vaccinated people are usually much less likely to become seriously ill.
Too many vaccines overload the immune systemThe immune system handles a huge number of antigens every day; vaccine antigens are a small fraction of these.
Diseases were already disappearing, so vaccines were unnecessaryCases of several diseases fell sharply after the introduction of vaccines in many countries, and outbreaks return where coverage falls.

9.How to Read Vaccine Claims

  1. Who says it? Prefer health agencies, universities and peer-reviewed research over anonymous posts.
  2. What is the evidence? A trial or large study is stronger than a single story.
  3. Compare groups. A claim needs a comparison with an unvaccinated group, not just a list of events after vaccination.
  4. Check for coincidence. Illnesses occur in people whether or not they were vaccinated recently.
  5. Watch for exaggeration. Words like 'always', 'never' or 'guaranteed' are warning signs in any health claim.

10.Vaccines and Public Health

Vaccination programmes have helped eliminate smallpox, have brought polio close to eradication, and have greatly reduced diseases such as measles, diphtheria and tetanus in many countries. Outbreaks of measles can return when too few people are immune, because measles is highly contagious and needs a very high immunity level to stop spread.

11.Key Terms

TermMeaning
AntigenA molecule the immune system recognises
Memory cellLong-lived lymphocyte that gives a fast response on re-exposure
AttenuatedWeakened so that it can no longer cause serious disease
AdjuvantA substance that helps a vaccine produce a stronger response
BoosterAn extra dose that raises or renews protection
Herd immunityIndirect protection when enough people in a community are immune
R0Average number of people one case infects in a fully susceptible population
EfficacyProtection measured in a clinical trial

12.Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Writing that a vaccine 'kills' the germ. It trains the immune system, which then deals with the germ.
  • Mixing up vaccination (the act) and immunisation (the protection).
  • Saying 90% efficacy means 10% of people get the disease.
  • Thinking herd immunity means every person is protected.
  • Believing antibiotics help with viral diseases or replace vaccines.

13.Summary

  • Vaccines present a safe antigen so the body forms memory cells without the disease.
  • Types include live attenuated, inactivated, subunit, toxoid, mRNA and viral vector vaccines.
  • Extra doses act like a secondary response; some protection fades, so boosters are used.
  • Herd immunity protects people who cannot be vaccinated; the threshold depends on R0.
  • Efficacy is measured in trials; effectiveness is measured in real life.
  • Evaluate claims by checking the source, evidence, comparison group and coincidence.

14.Check Your Understanding

1. Explain how a vaccine helps protect against a disease.
Answer: It exposes the immune system to an antigen safely, so B and T cells respond and memory cells form. If the real germ arrives, the response is faster and stronger.
2. Calculate the herd immunity threshold for R0 = 5.
Answer: 1 − 1/5 = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8, or 80%.
3. Why can a vaccine made from killed germs not cause the disease?
Answer: The germs are dead and cannot multiply, so they cannot cause infection. The immune system still recognises their antigens.
4. A trial: 20 cases in 500 unvaccinated, 2 cases in 500 vaccinated. Calculate efficacy.
Answer: Risk unvaccinated = 20/500 = 0.04; vaccinated = 2/500 = 0.004. Efficacy = (0.04 − 0.004) ÷ 0.04 = 0.9, or 90%.
5. Why do some people rely on herd immunity?
Answer: They cannot be vaccinated, for example babies or people with some medical conditions, so protection depends on others being immune.
6. Give one reason to be careful with a post saying 'my relative got ill after a vaccine'.
Answer: It does not show the vaccine caused the illness; illnesses happen by coincidence, and a comparison with an unvaccinated group is needed.

15.For Teachers and Parents

Notes for adults
Suggested use. Teach the memory idea first, then types, then herd immunity. Activity. Use a grid of counters or paper circles (as in Figure 2) and let learners remove 'vaccinated' counters to see outbreaks spread. Teaching note. The herd immunity formula is a simplification that assumes people mix evenly. Discussion should stay evidence-based and respectful. Questions about a particular child's or adult's vaccinations belong with a doctor or nurse. This is general education, not medical advice.
