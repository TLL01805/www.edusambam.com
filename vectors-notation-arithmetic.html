Vectors: Notation, Arithmetic & Magnitude

From column vectors and nose-to-tail addition to magnitude, midpoints, parallel vectors and short geometric proofs, with worked examples and tools to check your work.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|21 min read|Last updated: November 2026|Mathematics

If someone says “walk 5 km”, you still do not know where you will end up. If they say “walk 5 km north-east”, you do. A vector is a quantity with both a size (magnitude) and a direction, unlike a plain number (a scalar) which has only size. Vectors describe movements, velocities, forces and the positions of points, and they have their own arithmetic for adding, subtracting and scaling. This guide builds vector notation and arithmetic from the start, then applies them to geometry: finding midpoints, dividing lines in a ratio and proving that lines are parallel. It also sets up the column vectors used for translations and the matrices that act on them.

1.Scalars and Vectors

Some quantities need only a number. A temperature of 30°C, a distance of 5 km and a time of 2 hours are scalars. Others need a direction as well. “A force of 10 N downwards” and “a wind of 20 km/h from the west” are vectors.

The same idea gives you two pairs of words that sound alike but differ: distance (scalar) and displacement (vector); speed (scalar) and velocity (vector). If you walk 300 m east and then 400 m north, the distance you have travelled is 700 m, but your displacement from the start is 500 m in one particular direction (section 7 shows how).

A vector can be drawn as an arrow. The length of the arrow shows the magnitude and the way the arrow points shows the direction. Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and the same direction, wherever they are drawn on the page. A vector is a movement, and the same movement can start from any point.

2.Notation

There are three common ways to write a vector:

  • A bold lowercase letter such as a in print. By hand, write the letter with a line under it (a with an underline) so it is not mistaken for a number.
  • Two capital letters with an arrow above them, such as AB, meaning “the movement from A to B”. The first letter is the start and the second is the end.
  • A column vector such as 34 (section 3).

The negative of a vector, −a, has the same length but points the opposite way. Because going from B to A undoes going from A to B, BA = −AB. The zero vector 0 = 00 means “no movement”.

3.Column Vectors

A column vector splits a movement into two parts. The top number is the horizontal movement: positive means right, negative means left. The bottom number is the vertical movement: positive means up, negative means down. So 43 means “4 across, 3 up” and −2−5 means “2 left, 5 down”.

Figure 1. The vector from (1, 1) to (5, 4) is 43. The two gold parts draw first, then the red vector.
“The vector 3−2 means 3 up and 2 to the left”

This swaps the order of the components.

The fix: the top number always describes the horizontal movement. So 3−2 means 3 to the right and 2 down. Say “across first, then up (or down)” every time. This is the same order as the coordinates (x, y), and it matches the column vectors used for translations.

4.Adding Vectors

To add two vectors, add the top numbers and add the bottom numbers. The picture is the nose-to-tail rule: draw the second vector starting at the end (the “nose”) of the first. The sum is the single vector from the very start to the very end. It is also called the resultant.

Figure 2. Nose-to-tail addition. b starts where a ends, and a + b joins the start of a to the end of b.
Example 1 · Adding

a = 25, b = 4−1. Then a + b = 2 + 45 + (−1) = 64.

Vector addition is commutative: a + b = b + a (going along a then b arrives at the same place as b then a, the two sides of a parallelogram). It is also associative, and adding the zero vector changes nothing. Adding a vector and its negative gives zero: a + (−a) = 0.

5.Subtracting Vectors

Subtracting a vector means adding its negative: a − b = a + (−b). With column vectors, subtract the top numbers and the bottom numbers.

Example 2 · Subtracting

a = 72, b = 36. Then a − b = 7 − 32 − 6 = 4−4. Check by adding back: b + (a − b) = 3 + 46 + (−4) = 72 = a ✓.

As with ordinary numbers, a − b and b − a are different: they are negatives of each other. Here b − a = −44.

6.Multiplying a Vector by a Number

Multiplying a vector by a scalar k multiplies every component by k. The result is parallel to the original. If k is greater than 1 the vector gets longer, if k is between 0 and 1 it gets shorter, and if k is negative the direction is reversed.

Example 3 · Scaling and combining

(a) a = 3−2. Then 4a = 12−8 and −2a = −64 (the direction is reversed).

(b) ½ 6−4 = 3−2, which is half as long and points the same way.

(c) a = 12, b = −23. Find 3a + 2b. 3a = 36 and 2b = −46, so 3a + 2b = −112.

Example 4 · Finding the multiples

a = 21, b = 13 and c = 89. Find numbers m and n such that ma + nb = c.

The top numbers give 2m + n = 8. The bottom numbers give m + 3n = 9. From the first equation n = 8 − 2m. Substituting into the second: m + 3(8 − 2m) = 9, so m + 24 − 6m = 9, so −5m = −15 and m = 3. Then n = 8 − 6 = 2. Check: 321 + 213 = 63 + 26 = 89 ✓.

7.Magnitude (Length) of a Vector

The magnitude of a vector, written |a| or |AB|, is its length. For xy the two components are the legs of a right-angled triangle (Figure 1), so Pythagoras’ theorem gives:

|xy| = √(x² + y²)

Example 5 · Magnitudes

(a) |512| = √(25 + 144) = √169 = 13.

(b) |−68| = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10. (The negative sign disappears when you square.)

(c) |2−3| = √(4 + 9) = √13 = 3.61 (2 d.p.). Leave it as √13 if an exact answer is asked for.

(d) The walker who goes 300 m east then 400 m north has displacement 300400, with magnitude √(300² + 400²) = 500 m.

A unit vector has magnitude 1. To make a unit vector in the direction of 34, divide by its magnitude 5: 3/54/5 = 0.60.8, and indeed √(0.36 + 0.64) = 1.

“|a + b| = |a| + |b|”

Magnitudes do not add like this, except when the vectors point the same way.

The fix: take a = 30 and b = 04. Then |a| + |b| = 3 + 4 = 7, but a + b = 34 and |a + b| = 5. The straight route is shorter than going along two sides, so |a + b| ≤ |a| + |b|. Always add the vectors first, then find the magnitude of the answer.

8.Position Vectors and the Vector Between Two Points

A position vector shows where a point is, as the movement from a fixed origin O. The position vector of A is OA, usually written a. For the point A(2, 4), a = 24: the coordinates of the point are the components of its position vector.

To get from A to B, you can go back from A to the origin (−a) and then forward to B (b). So

AB = b − a (end minus start)

Figure 3. a and b both start at O. The red vector from A to B is b − a.
Example 6 · Vector between points

A has position vector a = 23 and B has position vector b = 81. Then AB = b − a = 8 − 21 − 3 = 6−2. Its length is |AB| = √(36 + 4) = √40 = 2√10 ≈ 6.32.

“AB = a − b”

This gives the vector from B to A, which points the wrong way.

The fix: the vector AB always ends at the second-named point, so it is “end minus start”: b − a. In Example 6, a − b = −62, which is BA. Test: start at A(2, 3) and add your answer. You should land on B(8, 1): (2 + 6, 3 − 2) = (8, 1) ✓.

9.Parallel Vectors and Points on a Line

Two vectors are parallel if one is a scalar multiple of the other. Parallel vectors point in the same direction (positive multiple) or in exactly opposite directions (negative multiple). Their magnitudes are in the ratio of the scalar.

Example 7 · Testing for parallel

(a) u = 46 and v = 69. Since 4 × 1.5 = 6 and 6 × 1.5 = 9, v = 1.5u, so they are parallel.

(b) 23 and 34. The multipliers 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5 and 4 ÷ 3 = 1.33… are not equal, so they are not parallel.

(c) 23 and −4−6. Here −4 = −2 × 2 and −6 = −2 × 3, so they are parallel and point in opposite directions.

Collinear points. Three points are on one straight line if the vectors joining them are parallel and the vectors share a point. Parallel alone is not enough, since parallel lines never meet.

Example 8 · Are A, B, C in a line?

A(1, 2), B(3, 5), C(7, 11). AB = 23 and BC = 46 = 2 × AB. The vectors are parallel and both contain the point B, so A, B and C lie on a straight line, and BC is twice as long as AB.

“23 and 34 are parallel because they both go up and to the right”

Similar-looking is not the same as parallel.

The fix: divide the matching components. If the same number turns the top of the first vector into the top of the second and the bottom into the bottom, they are parallel. Here 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5 but 4 ÷ 3 = 1.33, so no single scalar works. (Their slopes are 3/2 and 4/3, which are different.)

10.Midpoints and Dividing a Line in a Ratio

The position vector of the midpoint M of AB is the average of the two end points: m = ½(a + b). Reason: M is half-way along AB, so m = a + ½(b − a) = ½a + ½b.

The same idea works for any ratio. If P is on AB with AP : PB = m : n, then AP is m/(m + n) of the way along AB, so

p = a + (m/(m + n))(b − a) = (n·a + m·b) ÷ (m + n)

Example 9 · Midpoints

(a) A has position vector 24 and B has position vector 108. The midpoint is ½1212 = 66.

(b) A(−3, 5) and B(7, −1). The midpoint is ½44 = (2, 2).

Example 10 · A ratio

A(2, 1), B(14, 7). Find P on AB with AP : PB = 1 : 2.

AB = 126. AP is 1/3 of AB, which is 42. So p = 21 + 42 = 63. Check with the formula: (2a + 1b)/3 = (42 + 147)/3 = 189/3 = 63 ✓.

For AP : PB = 3 : 1, AP is 3/4 of AB: p = 21 + ¾126 = 21 + 94.5 = 115.5.

11.Vector Geometry and Proof

Vector proofs have a standard method:

  1. Choose two basic vectors (often two sides of the shape, such as a and c).
  2. Write each line you need in terms of them, by finding a route along known vectors (going backwards along a vector gives its negative).
  3. Simplify, and compare. One vector is a multiple of another? Then the lines are parallel, and the multiple gives the ratio of lengths.
  4. State the geometric conclusion in words.
Example 11 · A parallelogram

OABC is a parallelogram with OA = a and OC = c. M is the midpoint of AB and N is the midpoint of OC. Show that MN is parallel to OA.

Since OABC is a parallelogram, AB = OC = c, so OB = a + c. M is the midpoint of AB, so OM = a + ½c. N is the midpoint of OC, so ON = ½c.

MN = ON − OM = ½c − (a + ½c) = −a.

MN is a multiple (−1) of a, so MN is parallel to OA and the same length (pointing the opposite way).

Example 12 · The midpoint theorem

In triangle OAB, OA = a and OB = b. M is the midpoint of OA and N is the midpoint of OB. Show that MN is parallel to AB and half its length.

OM = ½a and ON = ½b, so MN = ½b − ½a = ½(b − a) = ½AB. So MN is parallel to AB, and its length is half of AB.

Figure 4. The red segment MN joins two midpoints. Vectors show it is half of AB and parallel to it.
Example 13 · Ratio in a triangle

In triangle OAB, OA = a and OB = b. P is on AB with AP : PB = 2 : 1. Find OP.

AB = b − a, and AP is 2/3 of AB, so OP = a + ⅔(b − a) = a + ⅔b − ⅔a = ⅓a + ⅔b. This matches the ratio formula: (1·a + 2·b) ÷ 3.

12.Vectors in Real Life

  • Navigation and wind. A boat heads north across a river at 4 m/s while the river flows east at 3 m/s. Its velocity over the ground is 34 m/s, so its speed is √(3² + 4²) = 5 m/s, and it is carried downstream as it crosses.
  • Forces. Forces are vectors. Two forces of 34 N and −3−4 N add to the zero vector, so the object does not move: the forces are balanced.
  • Games and animation. A character on a screen is moved by adding a velocity vector to its position at every frame. Changing direction means changing the vector.
  • Displacement vs distance. A runner who completes a lap of a track finishes with zero displacement, though she has travelled 400 m.
  • Transformations. A translation is a column vector, and a reflection or rotation can be written as a matrix acting on position vectors. See Transformations and Matrices.

13.Common Mistakes

  • Reading the components the wrong way round. Top = across (x), bottom = up (y).
  • AB = a − b. It is b − a: end minus start.
  • Adding magnitudes instead of adding the vectors first.
  • Forgetting the square root when finding a magnitude, or squaring a negative component wrongly (use brackets: (−5)² = 25).
  • Calling vectors parallel because they “look similar”. Check for a single scalar multiple.
  • Concluding collinear from parallel alone. The vectors must also share a point.
  • Sign errors with negatives when subtracting: a − b with b = 3−2 means subtracting −2, which adds 2.
  • Forgetting to simplify in a proof, so the multiple is never seen.

15.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [3 marks] a = 3−4 and b = −12. Find (a) 2a + b and (b) |a|.
  2. [3 marks] A is (−2, 3) and B is (4, −1). Find AB and its magnitude, giving your answer in surd form and as a decimal to 2 d.p.
  3. [3 marks] p = 69 and q = −4−6. Show that p and q are parallel. Are they in the same direction?
  4. [3 marks] a = 21, b = 13 and c = 89. Find the numbers m and n such that ma + nb = c.
  5. [3 marks] A(1, 2), B(3, 5) and C(7, 11). Show that A, B and C are collinear.
  6. [4 marks] OABC is a parallelogram with OA = a and OC = c. M is the midpoint of AB and N is the midpoint of BC. Find MN in terms of a and c, and state what it shows about MN and AC.
  7. [4 marks] In triangle OAB, OA = a and OB = b. P is the point on AB such that AP : PB = 2 : 1. Express OP in terms of a and b.
Show answers

1. (a) 2a = 6−8, so 2a + b = 5−6. (b) |a| = √(9 + 16) = 5.

2. AB = 4 − (−2)−1 − 3 = 6−4. Magnitude = √(36 + 16) = √52 = 2√13 ≈ 7.21.

3. p = −1.5q, since −1.5 × (−4) = 6 and −1.5 × (−6) = 9. So p and q are parallel, and because the multiple is negative they point in opposite directions.

4. 2m + n = 8 and m + 3n = 9. From the first, n = 8 − 2m. Then m + 24 − 6m = 9, so m = 3 and n = 2. Answer: m = 3, n = 2.

5. AB = 23 and BC = 46 = 2AB. The vectors are parallel and share the point B, so the three points are collinear.

6. OB = a + c. M = midpoint of AB, so OM = a + ½c. N is the midpoint of BC: ON = c + ½a. MN = c + ½a − a − ½c = ½c − ½a = ½(c − a). Since AC = c − a, MN = ½AC: MN is parallel to AC and half its length.

7. AB = b − a, and AP is two-thirds of AB. OP = a + ⅔(b − a) = ⅓a + ⅔b.

16.Key Points

  • A vector has magnitude and direction. Equal vectors have the same magnitude and direction, wherever they are drawn.
  • Column vector xy: x across (right is positive), y up (up is positive).
  • Add or subtract top with top and bottom with bottom. Multiply by a scalar by scaling both components. The result of scaling is parallel to the original.
  • |xy| = √(x² + y²). In general |a + b| ≠ |a| + |b|.
  • AB = b − a (end minus start). BA = −AB.
  • Midpoint: ½(a + b). AP : PB = m : n gives p = (n·a + m·b) ÷ (m + n).
  • Parallel vectors are scalar multiples of each other. Collinear points need parallel vectors that share a point.
  • For proofs: choose two basic vectors, write every line in terms of them, simplify, and state the conclusion in words.

17.For Teachers and Parents

Begin with a treasure-map activity on squared paper: each instruction is a column vector, and a route is a chain of them added nose to tail. Let learners predict the final position before drawing it, and then check by adding components. Draw vectors in two colours so that learners can see AB = b − a as a path through the origin. For proofs, give learners a sentence frame: “MN = … = … so MN is parallel to … and … as long.” The step that pupils most often skip is the final sentence in words, which carries the marks. Real contexts such as boats crossing rivers, forces on a rope and moving characters on a screen show why a direction needs a number in each direction.
