Constructions & Loci

Learn exact ruler-and-compass constructions, perpendicular and angle bisectors, standard angles and triangles from three sides, then the four standard loci and how to shade regions that combine two rules.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|20 min read|Last updated: November 2026|Mathematics

Constructions and loci are the drawing side of geometry. A construction is an exact drawing made with a pair of compasses and a ruler, so every angle and length comes from the method rather than from measuring. A locus (plural loci) is the set of all points that follow one rule, such as “exactly 4 cm from a fixed point”. Together they answer practical questions: where should a mobile mast cover, how far can a goat roam on a rope, and which houses are nearer to one school than to another? This guide teaches each construction step by step, explains why it works, builds triangles from their sides, and then shows how to draw and combine the four standard loci.

1.Tools and Rules for Constructions

In a formal construction the key tools are a pair of compasses (to draw arcs and circles of a fixed radius) and a straight edge (to draw straight lines). In school examinations a ruler marked in centimetres is also used to measure the given lengths, for example to set the compasses to 5 cm. What you must not do is measure or copy angles with a protractor when the question says “using ruler and compasses only”.

  • Use a sharp pencil and keep the compass setting fixed while drawing one arc.
  • Leave all construction arcs and lines on the page. They are the evidence of your method, and examiners award marks for them.
  • Draw final answers (such as a bisector) as clear solid lines, and construction arcs lighter.
  • Label points with capital letters so you can refer to them.

2.The Perpendicular Bisector

The perpendicular bisector of a line segment AB crosses AB at its midpoint and at a right angle. Follow these steps.

  1. Open the compasses to a radius more than half the length of AB.
  2. With the point on A, draw an arc above and below the line.
  3. Keeping the same radius, put the point on B and draw arcs that cross the first ones. Call the crossing points C and D.
  4. Join C and D with a straight edge. The line CD is the perpendicular bisector, and it meets AB at the midpoint M.
Figure 1. Equal arcs from A and from B meet at C and D. The line CD cuts AB in half at a right angle.

Why it works. The four lengths AC, BC, AD and BD are all equal, because they are all the same compass radius. A four-sided shape with four equal sides is a rhombus, and the diagonals of a rhombus cross at 90° and cut each other in half. Here the diagonals are AB and CD.

“I set my compasses to exactly half of AB, so the arcs meet in the middle.”

What happens to the arcs at that setting?

The fix: with exactly half of AB the two arcs only touch at M, so you get just one point and cannot draw the line. With less than half they never meet. You need a radius greater than half of AB so that the arcs cross at two points, one on each side of AB.

3.The Angle Bisector

An angle bisector cuts an angle exactly in half.

  1. With the compass point on the vertex V, draw an arc that crosses both arms. Call the crossing points X and Y.
  2. With the point on X, and then on Y, draw arcs of the same radius so that they cross inside the angle at Z.
  3. Draw the line from V through Z. This line is the bisector.
Figure 2. VZ bisects the angle at V, because triangles VXZ and VYZ are identical.

Why it works. VX = VY (the first arc), XZ = YZ (the equal arcs), and VZ is shared. Two triangles with three equal sides are identical, so the angles XVZ and YVZ are equal.

4.Perpendiculars to a Line

Example 1 · A perpendicular from a point to a line

To drop a perpendicular from a point P to a line l (P is not on the line): put the compass point on P and draw an arc that cuts l at two points, S and T. Then construct the perpendicular bisector of ST. It passes through P, because P is the same distance from S and from T.

Example 2 · A perpendicular at a point on a line

To draw a perpendicular at a point P that is on the line: with the compass point on P, draw two arcs of the same radius that cut the line on both sides of P, at S and T. Then construct the perpendicular bisector of ST. It passes through P and is at 90° to the line.

5.Standard Angles

Some angles can be constructed exactly. Start with 60° and 90°, then bisect or add.

  • 60°: draw a line and mark a point O on it. With the compass point on O, draw an arc crossing the line at P. Keep the same radius, put the point on P, and draw a second arc across the first, at Q. The angle QOP is 60°, because OPQ is an equilateral triangle (OP = OQ = PQ).
  • 90°: use the perpendicular at a point from Example 2.
  • 30°, 45° and 15°: bisect 60° to get 30°, bisect 90° to get 45°, bisect 30° to get 15°.
  • Adding angles: 75° = 60° + 15°, 105° = 90° + 15°, 120° = 60° + 60°, 135° = 90° + 45°.

6.Constructing Triangles

To construct a triangle from three side lengths (SSS), draw the longest side as a base. Then find the third corner as the crossing of two arcs.

Figure 3. Triangle ABC with AB = 7 cm, AC = 5 cm and BC = 6 cm.
Example 3 · Construct a triangle with sides 7 cm, 5 cm and 6 cm
  1. Draw AB = 7 cm.
  2. Set the compasses to 5 cm. With the point on A, draw an arc above AB.
  3. Set the compasses to 6 cm. With the point on B, draw an arc to cross the first at C.
  4. Join AC and BC. Do not rub out the arcs.

A check you can do. Measuring afterwards with a protractor is a fair way to check (not to construct). By the cosine rule the angles should be about A = 57.1°, B = 44.4° and C = 78.5°, which add to 180°.

When it is impossible. The arcs only cross if the two shorter sides together are longer than the longest side. A triangle with sides 2 cm, 3 cm and 6 cm cannot exist, because 2 + 3 = 5 is less than 6. The arcs fall short and never meet.

For a triangle given as two sides and the angle between them (SAS), draw one side, construct the angle at one end (for example 60° or 90°), mark off the second side with the compasses, and join. For two angles and the side between them (ASA), draw the side and construct the two angles at its ends. The rays cross at the third corner.

7.What Is a Locus?

A locus is the set of all points that satisfy one rule. The word is Latin for “place”. A locus can be a line, a curve, or a region. The rule is usually about distance, and there are four standard rules.

The ruleThe locusHow to draw it
A fixed distance from a pointA circleCompasses, centred on the point
A fixed distance from a line segmentA stadium (running-track) shapeParallel lines at that distance, plus semicircles at the ends
Equidistant from two points A and BThe perpendicular bisector of ABConstruction in Section 2
Equidistant from two lines that crossThe two angle bisectorsConstruction in Section 3

8.Rule 1 and Rule 2: Fixed Distances

From a point. The points exactly 4 cm from P form a circle of radius 4 cm. The points within 4 cm form the whole disc (the circle and its inside).

From a line segment. Points exactly 2 cm from segment AB lie on two straight lines parallel to AB, one on each side, joined at each end by a semicircle of radius 2 cm centred on A and B. The result is a “stadium”. Points within 2 cm of AB fill the inside of the stadium. If you drew a rectangle with square corners, you would wrongly include points more than 2 cm from the end of AB.

Figure 4. The region within 2 cm of segment AB. The dashed edge shows points exactly 2 cm away.
“The locus within 2 cm of AB is a rectangle round AB.”

Check a corner of the rectangle.

The fix: the corner of that rectangle is about 2.8 cm from the end of AB, since √(2² + 2²) ≈ 2.83 cm. That is further than 2 cm, so it must not be in the locus. Replace the corners with semicircles of radius 2 cm.

9.Rule 3 and Rule 4: Equidistant Loci

Two points. Every point on the perpendicular bisector of AB is the same distance from A as from B. Points on one side are nearer to A, and points on the other side are nearer to B.

Example 4 · Using the right-angled triangle

P lies on the perpendicular bisector of QR, with QR = 8 cm and PQ = 5 cm. How far is P from the midpoint M of QR?

QM = 4 cm, and PM is at 90° to QR. By Pythagoras, PM² = 5² − 4² = 25 − 16 = 9, so PM = 3 cm.

Two lines that cross. Points the same distance from both lines lie on the angle bisectors. Two crossing lines make two pairs of angles, so there are two bisectors, at 90° to each other. In many exam questions only one is needed, but be ready to use the one in the angle named in the question.

Two parallel lines. The points equidistant from both lie on a third parallel line exactly halfway between them.

Example 5 · A point 4 cm from both arms of a 60° angle

The point is on the bisector of the angle, which makes 30° with each arm. In the right-angled triangle formed by the vertex V, the point P and the foot of the perpendicular on one arm, sin 30° = 4 ÷ VP. So VP = 4 ÷ 0.5 = 8 cm.

10.Combining Loci into Regions

Many questions ask for a region that satisfies two or more rules at once. Use this method.

  1. Draw each boundary accurately (a circle, a bisector, and so on).
  2. For each boundary, decide which side you want. Pick a test point and check the rule.
  3. The answer is where all the wanted sides overlap. Shade it and label it R.
Figure 5. Region R is inside the circle and on A’s side of the perpendicular bisector.
Example 6 · Two masts

Masts are at A and B, 8 km apart. The signal from A reaches 5 km. Shade the region that is within 5 km of A and closer to A than to B. Find the length of the bisector inside the circle.

Draw the circle of radius 5 km around A. Draw the perpendicular bisector of AB, which is 4 km from A. Shade the part of the circle on A’s side. The bisector meets the circle where its distance from A is 5 km. Half the chord is √(5² − 4²) = 3 km, so the chord is 6 km long.

Example 7 · A goat and a wall

A goat on a 4 m rope is tied to a post P. A long straight wall is 2 m from P, and the goat cannot cross it. Find the area it can reach.

The full circle has area π × 4² = 50.27 m². The part beyond the wall is a circle segment. In the right-angled triangle from P to the wall, cos x = 2 ÷ 4, so x = 60°, and the segment’s angle at P is 2 × 60° = 120°. The segment area is the sector minus the triangle: (120 ÷ 360) × π × 16 − ½ × 4 × 4 × sin 120° = 16.76 − 6.93 = 9.83 m². The goat can reach 50.27 − 9.83 = 40.44 m².

“Within 5 km of A means I draw only the circle.”

Does the circle line cover the points inside?

The fix: the circle line is only the points exactly 5 km away. “Within” means every point up to 5 km, so you must shade the inside of the circle. Similarly, “closer to A than to B” means shading a whole side of the bisector, not just drawing the bisector.

11.Where Loci Are Used

  • Coverage and range. The reach of a mobile mast, a radio transmitter or a garden sprinkler is a circle, and several of them overlap into regions.
  • Nearest-point zones. “Closer to A than to B” is how a city can be split into zones, each served by its nearest school, hospital or fire station. When this is done for many points the regions are bounded by perpendicular bisectors. Mathematicians call such a map a Voronoi diagram.
  • Safety zones. The area within a fixed distance of a power line, a pipeline or a runway is a stadium-shaped locus.
  • Siting. A bus stop equidistant from two crossing roads lies on an angle bisector. A well equidistant from two villages lies on a perpendicular bisector.

12.A Short History

The ruler-and-compass tradition goes back to ancient Greek geometry. The geometer Euclid wrote the Elements around 300 BCE, and his geometry is built on constructions made with a straight edge and compasses. Three problems from that tradition puzzled people for about two thousand years: trisecting any angle, doubling a cube, and “squaring the circle”. In the 19th century mathematicians proved that none of them can be done with a straight edge and compasses alone. Pierre Wantzel proved the first two impossible in 1837, and Ferdinand von Lindemann’s 1882 proof that π is transcendental settled the third. Some particular angles, such as 90°, can still be trisected: only a general method is impossible.

13.Common Mistakes

  • Using a protractor when the question says ruler and compasses only.
  • Rubbing out construction arcs. They are the evidence of your method.
  • Changing the compass radius in the middle of a construction that needs equal arcs.
  • Mixing up the two bisectors. Equidistant from two points gives a perpendicular bisector. Equidistant from two lines gives an angle bisector.
  • Drawing only the boundary when the question asks for a region.
  • Drawing the wrong side. Test one point on each side before you shade.
  • A stadium with square corners instead of semicircular ends.
  • Forgetting the triangle rule. If two sides together are not longer than the third, the arcs will not cross.
“I used a protractor to check my 60° angle, so I rubbed out the arcs since they were no longer needed.”

Two problems here.

The fix: checking is fine, but the arcs are your proof and examiners award marks for them. Never erase them. If the question says “construct”, the angle must come from arcs, not from a protractor.

14.Try It Yourself

  1. Describe how to construct 75° using only ruler and compasses.
  2. Points P and Q are 6 cm apart. Describe the locus of points that are 5 cm from both. How far is each from the midpoint of PQ?
  3. Can you construct a triangle with sides 4 cm, 4 cm and 9 cm? Explain.
Show answers

1. From the same vertex and the same base line, construct a 60° angle and a 90° angle. The gap between their arms is 30°. Bisect that gap: it splits into two 15° angles, so the bisector makes 60° + 15° = 75° with the base line.

2. It is on the perpendicular bisector of PQ and 5 cm from P. The midpoint is 3 cm from P, so the distance from the midpoint is √(5² − 3²) = √16 = 4 cm. There are exactly two such points, one on each side of PQ.

3. No. The two shorter sides add to 4 + 4 = 8 cm, which is less than 9 cm. The arcs would not meet.

15.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [4 marks] Construct triangle ABC with AB = 7 cm, AC = 5 cm and BC = 6 cm. Measure angle ACB.
  2. [3 marks] PQ = 8 cm. The point X is on the perpendicular bisector of PQ and PX = 5 cm. Find the distance from X to the midpoint of PQ.
  3. [3 marks] Show how to construct an angle of 15° without a protractor.
  4. [4 marks] Find the area of the region within 3 cm of a line segment of length 6 cm. Use π = 3.142.
  5. [4 marks] Masts at A and B are 10 km apart. The signal from A reaches 7 km. Describe the region that receives A’s signal and is closer to A than to B, and find the length of the bisector inside the circle.
  6. [3 marks] A dog is tied with a 3 m rope to a corner of a rectangular lawn 8 m by 5 m. Find the area it can reach.
  7. [3 marks] A point is 4 cm from both arms of a 60° angle. Find its distance from the vertex.
Show answers

1. Draw AB = 7 cm. Draw an arc of radius 5 cm from A and an arc of radius 6 cm from B, crossing at C. Join AC and BC. Angle ACB should measure about 78.5° (cos C = (36 + 25 − 49) ÷ 60 = 0.2, so C = 78.46°). Accept answers from about 77° to 80°. The arcs must be left visible.

2. The midpoint M is 4 cm from P, and XM is at right angles to PQ. XM² = 25 − 16 = 9, so XM = 3 cm.

3. Construct 60° (an equilateral triangle). Bisect it to make 30°. Bisect 30° to make 15°. Leave all the arcs.

4. The region is a 6 cm by 6 cm rectangle (3 cm each side of AB) plus two semicircles of radius 3 cm, which together make one circle. Area = 6 × 6 + 3.142 × 3² = 36 + 28.278 = 64.28 cm² (about 64.3 cm²).

5. The region is inside the circle of radius 7 km around A and on A’s side of the perpendicular bisector of AB. The bisector is 5 km from A, so half the chord is √(7² − 5²) = √24 = 4.90 km, and the chord is about 9.80 km.

6. The two edges at the corner block the rope (3 m is less than 5 m and 8 m), so the dog reaches a quarter circle: ¼ × π × 3² = 7.07 m², about 7.07 m².

7. The point is on the bisector, which makes 30° with each arm. sin 30° = 4 ÷ d, so d = 4 ÷ 0.5 = 8 cm.

16.Key Points

  • A construction is exact. Use compasses and a straight edge, keep the arcs, and never use a protractor when told not to.
  • Perpendicular bisector: equal arcs, with a radius more than half of AB, from both ends.
  • Angle bisector: an arc across the arms, then equal arcs from the two crossing points.
  • 60° comes from an equilateral triangle, and 90° from a perpendicular. Bisect and add to get 30°, 45°, 15°, 75°, 105° and more.
  • A triangle from three sides needs the two shorter sides to add to more than the longest.
  • The four loci: a circle, a stadium, a perpendicular bisector, and angle bisectors.
  • For a region, shade the whole area that satisfies all the rules, and test a point to choose the side.

17.For Teachers and Parents

Learners often copy the steps of a construction without understanding them. Ask “why do the arcs have to be the same size?” and let them discover the rhombus, or the identical triangles, for themselves. Have them construct each bisector on tracing paper, then fold along it to check. For loci, ask learners to act it out: mark two points on the floor and ask classmates to stand where they are the same distance from both. The Region Builder on this page lets them switch rules on and off and see the shaded region change, and the Locus Test lets them check any point against a rule. Insist on compasses, a sharp pencil and a clear page. A protractor may be used to check a finished construction, never to make it.
