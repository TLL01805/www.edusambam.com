Know Your Emotions: Interactive Feelings Wheel

Name what you feel with a few taps. Explore six core feelings and 108 precise words, practise with real-life scenarios, and see what research says about why naming feelings helps.

Last updated: 12 October 2026. Written by the EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team. This article is for general education and does not replace advice from a qualified professional.

"How are you feeling?" Many of us answer with one of three words: fine, bad or tired. Yet the difference between feeling disappointed, embarrassed and overwhelmed is large, and each one calls for a different response. A feelings wheel is a simple tool that helps you move from a general word to a precise one. Below you will find a free interactive wheel with six core feelings, 36 groups and 108 specific words, practice situations, a word search, and tips for what to do next. After the tool, we explain what research says about why naming feelings is useful, how to use the wheel with children, students and adults, and where its limits lie.

Key Takeaways

Putting a feeling into words can reduce its intensity, and people who can tell their emotions apart in detail tend to react less aggressively when angry. A feelings wheel is a learning aid for building that skill. Start with the core feeling closest to yours, narrow it down, and add a "because". It is normal to feel more than one thing at once. The wheel is not a test or a diagnosis. If feelings stay very strong or painful for weeks, talk to a doctor, counsellor or trusted adult.

1.Why Naming Feelings Helps

Two lines of research explain why a wheel like this can be useful, and we have met both earlier in this series.

  • Affect labelling. In a brain-imaging study (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007), people who put the emotion shown in a picture into words showed a weaker response in the amygdala than people who did other tasks with the same pictures, along with more activity in a region of the right prefrontal cortex linked with control. Naming a feeling appears to take some of its sting away.
  • Emotion differentiation. This is the ability to tell similar feelings apart in detail, for example separating "irritated", "humiliated" and "disappointed" instead of using one word, "upset". In three daily-diary studies of 628 people (Pond and colleagues, 2012), those with higher emotion differentiation reported less aggressive behaviour when they felt angry. A review (Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight, 2015) linked fine-grained emotion words with less use of harmful coping behaviours such as aggression and binge drinking.

These studies show links and brain responses. They do not prove that using a wheel will change your life, but they give a good reason to practise precise emotional language, which is exactly what the wheel is designed to do.

2.The Interactive Feelings Wheel

How to use it: 1) tap the colour closest to how you feel, 2) tap the group that fits better, 3) tap the word that fits best. Then add a "because", rate the strength of the feeling, and see ideas for what to try. You can name up to three feelings, because feelings often come in mixtures. The same choices appear as buttons under the wheel, which are easier to use on a phone or with a keyboard.

3.How to Read the Wheel

  • Centre to edge means general to specific. The first level has six core feelings, the second has six groups inside each, and the third has three specific words in each group.
  • There is no single right answer. Two people can feel different things in the same situation, and you may find that two words fit at the same time.
  • Look for the body clues. A tight chest, hot face, heavy shoulders or a fluttering stomach can point you towards the right family of words.
  • Add a reason. The sentence "I feel [word] because [reason]" turns a vague mood into something you can understand and act on.
  • Rate the strength. As we explained in our article on anger and stress, it is easier to use calming tools at a level of 3 to 5 than at 9.
  • Be kind to yourself. All feelings are information. The aim is to notice, not to judge.

4.Where Feelings Wheels Come From

Wheels of emotion have a long history in psychology. In 1980, the psychologist Robert Plutchik proposed eight primary emotions (joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger and anticipation), arranged in four pairs of opposites, with each shown at different intensities (for example, annoyance, anger and rage) and with neighbouring emotions combining into others. A widely used teaching tool, the Feeling Wheel, was designed by Gloria Willcox (1982), with six core feelings that branch into more specific words. The wheel on this page is an original, simplified design for learners, built on the same idea. It is a learning aid, not a clinical instrument, and the grouping of words is a practical choice, not a scientific law.

The same family of feelings can be mild or very strong. This table shows common words arranged by strength. It is based on everyday language, not on measurements, and people may place words a little differently.

Using a milder word when a feeling is mild, and a stronger word when it is strong, makes your message accurate. Saying "I'm furious" when you are mildly annoyed can cause unnecessary conflict, while saying "I'm fine" when you are very hurt may keep others from helping.

Notice that a single event can bring several feelings at once, such as relieved and proud, or hurt and angry. Naming both is more honest and more useful than choosing one.

8.A Three-Minute Daily Check-In

  1. Pause and take one slow breath with a long exhale.
  2. Scan your body from head to feet and notice where you feel something.
  3. Find your word on the wheel, from core to specific.
  4. Add a "because" in one sentence.
  5. Choose one small action: a tool from the list, a conversation or a rest.

After a week, look back through your notes. Are there patterns in the times of day, people or places? You can combine this with the trigger log from our article on managing anger and stress.

9.Using the Wheel With Others

WhoSuggestions
Young children (about 4 to 7)Use only the six core feelings, with faces or colours. Name the child's feeling for them ("You look frustrated"), and read stories about feelings
Children (about 8 to 12)Add the groups. Use the wheel at bedtime or after school, and let children draw the feeling too
TeenagersOffer the full wheel privately, without pressure to share. Respect their privacy and listen before advising
TeachersUse a wheel poster for morning check-ins and journals. Let students pass if they prefer not to share, and model it by naming your own feelings calmly
FamiliesDo a dinner check-in: each person names a high point, a low point and a feeling word. Respond with curiosity, not correction
Adults and workplacesUse it for personal reflection and in supportive team conversations. Do not force anyone to share feelings at work or use the wheel to judge people

For ideas on responding to someone's feelings, see our articles on empathy and social skills and EQ at school, home and work.

10.Limits and Cautions

  • It is a learning aid, not a test. It cannot diagnose a condition or measure your emotional intelligence.
  • Words never capture everything. If no word fits, use your own, or describe the sensation.
  • Cultures and languages differ. Families and cultures carve up feelings in different ways, and some words feel natural in one language and awkward in another.
  • Feelings are not facts. "I feel useless" is a feeling, not proof that you are useless. Look for evidence and ask a trusted person.
  • Naming is the start, not the end. The wheel works best with the other skills in this series: breathing, rethinking the situation, listening and asking for help.
  • Know when to get help. If strong sadness, worry, anger or emptiness lasts for weeks, interferes with school, work or relationships, or comes with thoughts of harming yourself or others, speak to a doctor, counsellor or trusted adult. If anyone is in immediate danger, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line in your country.

11.Myths and Facts

MythFact
"Talking about feelings makes them worse"Putting feelings into words is linked with lower amygdala responses, though rumination (going round and round) can make things worse
"Feelings are either good or bad"Feelings are signals; even uncomfortable ones carry information
"You should only feel one thing at a time"Mixed feelings are very common
"A feelings wheel tells you what you really feel"It helps you search for words, but only you can decide what fits
"Big words are always better"The best word is the one that fits, at the right strength
"Boys and men do not need emotion words"Everyone benefits from precise emotional language

A Closing Thought

You do not need a perfect word. You only need a slightly better word than yesterday's. Each time you move from "bad" to "left out", or from "stressed" to "swamped by three deadlines", you give yourself and the people around you a clearer picture of what is going on, and a better chance of choosing what to do next.
