Text Structure & Organization

Writers organize ideas in patterns — and once you recognize the pattern, you can predict where a passage is headed before you even finish reading it.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 19 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Reading Comprehension
🔊 LISTEN TO THIS ARTICLE
SAVE YOUR EYES • IMPROVE YOUR LISTENING

No good passage is a heap of random facts. Behind every paragraph or article sits a pattern the writer chose on purpose, and once you can see the pattern, you can predict what comes next, find the main idea faster and remember far more.

This guide explains the five main text structures (sequence, cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution, and description), the signal words that point to each, the traps that make signal words misleading, and a five-step routine for naming the structure of any passage. It includes two worked examples, a checklist, common myths and ten practice questions. Spelling follows British English.

It works best alongside Main Idea & Supporting Details, Making Inferences & Drawing Conclusions, Author's Purpose, Tone & Point of View and Context Clues. If you write as well as read, see How to Write a Perfect Paragraph and Essay Structure.

1.What Is Text Structure?

Text structure is the pattern a writer uses to organise ideas in a passage. The same facts can be arranged in different ways, depending on what the writer wants the reader to understand. Take one topic, the invention of the bicycle:

  • Told in order (first the hobby-horse, then the pedals, then the chain), it is a sequence.
  • Told as a puzzle (why did riders keep falling off?) with an answer, it is problem and solution.
  • Set beside the horse-drawn carriage, it is compare and contrast.

Three different shapes, one subject. The shape changes how you read and what you remember.

Do not confuse these three ideas:

IdeaWhat it isExample
Text structureHow the ideas are organisedCause and effect
Text featureA visible help on the pageHeadings, bold words, captions
GenreThe kind of textNews report, recipe, essay

Features and genre give clues about structure, but they are not the same thing. A news report (genre) may use cause and effect (structure) with a bold heading (feature).

2.Why Text Structure Matters

  • It lets you predict. If a paragraph begins "There are two main causes…", you know two causes are coming and you read to find them.
  • It points to the main idea. Each structure keeps its main idea in a typical place (see section 9).
  • It helps you remember. Ideas stored as "problem, then three fixes" are easier to recall than a list of sentences.
  • It makes summaries easier. The structure gives you a ready-made frame: "X happened because of A and B, which led to C."
  • It makes you a better writer. When you choose a structure on purpose, your own essays become clearer.

Research supports this. Two large reviews of studies, reported by the literacy researcher Timothy Shanahan, found that teaching students about text structure improved their comprehension of factual (expository) texts. A separate review of studies in the upper primary years found a positive effect straight after teaching, but noted that the benefit faded unless the teaching continued. The lesson is practical: structure is a habit you build with regular practice, not a trick you learn once.

3.The Five Structures at a Glance

4.Structure 1: Sequence

What it does: arranges information in the order it happens or should happen. It comes in two forms: a timeline (events in history) and steps (how to do something).

Signal words: first then next after before meanwhile finally by 1990 in the morning. Dates and times are signals too.

Example

"Before sunrise, the baker mixes the dough. After an hour of rising, it is shaped into loaves. Finally, the loaves bake for thirty minutes."

Where the main idea sits: usually in the process as a whole ("how bread is made"), not in a single step. Useful organiser: a vertical flow chart or timeline. Typical questions: "What happens immediately after…?" "Which step comes before…?"

5.Structure 2: Cause and Effect

What it does: explains why something happened (the cause) and what resulted (the effect). Patterns include one cause with several effects, several causes with one effect, and a chain where each effect becomes the next cause.

Signal words: because so as a result therefore consequently due to led to this meant that.

Example

"Heavy rain fell for three days. As a result, the river burst its banks, two bridges were damaged and the village was cut off."

One cause (heavy rain), three effects (flooding, damaged bridges, isolation).

A chain

Heavy rain → flooded river → damaged bridges → no deliveries → empty shelves. Each result causes the next.

Where the main idea sits: in the relationship, such as "why the village was cut off", not in a single cause or effect. Organiser: arrows or a cause-and-effect map. Typical questions: "Why did…?" "What was the result of…?"

Careful: two things happening one after the other does not mean the first caused the second. Look for the writer's actual claim of cause, such as because or led to.

6.Structure 3: Compare and Contrast

What it does: examines how two or more things are alike (compare) and different (contrast).

Signal words: similarly likewise both unlike whereas however on the other hand in contrast but.

Example

"Both frogs and toads are amphibians that begin life in water. However, frogs usually have smooth, moist skin, whereas toads have drier, bumpy skin."

There are two ways to organise a comparison, and spotting which one is in use helps you find information quickly.

Two ways to organise a comparison
Subject A and subject B, with points 1, 2 and 3.

Diagram 2. Block method (A, A, A, B, B, B) and point-by-point method (A, B, A, B, A, B).

Where the main idea sits: in the writer's overall conclusion about how the things relate ("frogs and toads are close relatives with a few clear differences"). Organiser: a Venn diagram or a two-column table. Typical questions: "How are X and Y alike?" "What is the main difference?"

7.Structure 4: Problem and Solution

What it does: sets out a problem, then offers one or more solutions. Fuller versions also explain the causes of the problem and judge how well each solution works.

Signal words: problem issue challenge solution to address this one answer is a possible fix researchers propose.

Example

"Many students arrive at school tired because they go to bed late. To address this, some schools have moved the start of the day by an hour, and others run workshops on sleep habits."

Where the main idea sits: either in the problem's importance or in the proposed solution. Ask which one the writer spends most effort on. Organiser: a two-box chart (problem, solutions) with a column for results. Typical questions: "What problem does the writer describe?" "Which solution is suggested?"

Careful: a passage can mention a problem without being problem and solution. If no solution is offered, it is probably description or cause and effect.

8.Structure 5: Description

What it does: builds a picture of a topic by giving its features, qualities or details. It has no strong time order and no argument. It is the most common structure in encyclopedias, textbooks and travel writing.

Signal words: for example for instance such as includes features characteristics in particular specifically. Descriptive writing also uses many adjectives and the senses (colour, size, sound).

Example

"The octopus has eight arms lined with suckers, a soft body without a skeleton and three hearts. It can change colour in a moment to blend with rocks and seaweed."

Where the main idea sits: in the overall impression the details build up ("the octopus is a remarkable, flexible animal"), not in any one detail. Organiser: a web with the topic in the centre and details around it. Typical questions: "Which detail describes…?" "What is the passage mostly about?"

9.How Structure Helps You Find the Main Idea

StructureLook for the main idea in…
SequenceThe whole process or period, not one step
Cause and effectThe overall link between causes and effects
Compare and contrastThe writer's overall conclusion about the two things
Problem and solutionThe problem's importance, or the proposed solution
DescriptionThe overall impression built by the details

These are tendencies, not laws. Always check the main idea against the whole passage. See Main Idea & Supporting Details.

10.When Signal Words Mislead

Signal words are the quickest clue, but the same word can do different jobs. Always read the whole sentence.

WordDifferent uses
sinceTime: "Since 2010, the town has grown." Cause: "Since it rained, we stayed in."
whileTime: "While I cooked, he read." Contrast: "I like tea, while he prefers coffee."
afterUsually time, but "after all" can mean "because".
soResult: "It was late, so we left." Or purpose: "We left so we could rest."
butContrast, but sometimes just a turn in the story.

Also remember that a passage may have no signal words at all. Then you must ask what relationship the writer is building between the ideas.

11.Passages That Mix Structures

Real articles often combine structures. A news report may use cause and effect to explain a crisis, then problem and solution to discuss fixes. When you are asked for the "main structure", pick the pattern that organises the whole passage, and treat the others as supporting patterns.

Paragraphs can have their own structure inside a different overall structure. A "problem and solution" article may have a paragraph of description (what the problem looks like) and one of sequence (how the solution works).

Stories have structure too. Fiction usually follows the pattern of a plot: a beginning that sets the scene, a rising problem, a high point, and an ending. See Story Structure. This guide focuses on factual writing, where the five structures above are most common.

The questions behind step 2: Is the writer showing what happens in order (sequence)? why it happens (cause and effect)? how two things differ (compare and contrast)? how to fix something (problem and solution)? Or simply what something is like (description)?

13.Worked Examples

Example A: Naming the structure of three passages

1"To make a paper aeroplane, fold the sheet in half lengthways. Next, fold the top corners to the centre. Finally, fold the wings down." Clues: next, finally, steps in order. Sequence.
2"Many ponds are shrinking. Drought dries them, and farmland drains them. One answer is to protect the land around them." Clues: problem stated (shrinking), causes given, one answer is. Problem and solution. (Causes appear inside, but the passage ends with a fix.)
3"A lake and a river are both bodies of fresh water, but a lake stays in one basin, whereas a river keeps flowing." Clues: both, but, whereas. Compare and contrast.

Try it yourself. Name the structure: "The market is held on the old harbour. Stalls sell fish, spices and woven baskets, and the air smells of salt and frying onions."

Sample answer: Description. There is no time order, no cause, no comparison and no problem. The details build a picture of the market.

Example B: Using structure to find the main idea and summarise

Passage: "Corals get most of their food, and their colour, from tiny algae that live inside them. When the sea becomes too warm, corals expel the algae and turn white, an event called bleaching. A bleached coral is not dead: if the water cools soon enough, the algae can return. If the heat lasts, however, the coral starves. Researchers are now working on ways to help reefs cope."

1Read it all. The topic is coral bleaching.
2What is the writer doing? Explaining what happens when seas warm, and what follows.
3Signal words: when, if, however, now working on ways. The passage is mostly cause and effect (warm water causes bleaching, which can cause starvation), with a contrast (recovery or death) and a hint of problem and solution at the end.
4Main idea: the link between warm seas and bleaching, with its possible outcomes, not any one detail.
5Summary using the structure: "When seas get too warm, corals lose their algae and bleach; they can recover if conditions improve quickly but may die if the heat continues, so researchers are looking for ways to help."

Try it yourself. Read a short news article and write one sentence that names its main structure and one signal word that told you.

15.Using Structure When You Write

Choose the structure that fits your purpose before you write.

  • Explaining how to do something: sequence.
  • Explaining why something happened: cause and effect.
  • Helping a reader choose between two options: compare and contrast.
  • Persuading a reader that a change is needed: problem and solution.
  • Introducing a topic or a place: description.

Then add signal words so the reader never has to guess. This is also how good paragraphs are built: a topic sentence, then details arranged in a clear pattern. See Transitions & Cohesion for the linking words that make a structure visible.

16.Common Myths

MythEvery passage has exactly one structure.
FactLonger passages often mix several. Pick the pattern that organises the whole.
MythSignal words always tell you the structure.
FactWords like since and while have more than one job, and some passages have no signal words.
MythIf a passage mentions a problem, it is problem and solution.
FactIt needs a solution too. A passage that only describes a problem may be description or cause and effect.
MythText structure only matters in exams.
FactIt helps you study, take notes, summarise and write clearly in every subject.

17.Key Terms

Text structure: the pattern a writer uses to organise ideas.
Signal word: a word or phrase that points to a structure, such as because.
Sequence: ideas arranged in time order or step order.
Cause and effect: a link between why something happened and what followed.
Compare and contrast: showing how things are alike and different.
Problem and solution: a difficulty followed by one or more fixes.
Description: details that build a picture of a topic.
Graphic organiser: a chart or diagram that shows a structure.
Text feature: a help on the page, such as a heading or caption.
Expository text: writing that explains or informs.

18.Why This Matters

Text structure is the skeleton under every factual passage. Once you can see it, dense writing feels less frightening, main ideas are easier to find, and your own writing is better organised. Continue with Main Idea & Supporting Details to practise finding the central point, and Making Inferences to read between the lines.

Key points to remember
  • Text structure is how the ideas are organised, not how the page looks.
  • The five main structures are sequence, cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution, and description.
  • Signal words help, but read the whole sentence, because words like since and while change meaning.
  • Longer passages often mix structures; name the one that shapes the whole.
  • Each structure points to where the main idea usually sits.
  • Use the five-step routine: read, ask, scan, test, use.
  • Choose a structure on purpose when you write.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give each pupil a short factual article and a blank graphic organiser for each of the five structures. Ask them to choose the organiser that fits the article, fill it in, and underline the signal words that guided them. Then swap with a partner and compare choices. The aim is the reasoning, not a single right answer.

Please note: researchers and curricula group text structures slightly differently (some add "question and answer" or "definition"), so follow the names your syllabus uses.
