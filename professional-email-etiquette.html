Professional Email Etiquette

Email sits between formal and informal — faster than a letter, but still judged. Learn the conventions that keep a professional email clear, polite, and taken seriously.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 13 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Letter & Email Writing

A professional email is read in seconds, not minutes. That makes clarity and courtesy matter even more than they do in a printed letter.

Email borrows its structure from formal letters: a greeting, a body and a sign-off. It drops the address block, and its tone sits between formal and informal, depending on who you write to and how well you know them. Getting that balance right, and keeping the message easy to scan, is the whole skill.

You will use email for school, for applications and later at work. This guide covers where email fits, the parts of an email, tone, courtesy and safety, with two worked examples, a checklist, a glossary and ten practice questions.

1.Where Email Fits

Email is faster than a letter, but the person reading it still judges you by it. It is the right choice for questions, requests, updates and applications, when you want a written record and the reader can reply in their own time. It is not the best choice for urgent matters, which may need a phone call, or for sensitive or emotional matters, which are often better discussed face to face.

In many exam writing tasks, a formal email is written like a formal letter without the address block: a subject line, a greeting, short paragraphs and a closing. Follow your syllabus if it asks for a particular layout. For letters themselves, see Formal Letter Writing and Informal Letter Writing.

2.The Anatomy of an Email

Every professional email has the same basic parts. Learn them once and you can write any email quickly.

The parts of a professional email
Numbers appear one after another. Match each number to the list below.
  1. Recipients. Put the main person in "To". Use "Cc" only for people who need to know.
  2. Subject line. Say clearly what the email is about.
  3. Greeting. Address the reader politely, by name if you can.
  4. Opening sentence. State your purpose straight away.
  5. Body. Give the details in short paragraphs, one topic per email.
  6. Sign-off and name. Close politely, then add your name (and class or role).
  7. Attachment. Mention it in the body and give the file a clear name.

3.Addresses and Fields: To, Cc and Bcc

Choose your recipients carefully, because everyone you add will see the conversation.

To, Cc and Bcc: who sees what
One email, three kinds of recipient.

Diagram 2. People on To and Cc can see each other. People on Bcc are hidden from the rest.

  • To is for the person who needs to act or reply.
  • Cc ("carbon copy") is for people who only need to know, such as a manager or a parent.
  • Bcc ("blind carbon copy") sends a hidden copy. Use it to protect people's email addresses when you write to a large group, so that nobody's address is shared.
  • Reply answers only the sender. Reply all answers everyone on the email. Use it only when your answer is useful to everyone.

Your email address is part of your first impression. For school or work, an address based on your name is clearer than a joke or a nickname. Before you send, check that the program has filled in the right address, because similar names can cause mistakes.

4.The Subject Line

A good subject line tells the reader what the email is about before they open it. Keep it short and specific, so that it can be read in full on a phone screen. Vague lines such as "Hi" or "Question" are easy to ignore.

Weak subject lineStrong subject line
HiRequest for extension: English essay
QuestionQuestion about the school science fair
UpdateProject timeline update: new date is 10 March
URGENT!!!Reply needed by Friday: trip permission form

5.Greetings and Sign-Offs

Match your greeting to the relationship. Skipping the greeting in a first email can seem abrupt.

SituationGreetingSign-off
A person you do not know, name unknownDear Sir or Madam, or Dear Admissions Team,Yours faithfully,
A person you do not know, name knownDear Mr Khan, or Dear Dr Ahmed,Yours sincerely, or Kind regards,
A teacher or colleague you knowDear Ms Qureshi, or Hello Ms Qureshi,Kind regards, or Best wishes,
A friend or close colleagueHi Sana,Best, or Thanks,

In traditional British letter conventions, "Yours faithfully" follows "Dear Sir or Madam", and "Yours sincerely" follows a named person. Email is more flexible, and "Kind regards" is safe in almost any professional email. If your syllabus or teacher asks for the traditional pairs, use them. In British style, titles such as Mr, Ms, Mrs and Dr are written without a full stop. If you do not know whether a woman prefers Ms, Mrs or Miss, "Ms" is a safe choice.

6.Opening and Body: Purpose First

Readers scan emails, so state your purpose in the first sentence or two. A simple structure works for most emails:

  1. Purpose: why you are writing.
  2. Details: the facts the reader needs, such as what, when and why.
  3. Next step: what you would like the reader to do, and by when.
Example

I am writing to confirm the details for next week's science club visit. Could you please let me know the meeting time and how many students can come? A reply by Friday would help me plan the transport.

Keep to one topic per email. If you must ask several things, use a short list, so the reader can answer point by point.

Example

Could you confirm the following before Friday?

• Meeting time and place
• Number of students
• Whether slides are needed

7.Tone and Formality

Tone is how your writing sounds to the reader. Formal does not mean stiff. It means polite, clear and respectful. Polite phrases such as "Could you please..." and "I would be grateful if..." soften a request. You can read more about this in Formal vs. Informal English. Here is the same request at three levels of formality.

One request, three levels of formality
The question is the same. The reader is different.
To a friend (informal)
Hi Sana, do you know when the science club meets? Thanks, Ali
To a teacher you know (semi-formal)
Dear Mr Khan, could you tell me when the science club meets, please? Thank you. Kind regards, Ali Raza
To an official you do not know (formal)
Dear Sir or Madam, I would like to join the school science club. Could you please let me know when and where the club meets? Yours faithfully, Ali Raza

A few simple habits keep the tone professional. Avoid slang, text-message spellings and ALL CAPS, which can look like shouting. Use exclamation marks and emojis sparingly, and not at all in a first formal email. Be careful with humour, because jokes can be misunderstood when the reader cannot hear your voice. Never write an email when you are angry. Wait, then reread it before you send it.

8.Attachments, Replies and Timing

  • Mention attachments in the body. Write "I have attached my essay draft", and check that the file is really there before you send.
  • Give files clear names. "essay-draft.docx" is better than "document1".
  • Keep files reasonably small. Large attachments can fail to send or fill the reader's inbox.
  • Reply in good time. Many schools and workplaces expect a reply within a day or two. If you need longer, send a short note saying when you will answer.
  • Do not send the same email repeatedly. If you have not had a reply after a reasonable time, send one polite reminder.

9.Privacy and Safety

Email is convenient, but it is not private in the way a spoken conversation is. A message can be forwarded, copied or stored, so write only what you would be comfortable with others reading.

  • Do not share passwords or private details. A genuine organisation will not ask for your password by email.
  • Be careful with unexpected messages. Warning signs of a scam, called phishing, include a request to act at once, a threat, an offer that seems too good to be true, or a link you did not expect. Do not click. Check with the real organisation using a number or website you already know.
  • Do not forward other people's emails without permission.
  • Use the right account. Keep school or work business on the account provided for it.
  • Ask an adult if you are unsure whether a message is safe.

10.Worked Examples

Example A: Rewriting a weak email

Weak email. Subject: help. "hi sir i cant do the essay on time because i was sick and also computer broken can u give me more time. thanks"

1Write a clear subject line. "Request for extension: English essay (Class 9B)".
2Add a polite greeting. "Dear Mr Rahman,".
3State the purpose first. "I am writing to ask whether I may have a short extension for the essay due on Friday."
4Give the reason briefly and honestly. Add what you have already done.
5Make a specific request. Suggest a new date, so the teacher can say yes or no easily.
6Close politely. Thank the reader, then add a sign-off, your name and your class.

Improved email.

Subject: Request for extension: English essay (Class 9B)Dear Mr Rahman,

I am writing to ask whether I may have a short extension for the English essay that is due on Friday.

I was unwell at the start of the week, and my computer stopped working on Wednesday, so I have not been able to finish the final draft. Could I please hand it in on Monday? I have already completed the plan and the first two paragraphs.

Thank you for considering my request.

Kind regards,
Amina Siddiqui
Class 9B

Try it yourself: rewrite this: "Subject: question. hey, when is the science fair?? tell me asap". Write it to a teacher called Ms Qureshi. Sample answer: Subject: Question about the date of the science fair. Dear Ms Qureshi, I would like to ask when the science fair will take place. Could you please let me know the date and time? Thank you. Kind regards, [your name and class].

Example B: A complete professional email

Here a working adult confirms a meeting. Every part from Diagram 1 is present.

Subject: Meeting request: marketing budget reviewDear Ms Whitfield,

I am writing to confirm the details for next week's client presentation.

Could you confirm the following before Friday?

• Meeting time and place
• Number of attendees
• Whether slides are needed

Please let me know if any of these details change closer to the date.

Best regards,
Daniel Osei

The subject line names the topic. The greeting fits a first professional contact. The opening sentence gives the purpose. The list makes three questions easy to answer, and "Best regards" matches the tone.

13.Common Myths About Email

MythA short email is rude.
FactA short, clear email respects the reader's time. Add a greeting and a "thank you" to keep it polite.
MythLonger emails sound more professional.
FactLong emails often hide the point. Professional means clear and complete, not long.
MythGreetings and sign-offs are optional.
FactIn a first or formal email, they show respect. In quick replies between close colleagues they can be shorter.
MythYou can always take an email back.
FactOnce sent, you usually cannot control where it goes next. Check before you send.

14.Key Terms

Subject line: the short title that tells the reader what the email is about.
Greeting (salutation): the opening words, such as "Dear Mr Khan,".
Body: the main part of the email.
Sign-off (closing): the polite ending, such as "Kind regards,".
Signature: your name and details at the end.
Cc: "carbon copy", a visible copy for people who need to know.
Bcc: "blind carbon copy", a hidden copy.
Reply all: a reply sent to everyone on the original email.
Attachment: a file sent with the email.
Tone: how the writing sounds to the reader.
Phishing: a scam email that tries to steal your information.

15.Why This Matters

Email is likely the format you will use most often in school and work, far more than letters. Applying the discipline of Formal Letter Writing while keeping messages short and easy to scan is what makes an email effective, as well as polite. These skills also help with Job Applications and Cover Letters and Complaint and Request Letters, and a final read with the advice in Common Errors in English will catch small mistakes.

Key points to remember
  • A clear subject line, a greeting, the purpose first, short paragraphs and a polite sign-off make a professional email.
  • Use To for the person who must act, Cc for people who need to know, and Bcc to protect addresses.
  • Match your tone and greeting to the reader. Formal means polite and clear, not stiff.
  • One topic per email, and ask for a specific next step.
  • Check attachments, names and recipients before you press send.
  • Be careful with private information, and watch for phishing.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students a badly written email and ask them to rewrite it, then swap with a partner who checks it against the pre-send checklist. Ask them to write the same request to a friend, a teacher and an official, and discuss what changed.
