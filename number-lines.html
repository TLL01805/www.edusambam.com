Number Lines: Placing Whole Numbers, Fractions, and Decimals

A number line is the map that ties every kind of number together. Whole numbers, fractions, decimals and negatives all have their own exact place on it.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|16 min read|Last updated: October 2026|Mathematics

Every number you will ever work with, whether whole, fraction, decimal or negative, has an exact address on the number line. Learning to place numbers correctly is one of the fastest ways to build real number sense. It also turns addition, subtraction, comparing and estimating into things you can see, not just calculate.

A number line is simply a straight line where every point represents a number, arranged in order from smallest to largest, left to right. Distance along the line always matches distance in value. That is what makes it such a powerful tool for comparing, estimating and understanding numbers, rather than only calculating with them.

Did You Know?

The English mathematician John Wallis described using a line to picture numbers, including negative numbers, in a book on algebra published in 1685. Later, René Descartes’ coordinate system (1637) made the idea of putting numbers on lines and grids central to mathematics. Today’s rulers, thermometers and fuel gauges are all number lines in disguise.

1.The Rules of a Number Line

A good number line follows four simple rules.

  • Equal spacing. Every gap between neighbouring ticks represents the same amount. Check this before you read or place anything.
  • Order. Values increase from left to right. The further right a number sits, the larger it is.
  • Zero is the centre of the system. Positive numbers sit to the right of zero and negative numbers to the left.
  • Arrows or ends. Arrows at the ends mean the line carries on for ever. A line with a marked start and end (like a ruler) shows only a section.

2.Placing Whole Numbers

Whole numbers sit at evenly spaced points along the line. Larger numbers are always further right, and smaller numbers further left. The key habit is checking the spacing between marked ticks before placing a number, because every gap must represent the same amount.

On a line marked from −5 to 5, the number 2 sits exactly two equal steps to the right of 0. It is not “somewhere past the middle”, but at a precise, countable position.

Reading a scale when not every tick is labelled

Real scales (rulers, measuring jugs, speedometers) often label only some ticks. To read them, work out what one small gap is worth.

Example 2

The line above runs from 0 to 20 with 4 equal gaps. Each gap is worth 20 ÷ 4 = 5. The marked point is at the 3rd tick, which is 3 × 5 = 15.

“The marked point is at the 3rd tick, so it must be 3”

Counting ticks gives the number of steps, not the value, unless each step is worth exactly 1.

The fix: first find the value of one gap: (end − start) ÷ number of gaps. Then multiply by the number of steps from the start. Here (20 − 0) ÷ 4 = 5, and 3 steps give 3 × 5 = 15.

3.Placing Negative Numbers

Negative numbers sit to the left of zero, mirroring the positive side. The further left a number sits, the smaller its value. So −5 is smaller than −1, even though 5 looks like the “bigger” digit.

“−5 is bigger than −1 because 5 is a bigger number”

This mixes up the size of the digit with the value of the number, a very common early mistake with negatives.

The fix: position on the number line decides size, not the digit itself. −1 sits to the right of −5, so −1 is the larger number. Picture temperature: −1 °C is warmer than −5 °C.

Example 3: Ordering

Put −7, 3, −2, 0, 5 and −1 in order from smallest to largest. Place them on the line from left to right: −7, −2, −1, 0, 3, 5. Notice that among negatives, the one with the larger digit is further left, so it is smaller.

Distance from zero. The distance of a number from 0 is called its absolute value, written |x|. So |−3| = 3 and |3| = 3. They are on opposite sides of zero but the same distance from it.

4.Adding and Subtracting on the Number Line

The number line turns addition and subtraction into movement. Adding means moving right, and subtracting means moving left. Start at the first number, then jump by the second.

The jumps draw themselves one after another. Right for adding, left for subtracting.
Example 4

(a) −2 + 5: start at −2, jump 5 right, land on 3.

(b) 3 − 7: start at 3, jump 7 left, land on −4.

(c) −3 − 2: start at −3, jump 2 left, land on −5.

(d) 4 − (−3): subtracting a negative moves in the opposite direction, so jump 3 right and land on 7. The two minus signs act like a plus.

For many more of these, see Addition & Subtraction on the Number Line and Operations of Integers.

Distance between two numbers

The distance between two points on the line is the larger number minus the smaller number. It is never negative.

Example 5

How far is −2 from 6? Larger minus smaller: 6 − (−2) = 6 + 2 = 8. A temperature that rises from −3 °C to 4 °C goes up 4 − (−3) = 7 degrees.

5.Placing Fractions: Equal Partitioning

To place a fraction, first split the space between two whole numbers into the number of equal parts shown by the denominator, then count across by the numerator. The denominator decides how many pieces, and the numerator decides how many of them to count.

Placing 3/4 about three-quarters of the way to the number 4 (instead of to 1)

A common slip is aiming for the whole number that matches the numerator, rather than partitioning towards the next whole number.

The fix: split the space between 0 and 1 into 4 equal parts (since the denominator is 4), then count 3 of them across. 3/4 sits close to 1, not anywhere near the number 4.

Fractions greater than one

A fraction does not have to stay between 0 and 1. The same partitioning method just repeats between the next pair of whole numbers. An improper fraction such as 7/4 is the same point as the mixed number 1¾.

Where is 5/3? Since 5 ÷ 3 = 1 remainder 2, it is 1⅔. Split the space between 1 and 2 into three equal parts and count two across. It lands two-thirds of the way from 1 to 2.

6.Placing Decimals

Decimals work exactly like fractions with a denominator of 10, 100 or 1,000. The number line between 0 and 1 splits into 10 equal tenths, and each decimal digit finds its place among them.

Example 7

0.6 sits six-tenths of the way between 0 and 1, the same position as the fraction 6/10, or its simplified form 3/5.

Zooming in: hundredths and beyond

To place 0.47, first find the tenth it lives in: it is between 0.4 and 0.5. Then “zoom in” on that gap and split it into 10 more equal parts (hundredths). The point is the 7th mark after 0.4.

The gold block marks the part being magnified. Between any two numbers you can always zoom in again.
“0.45 is bigger than 0.5 because 45 is bigger than 5”

This reads the digits after the point as a whole number, ignoring their place values.

The fix: line up the place values: 0.50 and 0.45. Five tenths is more than four tenths, so 0.5 > 0.45. On the number line, 0.5 sits to the right of 0.45. Adding a zero to 0.5 to make 0.50 does not change its value.

7.One Point, Three Names

A single point on the number line can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage. These are three names for the same position. This is why you can compare a fraction with a decimal: both are just places on one line.

8.Comparing and Ordering Numbers

Whichever number sits further right is always the larger value. This holds for whole numbers, fractions, decimals and negatives alike, and it is often faster than comparing digits directly.

1/5
2/5
3/5
4/5
1
2/5
3/5
“1/3 is bigger than 1/2 because 3 is bigger than 2”

Comparing the denominators as if they were the sizes ignores what the denominator means.

The fix: the denominator says how many equal pieces the whole is cut into, so a bigger denominator means smaller pieces. One third of a pizza is smaller than one half. On the number line, 1/3 ≈ 0.33 sits to the left of 1/2 = 0.5. When denominators match (like 3/5 and 2/5), compare the numerators: 3 > 2, so 3/5 is larger.

Example 8: Mixed Types

(a) Order 3/8, 0.4, 1/2 and 0.45 from smallest to largest. Change to decimals: 3/8 = 0.375, 0.4, 0.5 and 0.45. In order: 0.375 < 0.4 < 0.45 < 0.5, so 3/8, 0.4, 0.45, 1/2.

(b) Order −1/2, −0.75, 0.2 and 1/4. Negatives are to the left. −0.75 is further left than −0.5, so the order is −0.75, −1/2, 0.2, 1/4.

9.Estimating and Finding Numbers In Between

Not every number lines up neatly on a marked tick. Estimating position between two known “benchmark” numbers, like 0, 1/2 and 1, builds a fast, practical sense of roughly where any number belongs.

Example 9

Where does 0.42 belong? It is a little less than halfway between 0 and 1 (which would be 0.5), so it sits just to the left of the midpoint.

The midpoint between two numbers is their average: add them and divide by 2. It is also a way to find a number in between.

Example 10

(a) The midpoint of −3 and 5 is (−3 + 5) ÷ 2 = 2 ÷ 2 = 1.

(b) A number between 0.2 and 0.5: the midpoint is (0.2 + 0.5) ÷ 2 = 0.35.

(c) A fraction between 1/4 and 1/2: change to eighths, 2/8 and 4/8. The one between is 3/8.

Between any two different numbers, however close, there are always more numbers. You can keep zooming in for ever. That is a key idea behind the real number line (see Number Systems).

Real-World Example

A thermometer is a vertical number line. If the temperature is −2 °C in the morning and rises to 6 °C by afternoon, the distance travelled along that “line” is 6 − (−2) = 8 degrees. It is found the same way as measuring the distance between any two points on a number line, whether whole, negative or otherwise.

Example 11: Sea Level and Basements

Sea level is 0. A diver at −12 m swims up to −5 m, which is a rise of −5 − (−12) = 7 m. In a building, the ground floor is 0 and a basement is −1. Going from floor 4 down to basement −2 is a drop of 4 − (−2) = 6 floors.

Example 12: A Bank Balance

An account is overdrawn by Rs 500, which is a balance of −500. A deposit of Rs 800 moves the balance right: −500 + 800 = Rs 300. The number line shows why: the deposit first brings the balance up to zero (500 steps), then 300 more into the positive.

11.Common Mistakes

  • Not checking the scale. Count the gaps and find what one gap is worth before reading or placing a number.
  • Counting ticks instead of value. Three ticks along a line in steps of 5 means 15, not 3.
  • Thinking −5 > −1. Further left means smaller.
  • Placing 3/4 near the number 4. Partition the space between 0 and 1.
  • Thinking a bigger denominator means a bigger fraction. 1/3 < 1/2.
  • Reading decimals like whole numbers. 0.45 < 0.5.
  • Jumping the wrong way when subtracting a negative. 4 − (−3) moves right, to 7.

12.Try It Yourself

  1. Put −4, 2, −1/2, 0.3 and −2.5 in order from smallest to largest.
  2. A number line runs from 0 to 30 with 6 equal gaps. What is each gap worth, and what number is at the 4th tick?
  3. What is 2 − (−5), and what is the distance between −1.5 and 3?
Show answers

1. Negatives first, most negative on the left: −4, −2.5, −1/2, then 0.3 and 2. The order is −4, −2.5, −1/2, 0.3, 2.

2. Each gap is 30 ÷ 6 = 5. The 4th tick is 4 × 5 = 20.

3. 2 − (−5) = 2 + 5 = 7. The distance between −1.5 and 3 is 3 − (−1.5) = 4.5.

13.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] Write these in order, smallest first: 0.6, 1/2, 0.45, 3/5.
  2. [2 marks] The temperature in a freezer is −18 °C and in a fridge is 4 °C. How many degrees warmer is the fridge?
  3. [3 marks] Find the number that is halfway between −7 and 3.
  4. [3 marks] On a number line from 0 to 2, a point is placed at 5/4. Between which two whole numbers does it lie, and write it as a mixed number and as a decimal.
Show answers

1. Change to decimals: 0.6, 1/2 = 0.5, 0.45 and 3/5 = 0.6. In order: 0.45, 1/2, then 0.6 and 3/5 (these two are equal).

2. 4 − (−18) = 22, so the fridge is 22 degrees warmer.

3. (−7 + 3) ÷ 2 = −4 ÷ 2 = −2.

4. 5/4 lies between 1 and 2. As a mixed number it is 1¼, and as a decimal it is 1.25.

14.Key Points

  • A number line is ordered and equally spaced. Values increase from left to right.
  • To read a scale, find what one gap is worth, then count the gaps.
  • Further left means smaller, so −5 < −1.
  • To place a fraction, split the space by the denominator and count the numerator. For decimals, split into tenths, then hundredths.
  • Add by moving right and subtract by moving left. Subtracting a negative moves right.
  • A fraction, a decimal and a percentage can all name the same point.

15.For Teachers and Parents

Draw a number line on the floor with tape and let learners stand on a number. Ask the class to place a fraction or decimal and justify it. Use a thermometer or a ruler to link the line to real objects. Always begin by asking “what is one gap worth?”, because this single question prevents most misreadings. Move on to jumping for addition and subtraction only after learners are comfortable placing numbers. Keep the line in view whenever negatives, fractions or decimals appear, since the picture usually clears up the confusion.

16.Why This Topic Matters

The number line is the bridge between arithmetic and algebra. Graphs, coordinates, inequalities and functions are all built from number lines. The habits you build here (equal spacing, careful reading of a scale, and checking that an answer is in a sensible place) will keep helping you right through school mathematics.
