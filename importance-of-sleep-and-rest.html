The Importance of Sleep and Rest

You spend roughly a third of your life asleep — and that time is not wasted. Sleep is one of the most active repair processes your body performs.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 14 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Health

Many people treat sleep as optional, something to give up for homework, screen time or friends. But sleep is not empty time. It is an active process in which the brain organises memories, the body repairs itself and the systems that control mood and energy reset for the next day. This guide explains what happens during sleep, how much you need, why teenagers often feel sleepy at the wrong time, and which habits make sleep better.

1.What Happens While You Sleep

Sleep is not one long, even state. During the night you pass through repeating sleep cycles, and each cycle lasts roughly 90 minutes. A cycle includes lighter sleep, deep sleep and REM sleep. REM stands for rapid eye movement, because the eyes move quickly under the closed eyelids during this stage.

Sleep stageWhat happens
Light sleepThe body begins to relax. Heart rate and breathing slow down. It is easy to be woken.
Deep sleepTissue repair, muscle growth and immune strengthening mainly occur. Growth hormone is released mostly in this stage.
REM sleepThe brain processes memories and emotions. Most vivid dreaming occurs here.
One night of sleep, in stages
A simplified picture. Real nights vary from person to person.

Notice that deep sleep (the lowest level) is more common in the first half of the night, while REM sleep gets longer towards morning. This is why cutting a night short, for example by waking very early, takes away a lot of REM sleep.

A full night of sleep passes through these stages several times. This is why both the length and the quality of sleep matter.

2.How Much Sleep Do You Need?

The amount of sleep a person needs changes with age. The ranges below come from general guidance by sleep medicine organisations such as the American Academy of Sleep Medicine. For young children the figures include daytime naps. Individual needs vary a little, so use these as a guide, not an exact rule.

3.Why Sleep Matters So Much

  • Memory and learning. Sleep helps the brain store and organise what you learned during the day, turning short-term memories into longer-lasting ones.
  • Physical growth. Growth hormone is released mainly during deep sleep, which is especially important for children and teenagers.
  • Immune function. Regular, sufficient sleep is linked to a stronger defence against illness.
  • Mood. A lack of sleep is strongly linked to irritability, anxiety and low mood.
  • Concentration and safety. Even one night of poor sleep can reduce focus and reaction time, and tired people make more mistakes.
Example

A student who stays up late revising the night before an exam often performs worse than one who reviews earlier and sleeps well, because the brain needs sleep to consolidate what was studied into long-term memory.

4.Too Little Sleep: Short-Term and Long-Term Effects

Missing sleep once in a while is not dangerous, but it has clear effects. After a short night, most people notice slower reaction times, difficulty concentrating, poorer memory and a shorter temper. Tired people also make more mistakes, which matters when crossing a road, cycling or doing practical work in a laboratory. Lack of sleep can also affect appetite and make high-sugar foods more tempting.

When a person regularly sleeps less than they need, the effects add up. Large studies link long-term short sleep with a higher chance of problems such as low mood, anxiety, weight gain, type 2 diabetes and heart disease. These are links found in groups of people, not a certainty for any one person, but they are a strong reason to treat sleep as part of health, like food and exercise.

5.Sleep and Exam Success

Many students try to cram the night before a test. Learning research suggests a better plan: study in short sessions over several days, then protect your sleep so the brain can store what you learned. On the night before an exam, a calm review and a normal bedtime usually help more than staying up. Sleep also helps you stay calm and think clearly during the exam itself.

6.The Teenage Body Clock

Your body has an internal 24-hour clock called the circadian rhythm. It tells you when to feel alert and when to feel sleepy, partly by controlling a hormone called melatonin, which the body releases as it gets dark and which signals that it is time to sleep.

During the teenage years this clock tends to shift later. Many teenagers naturally feel sleepy later at night and find it harder to wake early. This is a normal biological change, not laziness. It also means that an early school start can cut into the sleep a teenager needs.

Real-World Example

Research links school start times that clash with teenagers' natural sleep rhythms to lower academic performance. Some schools that moved to later start times reported improvements in student attendance, mood and grades. Results vary between schools, but they show how directly sleep affects everyday life, not just health in the abstract.

7.What Disrupts Sleep

  • Screen time before bed. Bright light, especially the blue-rich light from phones and tablets, can delay the release of melatonin. Exciting content can also keep the mind alert.
  • Caffeine. Found in tea, coffee, some fizzy drinks and energy drinks, caffeine can stay in the body for hours, so an afternoon or evening drink can delay sleep.
  • Irregular sleep schedules. Going to bed and waking at very different times confuses the body clock.
  • Stress and overstimulation. An overactive mind before bed makes falling asleep harder.
  • A bright, hot or noisy room. Light, noise and heat all make it harder to sleep deeply.

8.Worked Example: Plan Your Bedtime

What time should a 15-year-old go to bed?

Suppose a student must wake at 7:00 am. Teenagers need 8 to 10 hours, so let us aim for 9 hours. Falling asleep usually takes about 15 minutes.

1Wake-up time: 7:00 am.
2Subtract 9 hours of sleep: 7:00 am minus 9 hours = 10:00 pm. This is the time to be asleep.
3Allow 15 minutes to fall asleep: 10:00 pm minus 15 minutes = 9:45 pm.

So the student should be in bed with the screen away by about 9:45 pm. Nine hours is also six sleep cycles of about 90 minutes each (6 × 90 = 540 minutes = 9 hours), which fits the cycle pattern well.

Try it yourself: a student wakes at 6:30 am and wants 8.5 hours of sleep. What bedtime is needed, if falling asleep takes 15 minutes? Answer: 6:30 am minus 8.5 hours = 10:00 pm asleep, so in bed by 9:45 pm.

9.Habits for Better Sleep

  • Keep a regular schedule, with similar bed and wake-up times, even at weekends.
  • Switch off screens about an hour before bed, and charge your phone outside the bedroom if you can.
  • Avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening.
  • Keep the bedroom dark, quiet and comfortably cool.
  • Get daylight and some physical activity during the day. Avoid very hard exercise right before bed.
  • Do not eat a large meal just before bedtime.
  • Build a short, calm routine, such as reading a book, a warm shower or gentle stretching.
  • If worries keep you awake, write them down earlier in the evening so your mind can let go.

10.Naps and Weekend Catch-Up

A short nap of about 20 to 30 minutes in the early afternoon can improve alertness without spoiling night sleep. Long naps late in the day can make it harder to sleep at night. A little extra sleep at the weekend can help you recover, but sleeping in for many hours confuses the body clock and makes Monday morning harder. Regular sleep all week is better.

11.Common Myths About Sleep

MythSleep is wasted time.
FactSleep is when the brain organises memories and the body repairs itself.
MythYou can train yourself to need much less sleep.
FactYou can get used to feeling tired, but your performance and health still suffer.
MythTeenagers who sleep late are just lazy.
FactThe teenage body clock shifts later, so late sleepiness is partly biological.
MythCoffee or an energy drink can replace sleep.
FactCaffeine may hide tiredness for a while, but it does not repair the body or help memory, and it can disturb the next night's sleep.

12.When to Ask for Help

Everyone has a bad night sometimes. But if someone has trouble sleeping for weeks, feels very sleepy during the day, snores loudly with pauses in breathing, or if low mood or worry is affecting their sleep, they should talk to a parent, a school counsellor or a doctor. Sleep problems are common, and many can be helped.

Key points to remember
  • Sleep is an active process that supports memory, growth, immunity and mood.
  • A night's sleep has repeating cycles of light, deep and REM sleep.
  • Teenagers need about 8 to 10 hours, school-age children 9 to 12, and adults 7 to 9.
  • Screens, caffeine, irregular schedules and stress make sleep worse.
  • A regular routine and a dark, quiet, cool room help sleep.
For teachers and parents

Activity: ask students to keep a sleep diary for one week, writing their bedtime, wake-up time and how alert they felt. Then compare it with the recommended range and discuss one habit to change.

Please note: this article is general education. It is not medical advice and does not replace a doctor. Anyone with ongoing sleep problems should speak to a qualified health professional.
