Common Errors in English: A Complete Guide

Their, there, and they're. Affect and effect. Fewer and less. Here are the mistakes that trip up even confident writers — and exactly how to fix them for good.

Most English errors are not random. They repeat across millions of writers because a small number of words and rules are genuinely confusing. Learn these, and your writing instantly becomes sharper.

Across the first four topics in this series — parts of speech, sentence structure, punctuation, and tenses — a handful of mistakes come up again and again. This guide collects them in one place: the words that sound alike but mean different things, the rules that get misapplied, and the small errors that quietly undermine otherwise strong writing.

1.Their / There / They're

Three words that sound identical but serve completely different jobs.

Examples

Their (possession): "They left their bags at home." There (place/existence): "The keys are over there." / "There is a problem." They're (contraction of "they are"): "They're arriving at noon."

2.Your / You're

Examples

Your (possession): "Is this your book?" You're (contraction of "you are"): "You're going to love this."

3.Its / It's

Already covered in punctuation, but common enough to repeat here: "its" shows possession (no apostrophe); "it's" is short for "it is" or "it has."

Example

"The dog wagged its tail because it's finally time for dinner."

4.Affect / Effect

The most common word-confusion error in English writing.

Examples

Affect (usually a verb, meaning "to influence"): "The weather will affect our plans." Effect (usually a noun, meaning "a result"): "The effect of the storm was severe flooding."

A useful trick: Affect is an Action (verb); Effect is an End result (noun).

5.Then / Than

Examples

Then (time/sequence): "We ate dinner, then watched a movie." Than (comparison): "She is taller than her brother."

6.Fewer / Less

Fewer is used with countable nouns (things you can count individually). Less is used with uncountable nouns (things measured, not counted).

Examples

Fewer people, fewer chairs, fewer mistakes. Less water, less time, less information.

7.Who / Whom

Who is a subject pronoun (does the action). Whom is an object pronoun (receives the action).

Trick

If you can replace it with "he/she," use who. If you can replace it with "him/her," use whom. "Who called you?" (He called.) / "To whom should I address this?" (Address it to him.)

8.I / Me

I is a subject pronoun; me is an object pronoun. The confusion mostly happens in compound subjects/objects.

Examples

Incorrect: "Me and John went to the store." Correct: "John and I went to the store." Incorrect: "She gave the gift to John and I." Correct: "She gave the gift to John and me."

A quick test: remove the other person from the sentence. You would never say "Me went to the store" or "She gave the gift to I" — so the correct pronoun becomes obvious.

9.Subject-Verb Agreement

Singular subjects take singular verbs; plural subjects take plural verbs — but a few situations regularly cause mistakes.

Examples

Incorrect: "The list of items are on the table." Correct: "The list of items is on the table." (The subject is "list," not "items.")

Incorrect: "Neither of the answers are correct." Correct: "Neither of the answers is correct." ("Neither" is singular.)

10.Dangling Modifiers

A modifier that does not clearly and logically attach to the word it is meant to describe.

Example

Incorrect: "Walking to the store, the rain started falling." (This implies the rain was walking.) Correct: "Walking to the store, I got caught in the rain."

11.Double Negatives

Standard English uses only one negative per idea. Two negatives cancel each other out or are simply considered incorrect.

Example

Incorrect: "I don't know nothing about it." Correct: "I don't know anything about it," or "I know nothing about it."

12.Misplaced Apostrophes in Plurals

Apostrophes are never used simply to make a word plural.

Example

Incorrect: "We sell apple's and banana's." Correct: "We sell apples and bananas."

13.Run-Ons, Fragments, and Comma Splices

Already covered in sentence structure, but worth a final summary since they remain the most frequent structural errors:

  • Fragment: incomplete thought. "Because it was raining." (needs an independent clause attached)
  • Run-on: two independent clauses with no punctuation or connector. "It was raining we stayed inside."
  • Comma splice: two independent clauses joined by only a comma. "It was raining, we stayed inside."

Real-World Example

Consider this line from a hastily written email: "Their going to affect the schedule, so their's less time then we thought." Corrected, it should read: "They're going to affect the schedule, so there's less time than we thought." Four separate common errors in a single sentence — "their" for "they're," "affect" used correctly but easily confused, "their's" instead of "there's," and "then" instead of "than" — show exactly why these small words deserve careful attention even in quick, informal writing.

Why This Matters

These errors are so common precisely because English has so many words that sound alike but function differently. Mastering parts of speech (Topic I), sentence structure (Topic II), punctuation (Topic III), and tenses (Topic IV) builds the foundation — but it is avoiding these small, easily-fixed mistakes (Topic V) that separates writing that looks careless from writing that looks genuinely polished.
