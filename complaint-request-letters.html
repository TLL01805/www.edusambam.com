Complaint & Request Letters

The letters people are most tempted to write angry — and the ones where staying calm gets the best results. Learn how to ask for what you need without burning the relationship.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 19 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Letter & Email Writing

A complaint letter is not a place to vent. It is a tool with one job: to get something fixed. The calmer, clearer and more specific your letter is, the harder it is to ignore and the easier it is for the reader to say yes.

This guide covers two closely related kinds of writing. A complaint letter says that something went wrong and asks for it to be put right. A request letter asks for something: information, permission, a favour, a change of date. Both apply the formal letter layout from Formal Letter Writing and the polite, clear tone of Professional Email Etiquette, to situations with real consequences.

You will learn how to plan the letter, state the facts, explain the impact, make a specific request with a fair deadline, control your tone, keep evidence, follow up, and escalate calmly. It includes two worked examples, a school leave application, a checklist, common mistakes and ten practice questions.

1.Know Your Goal and Your Reader

Before you write, answer two questions on paper:

  1. What do I want to happen? A refund, a repair, a replacement, an apology, an explanation, a change of date? Write it in one sentence. If you cannot, the letter will be vague.
  2. Who can actually do it? A letter addressed to "To whom it may concern" may travel around for weeks. If you can, find the name or job title of the person responsible for complaints by phoning, checking the website or reading your receipt. Consumer advice bodies suggest addressing the person responsible for your type of complaint, or, if you cannot find one, a senior person such as the head of the organisation.

Remember also that the person who reads your letter is usually not the person who caused the problem. A calm letter treats the reader as someone who can help, not someone to attack.

Letter, email or phone call? A quick phone call can sometimes solve a simple problem, and consumer guidance suggests calling first to learn the complaint procedure or the right contact. But when the problem is complicated or you want a record, put it in writing. A letter or email gives you proof of what you said and when you said it.

2.The Five-Part Plan

Almost every successful complaint or request letter follows the same shape. The plan keeps the letter short, and it makes sure you do not forget anything important.

One short paragraph each, in this order.

3.Part 2: State the Facts

Describe exactly what happened, in order, using dates, names, order or reference numbers, and amounts. Facts are harder to dismiss than feelings, because a reader can check them. Leave out guesses about the other person's motives.

Feeling or opinion (weak)Fact (strong)
This is the worst service I have ever had.I ordered item 4521 on 2 October and it arrived on 9 October with a cracked casing.
Your staff are rude and useless.When I phoned on 10 October, I was transferred three times and was not given a reference number until the fourth call.
You always overcharge people.My bill shows a charge of 40 for a service I cancelled on 1 September.
You obviously ignored my message.I emailed on 12 September and have not received a reply.

Notice that the strong versions are not weaker; they are more powerful. A reader who sees the dates cannot say "that did not happen" without checking, and checking is exactly what you want them to do.

Evidence. Collect proof before you write: receipts, order confirmations, photographs, earlier emails, the names of people you spoke to. Consumer advice organisations recommend that you send copies, not originals, and keep the originals yourself. Mention each enclosure in the letter. They also advise that you do not send copies of bank or credit card statements, because of the risk of identity theft.

A timeline you can quote
Write your own timeline before you write the letter.

Diagram 2. A timeline keeps the letter accurate, and it is useful again if you have to escalate.

4.Part 3: Explain the Impact, Briefly

In one or two sentences, say what the problem has cost you: time, money, safety, missed work or schoolwork. This tells the reader why the matter deserves attention. Do not exaggerate. A claim that cannot be believed weakens the whole letter.

5.Part 4: Make a Specific Request with a Fair Deadline

The most common weakness in complaint letters is a vague ending ("Please do something about this"). The reader cannot act on a feeling. Say clearly:

  • What you want (a refund, a replacement, a repair, a written explanation, an apology, a corrected bill).
  • How you want it done, if that matters (refund to the original payment method, repair at your address).
  • By when. Give a clear date, or a clear period such as "within 14 days of this letter". A deadline gives the reader a target and gives you a reason to follow up.
VagueSpecific
Please do something about this.I would like a full refund to my original payment method within 14 days of the date of this letter.
This needs to be fixed.Could you confirm a repair date within the next seven days?
I want an explanation.Please explain in writing why the charge was made and confirm whether it will be removed.
Reply as soon as possible.I would be grateful for a reply by 27 October.

Be reasonable. Ask for something the reader can realistically provide, within a time that is realistic for their size and process. If you are happy with more than one outcome, state your preferred one first and say what else you would accept ("I would prefer a refund, but I would accept a replacement if it is sent within 14 days"). Also ask to be told how to return the faulty item if that is needed.

Legal rights. Many countries give consumers legal rights when goods or services are faulty, and these rules differ from place to place. If you are sure of a rule that applies to you, you may mention it politely and accurately. If you are not sure, do not guess. Describe the problem and ask for a fair solution, or ask the organisation to explain its complaints procedure. Never threaten actions you do not intend to take.

6.Tone: Firm, Not Furious

A calm letter signals that you are reasonable and serious. It gives the reader nothing to be defensive about and no excuse to dismiss you. Look at four versions of the same complaint:

Too angry
Your useless company has ruined my week and I demand an apology immediately!
Easy to dismiss. It insults and gives no facts.
Too weak
Sorry to bother you, but maybe someone could look at this if it is not too much trouble.
Polite, but it asks for nothing and apologises for complaining.
Better
I am unhappy with the service I received and would like the matter resolved.
Calm, but still vague.
Firm and polite
The keyboard arrived damaged on 9 October. I would be grateful if you could refund me in full by 27 October.
Factual, specific, courteous and clear about the deadline.

Useful firm-but-polite phrases include: I would be grateful if…, I would like to ask you to…, I am writing to draw your attention to…, I understand that mistakes happen, but…, I would appreciate a written reply by…. Avoid insults, sarcasm, capital letters, repeated exclamation marks and threats you cannot support.

Stay truthful. Only state what you can prove. Exaggeration and false statements can damage your complaint, and in some cases can have legal consequences if you repeat them publicly.

7.After You Send It: Records, Follow-Up and Escalation

  • Keep a copy of the letter or email, and a note of the date you sent it. Consumer guidance also suggests keeping a dated diary of every stage, including phone calls and the names of the people you spoke to.
  • Prove you sent it if it matters. For paper letters, you can ask for proof of posting or use a "recorded" or "signed for" service. For email, keep the sent message and any acknowledgement.
  • Ask for acknowledgement and for a copy of the complaints procedure, which many organisations have.
  • Follow up. If the deadline passes with no reply, send a short, polite second letter that refers to the first and gives a new, shorter deadline.
Escalating calmly
Move up one step at a time, and keep your tone the same.

Diagram 3. Complaint systems differ by country and organisation. Check the procedure for yours.

One advice charity suggests marking each letter clearly, such as "Complaint: Stage 1", so that the reader knows where the matter stands. Whatever the system, keep each letter short and mention your earlier letters, dates and reference numbers.

8.Request Letters

A request letter asks for something that the reader is free to say yes or no to. Because you are asking a favour, courtesy and ease matter even more. The shape is simple:

  1. Say what you are asking, in the first paragraph. Do not make the reader hunt for it.
  2. Give a brief, honest reason. One or two sentences is enough.
  3. Make it easy to say yes. Offer alternatives, supply any form or document, and say how long it will take.
  4. Close politely with a thank-you and a clear next step.
Kind of requestExample opening
InformationI am writing to ask whether you offer evening classes in beginners' Arabic, and if so, when the next course starts.
PermissionI am writing to request permission to reproduce a chart from your 2024 report in an academic publication. Full credit will be given to the original source.
ExtensionI am writing to ask whether I could submit my project on Friday instead of Wednesday.
AppointmentI would like to request a meeting of about fifteen minutes to discuss my course options.
Leave of absenceI am writing to request leave for two days, from 4 to 5 November, because of a family wedding.

A small style note. Some readers find "Thank you in advance" presumptuous, because it seems to assume the answer. "Thank you for considering my request" is a safer choice.

9.School Applications and Letters to Principals

In many schools, students are asked to write an application for leave or another request to the principal or class teacher. It follows the same plan as any request letter. Keep it short, state the request first, and give dates and a reason. Greetings and sign-offs vary between schools and countries: a formal "Dear Sir or Madam" with "Yours faithfully" is widely accepted, and some textbooks use "Respected Sir" with "Yours obediently". Follow the style your teacher or syllabus uses.

Class 9-B, Greenfield School
20 October 2026

The Principal
Greenfield School

Dear Sir or Madam,

Application for leave, 4 to 5 November

I am writing to request leave from school on 4 and 5 November 2026, because I must attend a family wedding out of town. I will return on 6 November. I will ask my classmates for the notes and homework I miss and complete them on my return.

Thank you for considering my request.

Yours faithfully,

Amna Rahman

10.Email or Paper?

For many complaints and requests, an email is accepted, but the same rules apply. Write a clear subject line (Complaint: damaged keyboard, order 4521), put the key facts in the body, attach copies of evidence, and keep the sent message. Reply to the right address (a general "info" mailbox may be slow), and copy in only the people who need to see it. See Professional Email Etiquette.

11.Worked Examples

Example A: A complete complaint letter

The situation (invented for teaching): Omar ordered a wireless keyboard online. It arrived with a cracked casing. He took photographs and phoned customer service, who asked him to write.

1Goal. A full refund (or a replacement within 14 days).
2Reader. The Customer Service Manager.
3Facts and evidence. Order date, delivery date, order number, call reference, two photographs, order confirmation.
4Write using the five-part plan, then check the tone.

27 Birchwood Drive
Eastfield
13 October 2026

The Customer Service Manager
Brightline Electronics
500 Commerce Way
Northbridge

Dear Sir or Madam,

Complaint: damaged wireless keyboard, order 4521

On 2 October 2026 I ordered a wireless keyboard (order number 4521) from your website. It was delivered on 9 October with a cracked casing, and several keys do not register when pressed. I photographed the damage on 10 October and phoned your customer service line the same day (reference 88213), where I was asked to put my complaint in writing.

I use the keyboard for my schoolwork, so I have had to borrow one from a family member until the matter is resolved.

I would like a full refund to my original payment method. I would also accept a replacement, provided it is sent within 14 days of the date of this letter. Please tell me how to return the damaged keyboard.

I enclose copies of my order confirmation and two photographs. I would be grateful if you would acknowledge this letter and reply by 27 October 2026. I can be reached at the address above or at omar.farouk@example.com.

Yours faithfully,

Omar Farouk

What makes it work. A subject line with the reference number; dates and numbers instead of adjectives; a one-sentence impact; a specific request with a fallback and a deadline; enclosures named in the letter; and a calm, courteous tone from start to finish. It is also short enough to fit on one page.

Example B: A request email, and repairing an angry one

Request email. A student wants a short extension.

Subject: Request for a short extension: history project

Dear Mr Hasan,

I am writing to ask whether I could submit my history project on Friday 30 October instead of Wednesday 28 October. I have finished the research and two of the four sections, but I was absent for two days last week and need extra time to write the conclusion carefully. I can send you my draft so far today if that would help.

Thank you for considering my request.

Kind regards,
Amna Rahman

Why it works. The request comes first, with exact dates. The reason is short and honest. It shows progress, offers the draft, and makes it easy to say yes.

Angry email to repair: "This is ridiculous. You lost my parcel AGAIN and your staff just don't care. I want my money back NOW or I'm telling everyone."

1Remove the insults and capitals, and the threat.
2Add the facts. What was ordered, when, what the tracking said.
3State the request and deadline.
4Keep it short and courteous.

Try it yourself, using these invented details: parcel number 7741, ordered on 5 October, expected on 9 October, tracking shows "delivered" on 10 October but nothing arrived, this is the second parcel lost, you want a refund.

Sample answer: "I am writing about parcel 7741, which I ordered on 5 October and expected on 9 October. Your tracking page shows it as delivered on 10 October, but it did not arrive at my address. This is the second parcel that has gone missing with your company. I would like a full refund, and I would be grateful for a reply by 24 October. Please tell me if you need any further details."

13.Common Mistakes

MistakeBetter habit
Writing while angry and sending at onceDraft, wait an hour, then edit out insults
A long story with no clear requestPut the request in its own paragraph
"As soon as possible"Give a date or number of days
Mixing several complaintsDeal with one main problem, or number them clearly
Sending original receiptsSend copies and keep the originals
No reference or order numberQuote every number the organisation gave you
Sending personal data you do not need to shareDo not send bank statements; share only what is needed
Threatening what you will not doState only the steps you really plan to take
Writing to "Sir/Madam" when a name was availableFind the name, then use Yours sincerely
Forgetting to keep a copySave a copy and a dated record of every contact

14.Common Myths

MythA louder, angrier letter gets faster results.
FactComplaints guidance advises keeping letters clear, brief and factual. An angry letter is easier to dismiss.
MythA longer letter shows how serious the problem is.
FactGuidance warns that genuine points can get lost in a long complaint. Be brief and well organised.
MythYou should send original documents so nobody can doubt you.
FactSend copies and keep the originals safe.
MythAsking politely means you are weak.
FactA polite, specific letter with a deadline is firm and professional.

15.Key Terms

Complaint: a statement that something is wrong, with a request to put it right.
Request letter: a letter that asks for information, permission or a favour.
Subject line: a short heading that states the topic of the letter.
Enclosure: a document sent with a letter and named in it.
Remedy: the solution you ask for (refund, repair, replacement, apology).
Deadline: the date by which you ask for a reply or action.
Escalate: to take a complaint to a more senior person or outside body.
Ombudsman: an independent official who investigates complaints against organisations.
Acknowledgement: a message confirming that a letter was received.

16.Why This Matters

Everyone eventually needs to complain or ask for something: a damaged product, a wrong bill, a delayed result, a change of date. People who can do it calmly and clearly solve problems faster, keep relationships intact and feel more in control. These letters also train the skills that every formal piece of writing needs: a clear purpose, facts that can be checked, and a respectful tone. Continue to review Formal Letter Writing and Job Applications & Cover Letters.

Key points to remember
  • A complaint letter has one goal: to get the problem fixed.
  • Use the five-part plan: subject line, facts, impact, specific request with deadline, close.
  • Use dates, numbers and names; leave out insults and guesses.
  • Be specific about what you want and by when.
  • Be firm and polite, and never threaten what you will not do.
  • Send copies, keep the originals, and keep a dated record.
  • Follow up, then escalate step by step, with the same calm tone.
  • A request letter states the request first, gives a brief reason, and makes it easy to say yes.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students an angry complaint email and ask them to rewrite it using the five-part plan. Then have a partner play the reader and decide whether they could act on it. Follow with a request letter that students write to their own teacher (for example, asking to use a classroom at break).

Please note: all companies, people and numbers in the examples are invented. Consumer rights and complaint procedures vary by country, so students should check the rules where they live before quoting any law. Greetings and sign-offs for school applications also vary, so follow your school's convention.
