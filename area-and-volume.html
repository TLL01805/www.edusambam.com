Area & Volume: A Complete Guide to Measuring Shapes and Solids

Learn what area, volume, surface area and capacity measure, how to convert their units, and how to find them for plane shapes, circles, sectors, prisms, cylinders, cones, pyramids and spheres, including composite solids, scale factors and bounds.

Area and volume are two of the most useful ideas in measurement. Area is the amount of flat surface a shape covers. Volume is the amount of space a solid takes up. They appear in building, farming, packaging, science and everyday decisions such as how much paint, how many tiles or how many litres of water you need. The real skill is not memorising formulas. It is recognising the shape, finding the right dimensions, choosing the right formula, keeping units consistent, and checking that the answer makes sense. This guide covers units and conversions, every common plane shape, circles and sectors, surface area, volume, capacity, composite solids, scale factors and bounds. It has 26 worked examples checked by computer, five diagrams, three calculators, a practice drill, exam questions with answers, and a 20-question quiz.

1.What Area and Volume Measure

Area measures a two-dimensional region and is written in square units such as cm², m² or km². Volume measures the space inside a three-dimensional solid and is written in cubic units such as cm³ or m³. Capacity is the volume of liquid or gas a container can hold, usually in millilitres (mL) or litres (L).

QuantityDimensionsTypical unitsMeaning
Length, perimeter, circumference1Dmm, cm, m, kmDistance along a line or round a boundary
Area2Dcm², m², ha, km²Flat surface covered
Surface area2D (on a 3D solid)cm², m²Total area of the outside of a solid
Volume3Dcm³, m³Space occupied by a solid
Capacity3DmL, LHow much a container holds
Example 1 · Which quantity?

A classroom floor measures 8 m by 6 m. The floor is flat, so we want area: 8 × 6 = 48 m².

A storage room measures 8 m by 6 m by 2 m. The space inside is three-dimensional, so we want volume: 8 × 6 × 2 = 96 m³.

2.Units and Conversions

A number without its unit is incomplete. Length conversions use a scale factor. Because area is length × length, the factor for area is squared. Because volume is length × length × length, the factor for volume is cubed.

Figure 1. One square metre is a 100 cm by 100 cm square, which holds 10 000 square centimetres.
QuantityConversion
Length1 m = 100 cm = 1000 mm; 1 km = 1000 m
Area1 m² = 10 000 cm²; 1 cm² = 100 mm²; 1 km² = 1 000 000 m²
Land area1 hectare (ha) = 10 000 m²; 1 km² = 100 ha
Volume1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³; 1 cm³ = 1000 mm³
Capacity1 L = 1000 mL = 1000 cm³; 1 mL = 1 cm³; 1 m³ = 1000 L
Example 2 · Why the factor is squared or cubed

Since 1 m = 100 cm, a 1 m by 1 m square is 100 cm by 100 cm. Its area is 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm², so 1 m² = 10 000 cm², not 100 cm².

A 1 m cube is 100 cm × 100 cm × 100 cm, so 1 m³ = 100³ = 1 000 000 cm³.

Example 3 · Converting areas and volumes

(a) 2.5 m² = 2.5 × 10 000 = 25 000 cm².

(b) 3500 cm² = 3500 ÷ 10 000 = 0.35 m².

(c) 0.4 m³ = 0.4 × 1 000 000 = 400 000 cm³ = 400 000 ÷ 1000 = 400 L.

(d) 2.5 km² = 2.5 × 100 = 250 hectares.

“3 m² is 3 × 100 = 300 cm², because 1 m = 100 cm.”

Does the factor 100 apply to areas?

The fix: 100 is the factor for lengths. For areas the factor is 100² = 10 000. So 3 m² = 3 × 10 000 = 30 000 cm². For volumes it would be 100³ = 1 000 000.

3.Rectangles, Squares, Parallelograms and Rhombuses

The area of a rectangle is A = lw. The area of a square of side s is A = s². The area of a parallelogram is A = bh, where b is the base and h is the perpendicular height. A parallelogram is a rectangle that has been slanted without changing its base or height, so the area stays the same. A rhombus (or any kite) with diagonals d₁ and d₂ has area ½d₁d₂.

Example 4 · Rectangle and perimeter

A garden is 12 m long and 7.5 m wide. Area = 12 × 7.5 = 90 m². Fencing round the edge needs the perimeter, not the area: 2(12 + 7.5) = 39 m.

Example 5 · Parallelogram and rhombus

(a) A parallelogram has base 14 cm and perpendicular height 9 cm. A = 14 × 9 = 126 cm².

(b) A rhombus has diagonals 10 cm and 24 cm. A = ½ × 10 × 24 = 120 cm². Its side is √(5² + 12²) = 13 cm, because the diagonals cross at right angles and halve each other.

4.Triangles and Perpendicular Height

The area of a triangle is A = ½bh. The height must be perpendicular to the chosen base. The sloping side of a triangle is usually not the height.

Figure 2. In each shape the dashed line h meets the base at a right angle.
Example 6 · Triangles

(a) Base 18 cm and perpendicular height 11 cm: A = ½ × 18 × 11 = 99 cm².

(b) An isosceles triangle has base 10 cm and equal sides 13 cm. The height splits the base into two halves of 5 cm, so h = √(13² − 5²) = √144 = 12 cm. A = ½ × 10 × 12 = 60 cm².

“Triangle with base 10 cm and sloping sides 13 cm. A = ½ × 10 × 13 = 65 cm².”

Is the sloping side the perpendicular height?

The fix: the height must be at right angles to the base. The sloping side is longer than the height. Using Pythagoras, h = √(13² − 5²) = 12, so A = ½ × 10 × 12 = 60 cm².

Two more ways to find a triangle’s area

If you know two sides and the angle between them, use A = ½ab sin C (see our sine and cosine rule article). If you know all three sides, use Heron’s formula: let s be half the perimeter, s = (a + b + c) ÷ 2, then A = √[s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)].

Example 7 · Sine formula and Heron’s formula

(a) Two sides 8 cm and 10 cm enclose an angle of 30°. A = ½ × 8 × 10 × sin 30° = ½ × 80 × 0.5 = 20 cm².

(b) A triangle has sides 13 cm, 14 cm and 15 cm. s = 21. A = √(21 × 8 × 7 × 6) = √7056 = 84 cm².

5.Trapezia and Compound Plane Shapes

A trapezium has one pair of parallel sides, a and b. Its area is A = ½(a + b)h, where h is the perpendicular distance between the parallel sides. It is the mean of the two parallel sides multiplied by the height.

Example 8 · Trapezium

Parallel sides 10 cm and 16 cm, height 7 cm. A = ½(10 + 16) × 7 = 13 × 7 = 91 cm².

For a compound shape, split it into familiar shapes, find each area, then add (or subtract for a hole or a missing corner).

Example 9 · Compound areas

(a) A 12 cm × 8 cm rectangle has a triangle attached on one 12 cm side, with perpendicular height 5 cm. Rectangle = 96 cm². Triangle = ½ × 12 × 5 = 30 cm². Total = 126 cm².

(b) A 10 cm × 8 cm rectangle has a 4 cm × 3 cm corner removed. Area = 80 − 12 = 68 cm².

6.Circles

A circle with radius r has diameter d = 2r. The circumference is C = 2πr = πd and the area is A = πr². Circumference is a length (a linear unit). Area is a surface (a square unit). If the question gives the diameter, halve it first.

Example 10 · Circles

(a) Radius 6 cm: area = π × 6² = 36π ≈ 113.1 cm². Circumference = 2π × 6 = 12π ≈ 37.7 cm.

(b) Diameter 10 cm: the radius is 5 cm, so area = π × 5² = 25π cm² (not 100π).

“A circle has diameter 10 cm, so its area is π × 10² = 100π cm².”

What should be squared?

The fix: the formula uses the radius. The radius is 10 ÷ 2 = 5 cm, so A = π × 5² = 25π cm² ≈ 78.5 cm². The wrong answer is four times too large.

Leave answers in terms of π when the question asks for an exact answer. When rounding is needed, keep full calculator precision until the last step.

7.Sectors and Arcs

A sector is a slice of a circle. If the angle at the centre is θ degrees, the sector is the fraction θ ÷ 360 of the whole circle.

Sector area = (θ ÷ 360) × πr²    Arc length = (θ ÷ 360) × 2πr
Figure 3. A 90° sector is one quarter of the circle, so its area and arc are one quarter of the full circle’s area and circumference.
Example 11 · Sectors

(a) Radius 8 cm and angle 90°: area = ¼ × π × 64 = 16π cm². Arc = ¼ × 2π × 8 = 4π cm. The perimeter of the sector is 8 + 8 + 4π ≈ 28.6 cm, because the two straight radii are part of the boundary.

(b) Radius 12 cm and angle 150°: the fraction is 150 ÷ 360 = 5/12. Area = 5/12 × π × 144 = 60π ≈ 188.5 cm². Arc = 5/12 × 2π × 12 = 10π ≈ 31.4 cm.

Example 12 · A window shape

A window is a rectangle 80 cm wide and 120 cm high with a semicircle of radius 40 cm on top. Area = 80 × 120 + ½ × π × 40² = 9600 + 800π ≈ 12 113.3 cm². Its perimeter goes round the two sides, the bottom and the curved top: 120 + 120 + 80 + π × 40 ≈ 445.7 cm. The straight line where the semicircle joins the rectangle is not part of the perimeter.

8.Surface Area

Surface area is the total area of the outside faces of a solid. It is measured in square units. A net is the solid unfolded flat, so its surface area is the area of the net.

Figure 4. A cuboid has three pairs of matching rectangular faces: 10 × 6, 10 × 4 and 6 × 4.
Cuboid: Surface area = 2(lw + lh + wh)    Cube: Surface area = 6s²
Example 13 · Cuboid and cube

(a) A 10 cm × 6 cm × 4 cm cuboid: 2(60 + 40 + 24) = 248 cm².

(b) A cube of side 5 cm: 6 × 25 = 150 cm².

9.Volume of Prisms

The volume of a cuboid is V = lwh. A prism is a solid with the same cross-section all the way along its length, so V = cross-sectional area × length. If you can find the cross-section and the length, you can find the volume of any prism, including a cylinder.

Example 14 · Cuboids and prisms

(a) A tank 2.4 m × 1.5 m × 1.2 m: V = 4.32 m³.

(b) A prism has cross-section 35 cm² and length 18 cm: V = 35 × 18 = 630 cm³.

(c) A triangular prism has a right-angled triangle cross-section with sides 3, 4 and 5 cm, and length 10 cm. The cross-section area is ½ × 3 × 4 = 6 cm², so V = 6 × 10 = 60 cm³. Its surface area is two triangles, 2 × 6 = 12 cm², plus three rectangles with total width (3 + 4 + 5) = 12 and length 10, which is 120 cm². Total = 132 cm².

10.Cylinders

A cylinder is a prism with a circle as its cross-section. V = πr²h. Its curved surface unrolls into a rectangle of width 2πr and height h, so the curved surface area is 2πrh. The total surface area adds the two circular ends: 2πr² + 2πrh.

Example 15 · Cylinder

Radius 5 cm and height 12 cm. V = π × 25 × 12 = 300π ≈ 942.5 cm³. Curved surface area = 2π × 5 × 12 = 120π cm². Total surface area = 2π × 25 + 120π = 170π cm². Always check whether the question asks for the curved or the total surface area.

The perpendicular height h is used for volume. The slant height l is used for the sloping surface. For a cone, l² = r² + h².

Example 16 · Pyramid and cone

(a) A square-based pyramid has base side 6 cm and perpendicular height 4 cm. V = ⅓ × 36 × 4 = 48 cm³. The slant height of each triangular face is √(3² + 4²) = 5 cm, so each face has area ½ × 6 × 5 = 15 cm². Total surface area = 36 + 4 × 15 = 96 cm².

(b) A cone has radius 3 cm and height 4 cm. The slant height is √(3² + 4²) = 5 cm. V = ⅓ × π × 9 × 4 = 12π cm³. Curved surface area = π × 3 × 5 = 15π cm². Total surface area = 9π + 15π = 24π cm².

Example 17 · Sphere and hemisphere

(a) A sphere of radius 3 cm: V = 4/3 × π × 27 = 36π cm³ and surface area = 4π × 9 = 36π cm².

(b) A hemisphere of radius 6 cm: V = 2/3 × π × 216 = 144π cm³. Its total surface area, including the flat circle, is 3π × 36 = 108π cm².

12.Capacity and Litres

1 cm³ holds 1 mL, so 1000 cm³ = 1 L, and 1 m³ = 1000 L. Use internal dimensions for capacity, because the thickness of the walls takes up room.

Example 18 · Tanks

(a) A tank measures 80 cm × 50 cm × 40 cm inside. V = 160 000 cm³ = 160 L. A tap that delivers 20 L per minute fills it in 160 ÷ 20 = 8 minutes.

(b) A cylindrical tank has radius 0.5 m and height 1.4 m. V = π × 0.25 × 1.4 ≈ 1.0996 m³, which is about 1100 litres.

13.Composite and Hollow Solids

For a composite solid, split it into familiar solids, find each volume, then add or subtract. For surface area, count only the faces that are actually on the outside. A face where two solids are joined is no longer exposed.

Example 19 · A cylinder with a hemisphere on top

Both have radius 3 cm and the cylinder is 8 cm high. Volume = π × 9 × 8 + 2/3 × π × 27 = 72π + 18π = 90π ≈ 282.7 cm³.

The outside surface, standing on its flat base, is the base circle π × 9 = 9π, the curved side of the cylinder 2π × 3 × 8 = 48π, and the curved hemisphere 2π × 9 = 18π. Total = 9π + 48π + 18π = 75π cm². The circle where they join is inside, so it is not counted.

Example 20 · Hollow and drilled solids

(a) A pipe has outer radius 5 cm, inner radius 3 cm and length 20 cm. Material = outer − inner = π × 25 × 20 − π × 9 × 20 = 500π − 180π = 320π cm³.

(b) A block 20 cm × 10 cm × 5 cm has a cylindrical hole of radius 2 cm drilled through its 5 cm thickness. Volume = 1000 − π × 4 × 5 = 1000 − 20π ≈ 937.2 cm³.

Example 21 · A frustum

A cone of radius 6 cm and height 9 cm has its top cut off by a plane parallel to the base. The piece removed is a smaller cone of height 3 cm, which is similar to the large cone with scale factor 3 ÷ 9 = 1/3 (so its radius is 2 cm). Large cone = ⅓ × π × 36 × 9 = 108π. Small cone = ⅓ × π × 4 × 3 = 4π. The remaining frustum has volume 108π − 4π = 104π cm³. As a check, the small cone is (1/3)³ = 1/27 of the large one, and 108π ÷ 27 = 4π.

14.Finding a Missing Dimension

Substitute the known values into the formula and solve the equation, as in our algebraic equations guide.

Example 22 · Missing dimensions

(a) A rectangle has area 96 cm² and length 12 cm. 96 = 12w, so w = 8 cm.

(b) A circle has area 49π cm². πr² = 49π, so r² = 49 and r = 7 cm (a radius cannot be negative).

(c) A sphere has volume 36π cm³. 4/3 πr³ = 36π, so r³ = 36 ÷ (4/3) = 27 and r = 3 cm.

(d) A cylinder has volume 200π cm³ and height 8 cm. π × r² × 8 = 200π, so r² = 25 and r = 5 cm.

15.Scale Factors for Length, Area and Volume

When a shape is enlarged by a linear scale factor k, lengths are multiplied by k, areas by k² and volumes by k³. This holds for any two similar shapes.

Figure 5. Doubling every length of a cube makes room for 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 small cubes, and each face gets 4 times as many squares.

Lengthk× 3

Example 23 · Similar shapes

(a) A model is built to scale 1 : 10 (k = 10 from model to real). The model has volume 200 cm³ and surface area 150 cm². The real object has volume 10³ × 200 = 200 000 cm³ = 200 L and surface area 10² × 150 = 15 000 cm².

(b) Two similar shapes have areas 20 cm² and 45 cm². The scale factor of lengths is √(45 ÷ 20) = √2.25 = 1.5. If the smaller solid has volume 80 cm³, the larger has volume 80 × 1.5³ = 80 × 27/8 = 270 cm³.

“The sides of a box are doubled, so its volume is doubled.”

How many small boxes fit in the big one?

The fix: doubling every length multiplies the volume by 2³ = 8, not 2. The surface area is multiplied by 2² = 4. A box twice as tall, wide and deep holds eight times as much.

16.Bounds and Accuracy

A measurement rounded to the nearest centimetre could be up to half a centimetre either side of the stated value. 8 cm to the nearest cm means 7.5 ≤ length < 8.5. For the largest area or volume, use the upper bounds of the dimensions. For the smallest, use the lower bounds.

Example 24 · Bounds of an area and a volume

(a) A rectangle is 12 cm by 8 cm, each to the nearest cm. Lower bound of area = 11.5 × 7.5 = 86.25 cm². Upper bound = 12.5 × 8.5 = 106.25 cm².

(b) The side of a cube is 5.0 cm to 1 decimal place, so 4.95 ≤ s < 5.05. The volume lies between 4.95³ ≈ 121.3 cm³ and 5.05³ ≈ 128.8 cm³.

17.Area and Volume in Real Life

Example 25 · Tiles, paint and land

(a) Tiles. A floor is 4.2 m by 3.6 m. Tiles are 30 cm by 30 cm. The floor needs 4.2 ÷ 0.3 = 14 tiles one way and 3.6 ÷ 0.3 = 12 tiles the other, so 14 × 12 = 168 tiles. In practice, buy extra for cutting and breakage.

(b) Paint. A wall is 5 m by 2.8 m, with a door 0.9 m by 2.1 m and a window 1.2 m by 1.0 m. Paintable area = 14 − 1.89 − 1.2 = 10.91 m². If one litre covers 8 m², you need 10.91 ÷ 8 ≈ 1.36 L, so buy 2 L.

(c) Land. In Pakistan, plots are often measured in marla and kanal. A marla is commonly taken as 272.25 square feet (about 25.3 m²), and 1 kanal is 20 marla (5445 square feet, about 506 m²). The size of a marla can vary by region and by authority, so always check the definition used in your own area before calculating.

Example 26 · Multi-step

A cylinder has radius 4 cm and height 10 cm. It is filled to 75% of its capacity. Full volume = π × 16 × 10 = 160π cm³. Liquid volume = 0.75 × 160π = 120π ≈ 377.0 cm³. The percentage applies to the volume, not to the radius or the height.

18.A Short History

People measured land long before they had formulas. Ancient Egyptian and Babylonian scribes calculated areas of fields and volumes of granaries. Greek mathematicians gave proofs. Archimedes (3rd century BCE) is credited with showing that a sphere has two-thirds of the volume of the cylinder that just encloses it. With radius r, that cylinder has height 2r, so its volume is πr² × 2r = 2πr³, and two-thirds of that is 4/3 πr³, the formula we use today. Archimedes also found that π lies between 3 10/71 and 3 1/7, a range that makes 22/7 a good approximation. Dates and details from the ancient world are approximate.

19.Common Mistakes

  • Using the diameter as the radius. Halve the diameter first.
  • Using the sloping side as the height of a triangle, parallelogram or trapezium.
  • Mixing up area and perimeter, or surface area and volume.
  • Forgetting the square or the cube in πr², 4πr² and 4/3 πr³.
  • Forgetting the ½ for a triangle or trapezium, or the ⅓ for a cone or pyramid.
  • Using slant height for volume, or perpendicular height for curved surface area.
  • Converting units wrongly. Area factors are squared and volume factors are cubed.
  • Counting hidden faces in the surface area of a composite solid.
  • Using external dimensions for the capacity of a container.
  • Rounding too early, or leaving out units.

20.A Reliable Method

  1. Read the question and decide exactly what is wanted: perimeter, area, surface area, volume or capacity.
  2. Draw or label the shape and mark every known dimension. Find any missing dimension first (Pythagoras, similar shapes or algebra).
  3. Split a compound shape into familiar parts.
  4. Convert all lengths to the same unit before calculating.
  5. Write the formula, substitute, and keep π or surds until the last step if you can.
  6. Check the unit: square units for area, cubic units for volume.
  7. Ask whether the size of the answer is reasonable.

21.Try It Yourself

  1. Convert 0.35 m³ to litres, and 7500 cm² to m².
  2. Find the area of a semicircle of radius 10 cm, to 1 decimal place.
  3. A cube has volume 125 cm³. Find its side and its surface area.
Show answers

1. 0.35 × 1000 = 350 L. 7500 ÷ 10 000 = 0.75 m².

2. Half of π × 10² = 50π ≈ 157.1 cm².

3. The side is the cube root of 125, which is 5 cm. The surface area is 6 × 5² = 150 cm².

22.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [4 marks] (a) An isosceles triangle has base 10 cm and equal sides 13 cm. Find its area. (b) A trapezium has parallel sides 9 cm and 15 cm and perpendicular height 6 cm. Find its area.
  2. [4 marks] A sector has radius 12 cm and angle 150°. Find (a) its area and (b) its arc length, and (c) its perimeter, each to 1 decimal place.
  3. [5 marks] A cylinder has diameter 14 cm and height 20 cm. Find (a) its volume to 1 decimal place, (b) its capacity in litres to 2 decimal places, (c) its curved surface area to 1 decimal place.
  4. [5 marks] A cone has radius 6 cm and perpendicular height 8 cm. Find (a) its slant height, (b) its volume, (c) its total surface area. Give (b) and (c) in terms of π.
  5. [4 marks] Two similar jars have heights 10 cm and 25 cm. The smaller jar holds 400 mL. Find (a) the ratio of their surface areas and (b) the capacity of the larger jar in litres.
  6. [4 marks] A block 20 cm × 10 cm × 5 cm has a cylindrical hole of radius 2 cm drilled through its thickness of 5 cm. Find the volume of the remaining solid to 1 decimal place.
  7. [3 marks] The side of a square is 7.4 cm to the nearest 0.1 cm. Find the lower and upper bounds of its area.
Show answers

1. (a) The height is √(13² − 5²) = 12 cm, so A = ½ × 10 × 12 = 60 cm². (b) A = ½(9 + 15) × 6 = 72 cm².

2. The fraction is 150 ÷ 360 = 5/12. (a) Area = 5/12 × π × 144 = 60π ≈ 188.5 cm². (b) Arc = 5/12 × 2π × 12 = 10π ≈ 31.4 cm. (c) Perimeter = 12 + 12 + 10π ≈ 55.4 cm.

3. The radius is 7 cm. (a) V = π × 49 × 20 = 980π ≈ 3078.8 cm³. (b) 3078.8 ÷ 1000 ≈ 3.08 L. (c) Curved surface area = 2π × 7 × 20 = 280π ≈ 879.6 cm².

4. (a) l = √(6² + 8²) = 10 cm. (b) V = ⅓ × π × 36 × 8 = 96π cm³. (c) Total surface area = π × 36 + π × 6 × 10 = 36π + 60π = 96π cm².

5. The scale factor is 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5. (a) The ratio of areas is 2.5² = 6.25, which is 25 : 4 (large to small). (b) The volume is 400 × 2.5³ = 400 × 15.625 = 6250 mL = 6.25 L.

6. V = 20 × 10 × 5 − π × 2² × 5 = 1000 − 20π ≈ 937.2 cm³.

7. The side lies between 7.35 and 7.45. Lower bound of area = 7.35² = 54.0225 cm². Upper bound = 7.45² = 55.5025 cm².

23.Key Points

  • Area is in square units and volume in cubic units. Convert area with the square of the length factor and volume with the cube.
  • Triangle ½bh, parallelogram bh, trapezium ½(a + b)h, circle πr². The height is always perpendicular.
  • Sector area and arc length are the fraction θ ÷ 360 of the circle’s area and circumference.
  • Volume of a prism or cylinder = cross-section × length. Cones and pyramids have a factor of ⅓.
  • Sphere: V = 4/3 πr³ and surface area = 4πr².
  • 1 cm³ = 1 mL, 1000 cm³ = 1 L and 1 m³ = 1000 L.
  • For similar shapes, lengths scale by k, areas by k² and volumes by k³.
  • Use upper bounds for the largest area or volume and lower bounds for the smallest.
  • For composite solids, split into parts and count only exposed faces for surface area.

24.For Teachers and Parents

Let learners build understanding with real objects. Cover a rectangle with squared paper to see that area is a count of squares, then show that a parallelogram can be cut and rearranged into a rectangle. Build a 1 m square from newspaper and fill it with 10 cm squares to see why 1 m² is 10 000 cm², not 100. Use a measuring jug and a small box to show that 1 cm³ holds 1 mL. For the sphere, fill a cone and a hemisphere with rice and pour them into a cylinder to compare. The Shape Area Calculator, Solid Calculator and Unit Converter on this page let learners check their own working, but they should always write the formula and substitute before using a calculator. Ask learners to say the unit aloud at the end of every answer.
