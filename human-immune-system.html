The Human Body's Immune System

A clear guide to barriers, innate and adaptive immunity, antibodies, T cells, inflammation, memory and what happens when immunity goes wrong.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|Biology|In-depth learning article|Last updated: October 2026

Every day your body meets bacteria, viruses, fungi and other microbes, and most of the time you never notice. That is because the immune system works constantly in the background, using layers of defence that range from your skin to cells that remember a microbe for years. This article explains each layer, how they cooperate and what happens when the system makes mistakes.

Written for students up to O-Level (grades 8 to 10), and useful for parents and teachers. This is general education, not medical advice.

1.What the Immune System Is and Does

The immune system is a network of cells, tissues, organs and chemical signals that protects the body against pathogens (disease-causing microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and some parasites) and also removes damaged or abnormal body cells. It has two difficult jobs at once: respond strongly enough to stop real threats, and avoid harming the body's own healthy tissue.

To do this, the body treats its defence as three layers. The first layers act quickly but generally; the last layer acts more slowly but very precisely and keeps a memory.

Figure 1. Original EDUSAMBAM diagram of the three layers of defence. A microbe that gets past one layer meets the next. The red dot is animated.

2.Self, Non-self and Antigens

Immune cells must tell the difference between self (the body's own cells) and non-self (foreign invaders). They do this by recognising antigens: molecules, usually proteins or carbohydrates on the surface of a cell or virus, that the immune system can detect. Each pathogen has its own pattern of antigens, like a name tag. Your own cells carry markers that normally tell the immune system 'do not attack'.

3.Layer 1: Barriers

Preventing microbes from entering is far safer than fighting them once inside. The main barriers are:

BarrierHow it protects
SkinA tough outer layer of dead cells with few gaps; microbes cannot easily pass through it when unbroken.
Mucus and ciliaSticky mucus in the airways traps dust and microbes; tiny hair-like cilia sweep it up toward the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out.
Tears and salivaContain lysozyme, an enzyme that breaks down the cell walls of many bacteria.
Stomach acidStrongly acidic, so it kills or damages many microbes that are swallowed with food.
Friendly gut and skin microbesHarmless bacteria use up food and space, making it harder for harmful microbes to settle.
Blood clottingPlugs a cut with a clot and scab, sealing the entry point.

4.Layer 2: The Innate Response

If a microbe breaks through, the innate (born-with) response starts within minutes to hours. It is not aimed at any particular microbe; it reacts to danger signs in general, and it is the same each time.

Inflammation

Inflammation is the redness, heat, swelling and pain around a cut or infection. It is a sign that defences are at work, not just a problem.

Figure 2. Inflammation at a cut. Original EDUSAMBAM diagram; numbers match the list below.
1 Bacteria enter through a cut2 Mast cells release signals (histamine)3 Vessel widens: more blood, redness, heat4 Fluid leaks out: swelling and pain5 Phagocytes leave the vessel and engulf bacteria
  1. Damaged cells and mast cells release chemicals such as histamine.
  2. The chemicals make small blood vessels near the injury widen, so more blood flows in. This causes redness and heat.
  3. The vessel walls become leakier, so fluid escapes into the tissue and causes swelling, which presses on nerve endings and contributes to pain.
  4. Phagocytes squeeze out of the vessel and move toward the microbes.

Phagocytes and other innate cells

  • Phagocytes (for example neutrophils and macrophages) engulf microbes and digest them inside lysosomes. Pus is largely a mixture of dead phagocytes, microbes and tissue fluid. Macrophages can also display pieces of microbes to other immune cells.
  • Natural killer (NK) cells destroy some virus-infected cells and abnormal cells.
  • Interferons are proteins released by virus-infected cells that warn neighbouring cells to strengthen their defences.
  • Complement is a group of blood proteins that can mark microbes for phagocytes and can damage some microbes directly.

Fever

Chemical signals can raise the body's temperature set-point, producing a fever. A moderate rise in temperature may slow the growth of some microbes and help some immune processes, but a very high or long-lasting fever, or any fever in a baby, needs medical advice.

5.Layer 3: The Adaptive Response

The adaptive response is slower (several days the first time) but highly specific, and it remembers. It is carried out by white blood cells called lymphocytes:

CellWhere it maturesMain job
B cellBone marrowRecognises a specific antigen; when activated, divides and becomes plasma cells that make antibodies, plus memory cells.
Helper T cellThymusCoordinates the response by activating B cells and other immune cells.
Killer (cytotoxic) T cellThymusFinds and destroys body cells infected with a virus, and some abnormal cells.
Memory B and T cellsFormed after an infection or vaccinationSurvive for a long time and respond quickly if the same antigen returns.
Figure 3. A simplified adaptive response in the body. The gold dot is animated.

Antibodies are Y-shaped proteins. Each type has tips shaped to fit one particular antigen, like a key and lock. Antibodies can: block a virus so it cannot enter cells (neutralisation), clump microbes together, and mark microbes so phagocytes can find them. They do not kill microbes by themselves in most cases; they help other defences do so.

The body has millions of different B and T cells, each with receptors for a different antigen. When an antigen matches one of them, that cell is selected and copied, which is why the response is called clonal selection.

6.Primary and Secondary Responses

The first time the body meets an antigen, it takes several days to build up enough antibodies. This is the primary response. If the same antigen returns, memory cells respond much faster, produce more antibodies and keep producing them for longer: the secondary response. That is why you usually catch diseases such as chickenpox only once, and it is the principle behind vaccination.

Figure 4. Antibody level over time (a shape sketch, not measured data). The curves draw themselves in turn; with reduced motion they are shown at once.
Worked example: reading the graph

Use the shape of Figure 4. Practice values (invented for this exercise): primary peak = 10 units at day 14; secondary peak = 80 units at day 5 after the second exposure.

  1. Compare the sizes of the peaks: 80 ÷ 10 = 8 times higher in the secondary response.
  2. Compare the timing: 14 days compared with 5 days, so the secondary response is 9 days faster.
  3. Explain: memory B cells already exist and can quickly become plasma cells, so antibodies appear sooner and in larger amounts.

Always answer 'explain' questions with the reason (memory cells), not just the description (faster and bigger).

7.Active and Passive Immunity

TypeExampleMemory?How long
Natural activeRecovering from an infectionYesOften long-lasting
Artificial activeVaccinationYesWeeks to a lifetime, depending on the vaccine
Natural passiveAntibodies passed from mother to baby across the placenta and in breast milkNoWeeks to months
Artificial passiveInjection of ready-made antibodies, for example some antivenoms and antitoxinsNoShort

Active immunity means your own body makes the antibodies and memory cells. Passive immunity means antibodies are supplied from elsewhere, so protection is immediate but temporary because no memory cells are formed.

Worked example: classify the immunity

Decide whether each is natural or artificial, and active or passive.

  1. A baby is protected against some infections by antibodies from breast milk: natural passive (antibodies come from the mother, no memory).
  2. A student has a vaccine and later makes her own memory cells: artificial active.
  3. A child recovers from chickenpox: natural active.
  4. A hospital gives antibodies to a patient after exposure to a dangerous toxin: artificial passive.

8.The Lymphatic System and Immune Organs

Tissue fluid that drains from the tissues becomes lymph and flows through lymph vessels back to the blood. On the way it passes through lymph nodes, small organs packed with lymphocytes and phagocytes that filter the lymph and are where immune cells often meet antigens. This is why nodes can swell and feel tender during an infection.

OrganRole in immunity
Bone marrowMakes all blood cells, including white blood cells; B cells mature here.
ThymusWhere T cells mature; most active in childhood.
Lymph nodesFilter lymph and host immune responses.
SpleenFilters blood, removes old red blood cells and contains many lymphocytes.
TonsilsLymphoid tissue at the throat that samples microbes that are breathed in or swallowed.

9.When the Immune System Goes Wrong

  • Allergy: the immune system overreacts to a harmless substance such as pollen, dust mites or a food. A severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) is a medical emergency that needs urgent help.
  • Autoimmune disease: the immune system attacks the body's own tissues. Type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis are examples; their exact causes are still being researched.
  • Immune deficiency: part of the defence is missing or weak. This can be inherited, or acquired, as in HIV infection, where the virus damages helper T cells.
  • Transplant rejection: the immune system recognises a donated organ as non-self, so patients usually need medicines to dampen this response.

10.Can You 'Boost' Your Immune System?

Advertisements often promise to 'boost immunity', but a healthy immune system is already working; making it 'stronger' than normal is not a goal and an overactive system causes allergy and autoimmunity. What is supported by evidence is helping it work normally: enough sleep, a varied diet, regular physical activity, not smoking, handwashing, safe food handling and keeping vaccinations up to date. If you often become ill or have symptoms that worry you, talk to a doctor.

11.Key Terms

TermMeaning
PathogenA microorganism that causes disease
AntigenA molecule the immune system can recognise, often on a pathogen's surface
PhagocyteA white blood cell that engulfs and digests microbes
LymphocyteA white blood cell (B or T cell) responsible for the adaptive response
AntibodyA Y-shaped protein made by plasma cells that fits one specific antigen
Memory cellA long-lived lymphocyte that allows a faster response next time
InflammationRedness, heat, swelling and pain caused by widened, leakier blood vessels
Active immunityProtection from your own antibodies and memory cells
Passive immunityTemporary protection from antibodies made elsewhere

12.Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Thinking antibodies are living cells. They are proteins made by plasma cells.
  • Saying white blood cells 'fight' using one method. Phagocytes engulf; B cells make antibodies; killer T cells destroy infected cells.
  • Confusing immunity with vaccination. Vaccination is one way of gaining active immunity.
  • Saying passive immunity has memory. It does not, so it is short-lived.
  • Assuming all fevers or all inflammation are harmful. They are often part of a normal defence, but severe or prolonged signs need medical advice.
  • Mixing up antibiotics and antiviral medicines with the immune system. Antibiotics act on bacteria and do not work on viruses.

13.Summary

  • The immune system has three layers: barriers, the fast general innate response, and the slower specific adaptive response.
  • Inflammation widens vessels and brings phagocytes to the site of injury.
  • B cells become plasma cells that make antibodies; T cells coordinate the response or kill infected cells; memory cells give faster protection later.
  • Active immunity (infection or vaccine) makes memory cells; passive immunity gives short-term antibodies only.
  • Allergy, autoimmune disease and immune deficiency are examples of the system going wrong.
  • Healthy habits help the immune system work normally, but no food or supplement can 'boost' it beyond normal.

14.Check Your Understanding

1. List the three layers of defence in order.
Answer: Barriers; the innate response; the adaptive response.
2. Explain why a cut becomes red, warm and swollen.
Answer: Chemicals such as histamine widen nearby blood vessels, so more blood arrives (redness, heat). The vessels also become leakier, so fluid enters the tissue (swelling).
3. Why does a second infection with the same microbe usually cause less illness?
Answer: Memory B and T cells recognise the antigen and respond faster and more strongly, so the microbe is removed before it spreads far.
4. A baby receives antibodies through breast milk. Classify this type of immunity and say why it does not last long.
Answer: Natural passive immunity. The antibodies are made by the mother, not the baby, so there are no memory cells to keep making them.
5. What is the role of helper T cells?
Answer: They coordinate the response by activating B cells and other immune cells.
6. Why are antibiotics not used to treat colds?
Answer: Colds are caused by viruses, and antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses.

15.For Teachers and Parents

Notes for adults
Suggested use. Work through the three-layer idea first, then ask learners to retell the cut story in their own words using Figure 2. Activity. Role-play: learners act as microbes, barriers, phagocytes, T cells and B cells in a short classroom drama. Teaching note. Immunology is simplified here to O-Level depth; real immune responses involve many more cell types and signals. Keep discussion of allergy, autoimmune disease and fever general, and refer individual health concerns to a doctor. This is general education, not medical advice.
