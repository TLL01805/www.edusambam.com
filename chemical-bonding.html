Chemical Bonding: How Atoms Connect to Form Everything

Why does salt melt at 801 °C while oxygen is a gas? The answer is in how atoms connect. Learn the three types of bonding and what they mean for properties.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 18 min read | Updated 21 October 2026

Atoms rarely exist on their own. Almost every substance you meet is made of atoms joined together, and the way they are joined decides whether the substance is a hard crystal, a flexible metal or a gas. This article explains the three main types of bonding, how to draw them, and how to link bonding to properties.

1.Why Do Atoms Bond?

Most atoms are more stable when their outer shell is full, as in the noble gases. Atoms bond by losing, gaining or sharing electrons to reach a full outer shell. Which of these happens depends on the types of atoms involved.

Bonding typeBetweenWhat happens to electrons
IonicMetal and non-metalElectrons are transferred, forming positive and negative ions
CovalentNon-metal and non-metalElectrons are shared in pairs
MetallicMetal atoms onlyOuter electrons are delocalised, shared in a sea around positive ions

2.Ionic Bonding

When a metal reacts with a non-metal, the metal atom loses its outer electrons to form a positive ion, and the non-metal atom gains them to form a negative ion. The strong electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions is the ionic bond.

Figure 1. Sodium chloride. The sodium atom gives its one outer electron (gold, moving) to chlorine. The resulting Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions both have full outer shells and attract each other.

Ionic compounds do not form separate molecules. Instead, each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge in a regular, repeating arrangement called a giant ionic lattice.

Working out ionic formulae

The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge, so the compound is neutral overall. Use the charges of the ions (from the group number): Group 1 gives +1, Group 2 gives +2, Group 13 (aluminium) gives +3, Group 16 gives −2 and Group 17 gives −1.

Worked example 1: formula of aluminium oxide

Question: Write the formula of aluminium oxide.

  1. Aluminium ion: Al³⁺. Oxide ion: O²⁻.
  2. Find the lowest common multiple of 3 and 2, which is 6.
  3. Two Al³⁺ give +6 and three O²⁻ give −6, so the ratio is 2 : 3.
Al₂O₃

Check: 2 × (+3) + 3 × (−2) = 0.

Worked example 2: formula of magnesium chloride

Question: Write the formula of magnesium chloride.

  1. Magnesium ion: Mg²⁺; chloride ion: Cl⁻.
  2. One Mg²⁺ (+2) needs two Cl⁻ (−2).
  3. Formula: MgCl₂.

Properties of ionic compounds

  • High melting and boiling points: a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong attractions throughout the lattice. Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C.
  • Hard but brittle: if a layer shifts, ions of the same charge line up and repel, so the crystal splits.
  • Do not conduct when solid (ions are fixed in place) but conduct when molten or dissolved in water, because the ions are free to move.
  • Many are soluble in water, though not all.

3.Covalent Bonding

When two non-metal atoms meet, neither can easily lose electrons, so they share them. A shared pair of electrons is a covalent bond, held between the two positive nuclei. Sharing one pair makes a single bond, two pairs a double bond, and three pairs a triple bond. Each atom counts the shared electrons as part of its outer shell.

Figure 2. Dot-and-cross diagrams. In water, oxygen shares two pairs with two hydrogen atoms; in ammonia, nitrogen shares three pairs; in nitrogen gas, the two atoms share three pairs, a triple bond. Unshared pairs are called lone pairs.
MoleculeFormulaBondsNotes
HydrogenH₂1 single bondEach H has 2 electrons (a full first shell)
ChlorineCl₂1 single bondEach Cl has 3 lone pairs
OxygenO₂1 double bondTwo shared pairs
MethaneCH₄4 single bondsCarbon shares 4 pairs
Carbon dioxideCO₂2 double bondsO=C=O

Simple molecular substances

Substances such as water, methane, oxygen and carbon dioxide are made of separate small molecules. The covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong, but the forces between molecules (intermolecular forces) are weak. When such a substance melts or boils, only these weak forces are overcome, not the covalent bonds.

Figure 3. Compare a giant ionic lattice with simple molecules. Strong forces act throughout the lattice, but only weak forces act between separate molecules, which explains their very different melting points.
  • Low melting and boiling points, so many are gases or liquids at room temperature.
  • Do not conduct electricity, because there are no free charged particles.
  • Often soluble in organic solvents; some, like sugar and ethanol, dissolve in water.

4.Giant Covalent Structures

Some covalent substances form huge networks of atoms joined by covalent bonds, with no separate molecules. They have very high melting points because many strong bonds must be broken.

SubstanceStructureProperties and uses
DiamondEach carbon bonded to 4 others in a rigid 3-D tetrahedral networkExtremely hard, does not conduct; cutting tools, jewellery
GraphiteEach carbon bonded to 3 others in flat layers; one electron per atom is free to moveSoft and slippery (layers slide), conducts electricity; pencils, electrodes, lubricants
Silicon dioxide (silica)Each silicon bonded to 4 oxygen atoms in a 3-D networkHard, high melting point; sand and quartz, used in glass

Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon, but their different structures give them completely different properties. Carbon can also form other structures, such as graphene (a single layer of graphite) and fullerenes (cage-like molecules), which are important in current materials research.

5.Metallic Bonding

In a metal, atoms lose their outer electrons, which become delocalised and move freely among a lattice of positive ions. The attraction between the ions and the electron sea is the metallic bond. This explains why metals conduct electricity and heat and can be bent and drawn into wires (the layers of ions slide while remaining bonded), as discussed in the Metals and Non-Metals article.

When answering a question about properties, use the pattern: name the structure, name the particles, say what forces must be overcome or what is free to move. For example: “Sodium chloride has a high melting point because it has a giant lattice with strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions that need much energy to break.”

Worked example 3: explain the property

Question: Explain why methane (CH₄) has a very low boiling point, although it contains strong covalent bonds.

Answer: Methane is a simple molecular substance. When it boils, only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules are overcome, not the strong covalent bonds inside the molecules. Little energy is needed, so the boiling point is very low.

7.Predicting the Type of Bonding

You can usually decide the bonding type from the elements, using the periodic table:

  1. Is the substance a single metal or a mixture of metals (an alloy)? It has metallic bonding.
  2. Is it a metal combined with a non-metal? It is usually ionic.
  3. Is it made of non-metals only? It is covalent. Then ask whether it forms separate small molecules (simple molecular) or a huge network (giant covalent, as in diamond or silica).
Worked example 4: classify and predict

Question: Classify the bonding in (a) potassium bromide, KBr, (b) sulfur dioxide, SO₂, (c) copper. Say whether each conducts when solid.

  1. (a) Potassium is a metal and bromine is a non-metal, so KBr is ionic with a giant lattice. It does not conduct when solid, but conducts when molten or dissolved.
  2. (b) Sulfur and oxygen are both non-metals, so SO₂ is covalent, made of simple molecules. It does not conduct.
  3. (c) Copper is a metal, so it has metallic bonding. It conducts when solid because of delocalised electrons.

8.Water: Why Forces Between Molecules Matter

Water, H₂O, is a simple molecular substance, yet it boils at 100 °C, while methane, CH₄, a molecule of similar size, boils at about −162 °C. The reason is that the oxygen atom in water pulls the shared electrons towards itself, so the oxygen end of the molecule is slightly negative and the hydrogen ends are slightly positive. Neighbouring water molecules attract each other through these charges in a special strong type of intermolecular force called a hydrogen bond.

Hydrogen bonds hold ice molecules in an open pattern, which is why ice is less dense than liquid water and floats. They also help explain why water is a good solvent for many ionic compounds and why it takes a lot of heat to warm up. This shows that the forces between molecules can be very important, even when the bonds within molecules are covalent.

9.Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular substance melts. Only the weak forces between molecules are overcome.
  • Saying “ionic compounds contain molecules”. They have a lattice of ions.
  • Forgetting that ionic compounds conduct when molten or dissolved. The ions are free to move.
  • Drawing lone pairs or shared pairs incorrectly. Count the outer electrons of every atom.
  • Mixing up graphite and diamond properties. Graphite conducts because of free electrons; diamond does not.
  • Forgetting to balance charges in formulae. The overall charge must be zero.

10.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. What type of bonding is found in calcium oxide, and why?

Answer: Ionic: it is a metal combined with a non-metal, with electron transfer.

2. Write the formula of sodium oxide.

Answer: Na₂O (two Na⁺ for one O²⁻).

3. How many shared pairs are in a carbon dioxide molecule?

Answer: Four (two double bonds).

4. Why does solid sodium chloride not conduct, while molten sodium chloride does?

Answer: In the solid the ions are fixed; when molten they can move and carry charge.

5. Explain why graphite conducts electricity.

Answer: Each carbon uses three electrons in bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom free to move between layers.

6. Why is diamond very hard?

Answer: Each carbon atom forms four strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3-D network.

7. Why do simple molecular substances have low melting points?

Answer: Only weak forces between molecules need to be overcome.

11.Summary

  • Atoms bond to reach full outer shells, by transferring or sharing electrons.
  • Ionic bonding joins metals to non-metals through electron transfer; ions form a giant lattice.
  • Covalent bonding is the sharing of electron pairs between non-metal atoms; the molecules can be simple or giant.
  • Metallic bonding is a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.
  • Ionic compounds have high melting points and conduct when molten or dissolved.
  • Simple molecular substances have low melting points and do not conduct.
  • Always link properties to the structure, the particles and the forces involved.
For teachers and parents

Suggested use: have students build ionic lattices and molecules with modelling kits or marshmallows and toothpicks, and draw dot-and-cross diagrams for each molecule they make. Practise the three-part answer pattern for property questions until it is automatic. Parents can quiz students on formulae using cards of ions. Please follow your own syllabus for which diagrams (dot-and-cross, outer shells only) are expected.
