Formal vs. Informal English: The Complete Guide

The same idea can be said a dozen different ways — and choosing the right register is often what separates a message that lands well from one that doesn't.

"Can you help me out?" and "I would be grateful for your assistance" mean nearly the same thing. But you would not use the second one in a text to a friend, or the first one in a job application.

This difference is called register: the level of formality that a speaker or writer chooses to suit the situation, the audience and the purpose. Formal English is not "more correct" than informal English, and informal English is not "lazy". They are different tools, and a skilled writer can use both. This guide covers how register works, the features that make English formal or informal, how to show politeness, and how to rewrite a message for a different audience. It ends with worked examples, a checklist, a glossary and ten practice questions.

1.What Is Register?

Register is the variety of language that you choose for a particular situation. We shift register all the time without thinking. The same person may text a friend informally and, an hour later, write a formal email to a teacher.

Example

Informal (text to a friend): "Hey, can't make it tonight, sorry!" Formal (email to a teacher): "I am writing to let you know that I am unable to attend this evening. I apologise for any inconvenience."

The linguist M. A. K. Halliday described register as a variety of language that matches a variety of situation, and he described the situation using three features: field (what is happening), tenor (who is involved and how they relate) and mode (how the message is communicated, for example spoken or written). When you decide how formal to be, you are reading these three features.

2.A Scale, Not Two Boxes

Register is not a choice between only two options. It is a scale. In 1961 the American linguist Martin Joos described five styles of English, in an essay called The Five Clocks. Just as we change our clocks for different time zones, he said, we change our language for different situations. His five styles run from the most informal to the most formal: intimate (used between very close people), casual (friends and acquaintances), consultative (strangers, colleagues and meetings), formal (public and official situations) and frozen (fixed, ceremonial wording). Many classrooms use a simpler scale of three levels, formal, neutral and informal. Both are useful.

4.Features That Make English Formal or Informal

Register is shown by many features at once. No single feature decides it, but together they set the tone.

FeatureInformalFormal
Contractionsdon't, it's, can'tdo not, it is, cannot
Vocabularyfind out, get, kidsdiscover, obtain, children
Verbslook into, figure outinvestigate, determine
SentencesShort, with fragmentsComplete, often longer
Link wordsbut, so, alsohowever, therefore, in addition
Addressing the readeryouone, people, the reader
Punctuation!, ..., dashes, emojisFull stops, commas, few exclamation marks
Slang and fillerscool, kind of, you knowNone

Contractions

Informal English uses contractions freely. Formal writing usually avoids them: "I do not believe it will work, and we cannot fix it in time." Some modern business writing allows a few contractions, so follow the style guide of your course or workplace.

Vocabulary

Many everyday English words come from Old English (ask, buy, start, help), while many formal words came into English from French and Latin (enquire, purchase, commence, assist). This is why a longer, Latin-based word often sounds more formal. But longer is not always better. A clear, simple word in a formal tone often works best. See Synonyms and Antonyms for more on choosing between close words.

Phrasal verbs

Informal English relies on phrasal verbs ("find out", "put off", "look into"). Formal English often uses a single verb instead ("discover", "postpone", "investigate"). Not every phrasal verb is informal, though. "Carry out" and "set up" are common in formal writing. Avoid mainly the very casual ones.

Passive voice and nominalisation

Formal and scientific writing often uses the passive voice to focus on the action or the result rather than on the person: "The sample was tested three times." It also uses nominalisation, which means turning a verb into a noun: "They investigated the problem" becomes "The investigation of the problem was thorough". These choices can make writing sound objective, but too many make it heavy and hard to read. Many style guides advise the active voice when it is clearer, so use the passive when you have a reason.

Hedging

Formal and academic writing often uses careful words to avoid claiming too much: "may", "appears to", "it is likely that", "suggests". Informal speech is often more direct: "It will definitely work."

Link words and punctuation

Formal writing connects ideas with words such as "however", "therefore", "moreover" and "in addition". Remember that these words are adverbs, not conjunctions. They cannot join two sentences with only a comma. Use a full stop or a semicolon before them: "The plan was simple; however, it was costly." Formal writing also uses exclamation marks, capital letters for emphasis and emojis very rarely, if at all.

Slang, idioms and filler words

Slang ("that's lit"), casual idioms ("piece of cake") and filler words ("like", "kind of", "you know") belong mainly to informal English. Formal writing prefers precise, literal language: "This project proved relatively straightforward once it was under way." Not every idiom is informal, though. Phrases such as "on the other hand" are fine in formal writing.

5.Politeness and Indirectness

English does not have special words for "you" to show respect. It shows politeness in other ways, mainly through indirectness. The more distant or important the person, the more indirect and softened the request. Here is a ladder of requests, from direct to very polite.

LevelRequest
Very directSend me the file.
Direct, friendlyCan you send me the file?
PoliteCould you send me the file, please?
More politeWould you be able to send me the file?
Very politeI would be grateful if you could send me the file.

Learners who speak Hindi or Urdu already know that politeness matters, because these languages have different words for "you": aap (respectful), tum (familiar) and tu (very close or informal). English has only "you", so a speaker must show respect through word choice and structure instead. A direct request that would be normal in one language can sound sharp in English, and a very elaborate request may sound odd between friends. The right level depends on the relationship and the situation.

6.Greetings and Sign-Offs

SituationInformalFormal
GreetingHi, HeyDear Mr Khan, Dear Sir or Madam,
ClosingThanks!, CheersYours sincerely, Yours faithfully, Kind regards
RequestingCan you...?Would you be able to...?
ApologisingSorry about thatI apologise for the inconvenience

In traditional British letters, "Yours sincerely" follows "Dear" plus a name, and "Yours faithfully" follows "Dear Sir or Madam". Titles such as Mr, Ms and Dr are written without a full stop in British style.

7.Register, Tone and Culture

Register and tone are related but not the same. Register is the level of formality. Tone is the attitude, such as friendly, angry, serious or polite. You can write a formal letter in a calm tone or in an angry one. Writing that is formal and angry is usually less effective than writing that is formal and calm.

Norms also differ between cultures, workplaces and online communities. In some workplaces first names are normal even with managers, while in others titles are expected. A useful habit is to notice how the other person writes to you and to match their level, or to be slightly more formal if you are unsure. Being a little too formal is usually safer than being too casual in a first message.

8.Worked Examples

Example A: From informal to formal

The message. "Hi, my order still hasn't turned up, which is really annoying. Can you sort it out asap?"

1Identify the reader. A company's customer service team. Use a formal register.
2Change the greeting and the sign-off. "Dear Sir or Madam," and "Yours faithfully,".
3Remove contractions and slang. "hasn't turned up" becomes "has not arrived". "Asap" becomes "at your earliest convenience".
4Soften the request. "Can you sort it out" becomes "I would be grateful if you could resolve this".
5Keep the facts and a calm tone. Replace "really annoying" with a clear statement of the problem.

The formal version.

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to report that my order has not yet arrived. I placed it two weeks ago and have received no update.

I would be grateful if you could look into this matter and let me know the status of the delivery at your earliest convenience.

Yours faithfully,
Maria Chen

Try it yourself: make "Sorry, I can't come, I'm ill and can't get out of bed" suitable for an email to a teacher. Sample answer: Dear Mr Khan, I am sorry to say that I will be unable to attend school today because I am unwell. I will catch up on the work that I miss. Kind regards, (your name).

Example B: From formal to friendly

The notice. "Students are reminded that the library will be closed on Friday. Any books due on that date should be returned by Thursday."

1Identify the new reader. Students in a friendly message from a classmate.
2Use direct address and contractions. "you" instead of "students". "will be" becomes "will be" or "'ll be".
3Use active voice and everyday words. "Should be returned" becomes "bring them back".

The friendly version. "Hi everyone! Just a reminder that the library is closed on Friday. If you have books due that day, please bring them back by Thursday."

11.Common Myths About Register

MythFormal English is more correct than informal English.
FactBoth follow rules. They suit different situations. "Correct" depends on the situation.
MythInformal English is lazy or bad English.
FactInformal English is natural and skilful in its place. It would be wrong in a legal document, but just right with friends.
MythContractions are always wrong in writing.
FactThey are usually avoided in formal and academic writing, but they are normal in informal writing and in many friendly emails.
MythUsing longer words makes writing more formal and better.
FactClear, exact words matter more. Formal writing can use simple words.

12.Key Terms

Register: the level of formality of language, chosen to suit the situation.
Field, tenor, mode: Halliday's three features of a situation: what is happening, who is involved, and how the message is communicated.
Tone: the attitude of a piece of writing, such as polite or angry.
Contraction: a short form such as "don't".
Phrasal verb: a verb with a small word, such as "find out".
Passive voice: a sentence in which the subject receives the action, as in "The sample was tested".
Nominalisation: turning a verb into a noun, such as "investigate" into "investigation".
Hedging: using careful words, such as "may" or "appears", to avoid claiming too much.
Slang: very informal words used by particular groups.
Indirectness: softening a request to be more polite.

13.Why This Matters

Knowing grammar and vocabulary is not enough. Knowing which register fits which situation is what makes communication effective. A perfectly correct sentence can still feel wrong if it is too casual for a job application, or too stiff for a text to a friend. Mastering register turns correct English into appropriate English. It connects to Idioms and Phrases and to the stress and intonation work in Intonation in Conversation, because speakers show formality in how they sound as well as in what they say.

Key points to remember
  • Register is the level of formality, and it is a scale with many levels.
  • Field, tenor and mode (what, who and how) help you choose the right level.
  • Formal English usually avoids contractions, slang and very casual phrasal verbs, and softens requests.
  • The passive voice and long nouns are common in formal writing, but clarity comes first.
  • Match the greeting and the sign-off to the level, and keep the register steady.
  • Formal does not mean more correct, and informal does not mean wrong.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students one message, such as asking for a deadline extension, and ask them to write it for a friend, a teacher and an official. Then ask them to list the features that changed, and to say how they knew which level to choose.

Please note: examples are original, written for teaching. Joos's five styles and Halliday's three features are two well-known models, and teachers use other scales too. Norms vary between countries, schools and workplaces, so follow the style guide of your own course.
