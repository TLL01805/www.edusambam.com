EQ Practice Test: 24 Scenarios With Answers

Work through 24 everyday situations covering perceiving, using, understanding and managing emotions. Get instant, research-based explanations, then see which area to practise next.

Last updated: 15 October 2026. Written by the EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team. This tool is for general education and practice and does not replace advice from a qualified professional.

Knowing about emotional intelligence is one thing. Using it when a friend goes quiet, a message annoys you or a deadline piles up is another. This page gives you 24 everyday situations to practise on, grouped into the four areas of the Salovey and Mayer ability model: perceiving, using, understanding and managing emotions. Each answer comes with an explanation based on the research summarised in our articles. Unlike our EQ Self-Check, which asks how you see yourself, this practice test asks what you would do, which is closer to how real ability tests work. It is a learning tool, not a scientific test, and we explain below exactly why.

Key Takeaways

Ability-style EQ tests present situations and score the quality of the response, often against expert or group agreement. This practice test follows that idea but chooses its best answers from published research, such as asking rather than assuming, naming feelings, rethinking a situation instead of hiding feelings, and apologising with responsibility and repair. Your result is a practice score, not an EQ score. Real tests need pilot studies, reliability and validity checks and norm groups.

1.How This Test Works

  • 24 situations in four parts, six each for perceiving, using, understanding and managing emotions.
  • Tap one answer. You see straight away whether it was the best choice, which one was best, and the reasons with a source.
  • Answers are shuffled every time you start, so a second attempt is not just memory.
  • At the end you see your practice score, a bar for each part, and which area to practise next, with links.
  • Nothing is saved or sent.

3.How the Test Was Designed

We followed the same logic as ability-based EQ tests, in simplified form.

Design choiceWhat we didWhy
ModelUsed the four-branch ability model (Salovey and Mayer, 1990; Mayer and Salovey, 1997)It describes emotional intelligence as abilities that can be performed well or badly, which suits a test with better and worse answers
Item formatA short situation, a question and four answersSituational formats are common in ability and workplace tests, and they are close to real life
Keying (choosing the best answer)Chose best answers from published research (see Section 5), not from a poll of opinionsAgreement among people shows what is common, not necessarily what works
Wrong answersWrote distractors that reflect common mistakes (venting, hiding feelings, blaming, assuming)Wrong answers should be tempting and instructive, not silly
FeedbackGave the reason for every answer, with a sourceThis is a learning tool, and explanations teach more than a score
FairnessAvoided slang, kept situations common to school, home and friendships, and added a note that culture and context matterThe "best" response depends on context, so no test is perfectly culture-free

4.How Real Ability Tests Are Scored

The best-known ability test, the MSCEIT (Mayer, Salovey and Caruso), has 141 items in eight tasks that cover the four branches. Tasks include judging the emotions in faces and pictures, blending emotions, and choosing the most effective way to manage a feeling in a situation. Answers are scored against the agreement of a large sample (consensus scoring) and of expert judges (expert scoring). In one analysis, the authors reported a split-half reliability of about .93 and a test-retest reliability of about .86 for the full test (Mayer and colleagues, 2003).

Scoring against agreement has its critics. A response may be popular without being the most effective, and a high score may partly reflect knowing the conventional answer. Studies also show that ability EI is only weakly related to self-reported EI (Brackett and Mayer, 2003), and that its added value beyond intelligence and personality is small, though real (MacCann and colleagues, 2020). Because of these debates, an ability test is best seen as one piece of information.

6.Using the Test for Learning

WhoIdeas
StudentsTake the test, then write in your own words why each wrong choice is tempting. Retake it after a week and compare the explanations you can give, not just the score.
TeachersProject one situation at a time and have pairs choose an answer before revealing it. Ask: "What would you actually do?" and "What might make the second-best answer reasonable in a different situation?"
ParentsRead a situation aloud at dinner and discuss it. Avoid quizzing; ask open questions about what different people might feel.
AnyoneChoose one situation from the test that resembles something in your life and make an if-then plan for it.

Six discussion questions

  1. Which question did you find hardest, and what made the best answer less obvious?
  2. When have you reacted like one of the wrong choices? What would you do differently?
  3. Can you think of a situation where the second-best answer might be reasonable?
  4. How might family or cultural expectations change the best response?
  5. Which of the research findings surprised you?
  6. Which skill will you practise this week, and how?

7.Limits and Cautions

  • It is a practice tool. It cannot measure your real EQ, diagnose a condition or predict how you will behave.
  • Knowing the best answer is not the same as doing it. Skills grow through practice in real situations, ideally with feedback.
  • Context and culture matter. The best response in one family, school or country may differ in another.
  • Serious situations need real help. For bullying, violence, abuse or thoughts of self-harm, talk to a trusted adult, doctor or counsellor, or contact your local emergency number or crisis line.
  • Do not use any quiz to judge people. Researchers advise great caution about using EQ tests for hiring or school decisions.

8.The Answer Key

If you prefer to read the situations and best answers together, or to print them for class, open the key below.

Show the answer key and reasons (spoilers: try the test first)
#SituationBest responseWhy
1Your classmate Zainab has been quiet at lunch for three days. When you ask if she is okay, she says "I'm fine" in a flat voice and looks away.Kindly say that you have noticed she seems quiet, and that you are there if she wants to talk.Words and tone can disagree, and a flat voice could mean many things. In 25 experiments, getting the other person's perspective directly made people more accurate than imagining it (Eyal, Steffel and Epley, 2018). A gentle check-in with the door left open gives real information.
2Before a class presentation, you notice a tight chest, a dry mouth and a racing heart.Your body is in an alert state, probably nervousness or excitement, and naming it helps.A racing heart and tight chest are typical signs of arousal before a performance. Putting a feeling into words was linked with a weaker amygdala response (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007), and students told that nervous arousal can help performed better on a practice and real GRE (Jamieson and colleagues, 2010). If symptoms are severe or unusual, see a doctor.
3In a group chat, a friend replies only "ok." to your long message about weekend plans.Tone is hard to read in text; she may be busy, tired or upset, so check in another way if it matters.Tone is easily misread in writing. In experiments, senders expected to be understood about 78% of the time, but the actual rate for email was about 56%, close to chance (Kruger and colleagues, 2005).
4You see a classmate trip in front of everyone. His face turns red, he laughs nervously and avoids eye contact for the rest of the lesson.EmbarrassmentBlushing, nervous laughter and looking away are typical clues for self-conscious feelings such as embarrassment, especially after a public slip. They are clues, not proof, so a quiet, kind word later is better than assuming.
5Your brother comes home and goes straight to his room without greeting anyone.A combination of clues (what happened today, his posture and tone) and what he says if you ask.No single cue is reliable on its own, and facial or body signals depend on context (Barrett and colleagues, 2019). Combining clues with a direct, kind question is the most accurate approach.
6You feel irritated all afternoon after reading an online post.Pause and ask what the feeling is, what set it off, and whether tiredness, hunger or worry might be underneath.Naming a feeling precisely and looking for its cause is the core of emotional awareness. People who can tell their emotions apart in detail were less aggressive when angry (Pond and colleagues, 2012).
7It is the night before an exam and you feel anxious.Take it as a signal that the exam matters: list your three weakest topics, review them briefly, then sleep.Reading arousal as preparation, and turning worry into a short plan, uses the feeling as information. In studies, reappraising arousal as helpful improved performance and cardiovascular responses to stress (Jamieson and colleagues, 2010, 2012).
8A teammate's comment makes you very angry in the middle of a group project meeting.Notice that the anger signals something matters (such as fairness or respect), pause, then raise it calmly after the meeting.Anger can be useful information, but acting on it at its peak tends to backfire. Venting while ruminating increased anger and aggression, while doing nothing was better (Bushman, 2002).
9You must choose subjects for next year. You feel excited about one option and uneasy about another.Treat both as information: ask what they are telling you (interest, worry, missing facts), then check facts and talk to a trusted adult.Feelings are useful signals, not instructions. Asking what a feeling is about, and then checking facts, combines emotion with reasoning.
10You have no energy and do not feel like starting your homework.Start with a ten-minute "tiny start" and notice how action builds momentum.Motivation often follows action. If-then plans ("if it is 4 p.m., I will open my book for ten minutes") had a medium-to-large effect on goal achievement across 94 tests (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006).
11You win a competition. Your friend, who also entered, lost.Enjoy it, thank the people who helped, and check in kindly with your friend.Feelings of joy can be shared without ignoring other people's feelings. Noticing a friend's disappointment is empathy in action.
12A teacher gives tough feedback on your essay and you feel hurt.Name the hurt, take a short break, then reread the comments looking for specific changes to make.Feedback works best when attention goes to the task, not to the self (Kluger and DeNisi, 1996), and treating a failure with self-compassion increased the motivation to improve in experiments (Breines and Chen, 2012).
13After weeks of effort, Ali does not make the team.Disappointment, which can turn into anger or resentment if he keeps dwelling on the unfairness.Feelings change over time and thoughts feed them. Going over a problem again and again (rumination) keeps negative feelings alive (Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky, 2008).
14You are choosing a word to describe how you feel.Annoyed and furiousFeelings come in families and strengths. Using a milder word for a mild feeling and a stronger word for a strong one keeps communication accurate.
15A student feels both relieved and sad on the last day at her school.People can feel mixed emotions at the same time.Mixed feelings are normal. Naming both gives a more accurate picture than forcing a single word.
16Someone says something unkind, and later feels bad about the harm it caused a friend.GuiltGuilt usually focuses on a specific action that hurt someone, which is why it often leads to an apology and repair.
17Your friend has been irritable, snaps at everyone and has been sleeping badly.Tiredness and stress can make people more reactive, so her irritability may have causes beyond this situation.After a night without sleep, amygdala reactivity to negative images was over 60% greater in one study of 26 adults (Yoo and colleagues, 2007). Context such as sleep helps explain moods.
18You hear this advice: "Let it all out. Hit a pillow and your anger will go away."Venting while ruminating tends to increase anger and aggression; calming the body and rethinking the situation work better.In an experiment with about 600 students, hitting a punching bag while thinking about the person who angered them led to the most anger and aggression (Bushman, 2002).
19A classmate mocks you in front of others and you are furious.Breathe slowly with a long exhale, step away for a few minutes, then respond calmly or speak to a teacher.Slow breathing with a long exhale calmed people in a randomised study (Balban and colleagues, 2023), and people who habitually hide feelings (suppression) reported worse well-being than those who rethink the situation (Gross and John, 2003).
20You said something unkind and hurt your sister's feelings."I was wrong to say that, and I'm sorry I hurt you. How can I make it right?"In a study of 755 people, taking responsibility was the most important part of an apology, followed by an offer to repair (Lewicki, Polin and Lount, 2016).
21A friend is upset about a low mark and wants to talk.Listen, reflect what you hear ("You sound really disappointed"), and ask what would help.Listening and reflecting before advising helps the other person feel understood. In experiments, being listened to well reduced social anxiety and defensiveness (Itzchakov and colleagues, 2017, 2018).
22You need to tell a teammate that their part of the project is weak.Describe the specific issue and its effect, ask for their view, and agree a plan: "The summary has no sources, which weakens our grade. Can we add them together by Friday?"Feedback aimed at the task works better than feedback aimed at the person; in a large meta-analysis, more than one third of feedback interventions made performance worse (Kluger and DeNisi, 1996).
23You feel overwhelmed because three assignments are due on the same day.Slow your breathing, list each task with its next small step, plan time blocks, and ask for help or an extension if needed.Coping works best when it deals with the feeling (calming) and with the problem (planning), the distinction made by Lazarus and Folkman (1984).
24A message arrives that makes you angry.Wait, reread it, consider other explanations, and reply (or call) when calm.A short pause lets the thinking part of the brain catch up, and because tone is easily misread in text, other explanations are worth considering first (Kruger and colleagues, 2005).

9.Frequently Asked Questions

QuestionAnswer
Is this a real EQ test?No. It is a practice tool based on the ability-test idea. Real tests have norm groups and reliability and validity studies.
Why is there no EQ score?A meaningful score needs a norm group for comparison. Without one, a number would be invented.
Why are the answers always "the second most obvious"?They are not fixed: the order is shuffled each time, and the best answer is chosen from research.
What if I disagree with an answer?Good. Think about why, read the source named in the explanation, and discuss it with someone you trust. Some situations have more than one reasonable response.
Can I use it in class?Yes, for discussion and practice. Please do not use it to grade or label students.

A Closing Thought

A test can show you what to practise, but only real situations can build the skill. Next time a friend goes quiet, a message irritates you or a deadline closes in, try one move from these answers: ask instead of assuming, name the feeling, pause, rethink, listen, repair. Then notice what changes.
