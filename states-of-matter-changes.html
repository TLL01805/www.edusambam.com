States of Matter and Changes: Why Substances Transform

Ice, water and steam are the same substance in three different states. The particle model explains why, and how matter changes from one state to another.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 16 min read | Updated 21 October 2026

A puddle freezes, a pan of water boils dry, a bottle of perfume smells across the room. All of these are about the same idea: matter is made of tiny moving particles, and what they do decides whether a substance is a solid, a liquid or a gas. This article explains the particle model, what happens during changes of state, and how gases behave when you squeeze or heat them.

1.The Three States and the Particle Model

Everything around us is matter: it has mass and takes up space. Matter usually exists in three states: solid, liquid and gas. The particle model says that all matter is made of very small particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are always moving, with forces of attraction between them. The state depends on how strongly the particles are held together compared with how much energy they have.

Figure 1. The particle model. Solid particles vibrate in fixed positions, liquid particles slide past each other, and gas particles fly freely. Watch the animation: the solid shakes, the liquid wobbles, the gas moves widely.
PropertySolidLiquidGas
Particle arrangementRegular, tightly packedClose together, randomFar apart, random
Particle movementVibrate in fixed positionsMove around and slide past each otherMove quickly in all directions
Forces between particlesStrongWeakerAlmost none
ShapeFixedTakes the shape of its containerFills its container
VolumeFixedFixedChanges to fill the container
Compressible?NoAlmost notYes, easily
DensityUsually highUsually slightly less than the solidVery low

Water is an important exception: ice floats on liquid water because its particles are held in an open pattern, so ice is less dense than liquid water. This is why lakes freeze from the top down and fish can survive underneath.

2.Changes of State

A change of state is a physical change: the particles are the same, only their arrangement and energy change. No new substance is formed, and the change can be reversed.

ChangeFrom → ToEnergyExample
MeltingSolid → liquidTaken inIce melting in a cup
FreezingLiquid → solidGiven outWater becoming ice
BoilingLiquid → gas (throughout the liquid)Taken inWater boiling in a kettle
EvaporationLiquid → gas (at the surface only)Taken inPuddles drying
CondensationGas → liquidGiven outMist on a cold mirror
SublimationSolid → gas directlyTaken inDry ice (solid CO₂), iodine
DepositionGas → solid directlyGiven outFrost forming on a cold window

When a solid is heated, its particles vibrate more strongly. At the melting point they have enough energy to break free from their fixed positions and the solid becomes a liquid. At the boiling point the particles have enough energy to overcome the forces between them completely and form bubbles of gas inside the liquid.

Safety note

Dry ice is extremely cold (about −78 °C) and can cause burns, so handle it only with insulated gloves. Never seal it in a closed container or bottle, because as it turns to gas it can build up pressure and burst the container. Use it in a well-ventilated room.

3.The Heating Curve of Water

A heating curve shows temperature against the energy supplied. Look at the flat parts (plateaus): while a substance is melting or boiling, the temperature stays constant even though heat is still being supplied. The energy goes into breaking the forces between particles, not into making them move faster, so the temperature does not rise until the change is complete.

Figure 2. Heating curve for water. The temperature rises while ice, water and steam are being warmed, but stays at 0 °C during melting and at 100 °C during boiling (at normal pressure).

The boiling plateau is longer than the melting plateau because far more energy is needed to separate particles completely into a gas than to loosen them into a liquid. The same curve run backwards, as a substance cools, shows plateaus at the freezing and condensing points.

Worked example 1: heating water

Question: How much energy is needed to warm 200 g of water from 20 °C to 70 °C? (Specific heat capacity of water ≈ 4.2 J/(g·°C).)

  1. Use Q = m × c × ΔT.
  2. Temperature change ΔT = 70 − 20 = 50 °C.
  3. Q = 200 × 4.2 × 50 = 42 000 J = 42 kJ.

Answer: 42 kJ. This formula applies only while the temperature is changing, not during a change of state.

4.Melting and Boiling Points of Common Substances

Melting and boiling points tell you the state of a substance at a given temperature. The values below are rounded and refer to normal atmospheric pressure.

Worked example 2: deciding the state

Question: What is the state of ethanol at 90 °C, and of mercury at −50 °C?

  1. Ethanol boils at 78 °C. At 90 °C the temperature is above the boiling point, so it is a gas.
  2. Mercury melts at −39 °C. At −50 °C the temperature is below the melting point, so it is a solid.

Rule: below the melting point is solid; between melting and boiling points is liquid; above the boiling point is gas.

Pure substances have sharp melting and boiling points. If a substance melts over a range of temperatures, it is probably impure. Substances with strong forces between particles, such as ionic compounds and metals, have high melting points, while those held together by weak forces between small molecules, such as oxygen, have very low ones.

5.Evaporation and Boiling Compared

EvaporationBoiling
Where it happensAt the surface onlyThroughout the liquid (bubbles)
TemperatureAt any temperatureOnly at the boiling point
SpeedSlowFast
EnergyFastest particles escape, so the remaining liquid coolsHeat is supplied continuously

Evaporation is faster when the liquid is warmer, has a larger surface area, is in moving air (wind carries vapour away) and when the air is dry. Evaporation causes cooling because the particles with the most energy escape first; this is how sweating cools your body.

The boiling point depends on pressure. At high altitudes the air pressure is lower, so water boils at a lower temperature and food takes longer to cook. In a pressure cooker the pressure is higher, so water boils at a higher temperature and food cooks faster.

6.Diffusion

Diffusion is the movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, caused by random particle motion, until they are spread evenly. It explains why a smell spreads through a room, and why a drop of ink slowly colours a glass of water. Diffusion is fastest in gases, slower in liquids and very slow in solids.

Figure 3. A classic experiment. Cotton wool soaked in ammonia solution and another soaked in hydrochloric acid are placed at opposite ends of a dry glass tube. White ammonium chloride forms where the gases meet, nearer the hydrochloric acid end, showing that lighter ammonia particles diffuse faster.

The reaction is NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl. Because ammonia (relative molecular mass 17) is lighter than hydrogen chloride (36.5), its particles move faster on average, so they travel farther in the same time. In general, lighter particles diffuse faster at the same temperature, and higher temperature makes diffusion faster for all particles.

Safety note

Ammonia and hydrogen chloride fumes irritate the eyes, nose and lungs. This experiment is a teacher demonstration in a well-ventilated room or fume cupboard, with eye protection.

7.Gas Pressure and the Gas Laws

Gas pressure is caused by gas particles colliding with the walls of their container. More frequent or harder collisions mean higher pressure.

  • Heating a gas in a fixed volume makes the particles move faster, so they hit the walls harder and more often: pressure rises.
  • Squashing a gas into a smaller volume packs the particles closer, so they hit the walls more often: pressure rises.

Boyle’s law (constant temperature)

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure × volume stays constant:

p₁V₁ = p₂V₂
Worked example 3: compressing a gas

Question: A gas has a volume of 120 cm³ at 100 kPa. It is compressed to 40 cm³ at the same temperature. What is the new pressure?

  1. p₁V₁ = p₂V₂, so p₂ = (p₁ × V₁) ÷ V₂.
  2. p₂ = (100 × 120) ÷ 40 = 300 kPa.
  3. Check: volume is three times smaller, so pressure is three times bigger.

Charles’s law (constant pressure)

For a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure, volume is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvin. To convert, add 273: K = °C + 273 (absolute zero is −273 °C, or 0 K).

V₁ ÷ T₁ = V₂ ÷ T₂
Worked example 4: warming a gas

Question: A balloon has volume 600 cm³ at 27 °C. What is its volume at 57 °C at constant pressure?

  1. Convert to kelvin: T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K; T₂ = 57 + 273 = 330 K.
  2. V₂ = V₁ × T₂ ÷ T₁ = 600 × 330 ÷ 300 = 660 cm³.

Always use kelvin, never °C, in gas-law calculations.

8.Beyond Solid, Liquid and Gas

Plasma is often called the fourth state of matter. It is a very hot gas whose atoms have lost electrons, so it contains charged particles. Plasma is found in lightning, the Sun and other stars, neon signs and fluorescent lamps. Some other special states exist at extreme temperatures in laboratories, but they are beyond school level.

9.Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Saying particles “expand” when heated. Particles stay the same size; they move faster and spread farther apart.
  • Thinking the temperature rises during melting or boiling. It stays constant while the state changes.
  • Mixing up evaporation and boiling. Evaporation is at the surface at any temperature; boiling is throughout the liquid at the boiling point.
  • Using °C in gas-law calculations. Convert to kelvin first.
  • Confusing a change of state with a chemical reaction. Melting and boiling are physical changes and are reversible.
  • Drawing solid particles far apart. They are tightly packed in a regular pattern.

10.Check Your Understanding

Try each question first, then tap it to reveal the answer.

1. Describe the movement of particles in a gas.

Answer: They move quickly and randomly in all directions, with almost no forces between them.

2. Why does the temperature stay constant while ice melts?

Answer: The energy supplied is used to break the forces holding the particles in place, not to increase their speed.

3. What is the name for the change solid → gas directly?

Answer: Sublimation.

4. Why does sweating cool the body?

Answer: The most energetic particles evaporate from the skin, taking energy with them so the remaining liquid cools.

5. Which diffuses faster: hydrogen (Mr 2) or oxygen (Mr 32)?

Answer: Hydrogen, because its particles are lighter.

6. A gas at 50 kPa and 200 cm³ is compressed to 100 cm³ at constant temperature. What is the new pressure?

Answer: p₂ = 50 × 200 ÷ 100 = 100 kPa.

7. Convert 25 °C to kelvin.

Answer: 25 + 273 = 298 K.

11.Summary

  • Matter is made of moving particles; the three states differ in arrangement, movement and forces.
  • Changes of state are physical and reversible: melting, freezing, boiling, evaporation, condensation, sublimation, deposition.
  • During a change of state the temperature stays constant because energy breaks or forms forces between particles.
  • Evaporation occurs at the surface at any temperature; boiling occurs throughout at the boiling point.
  • Diffusion is faster for lighter particles and at higher temperature.
  • Gas pressure comes from particle collisions; Boyle’s law (p₁V₁ = p₂V₂) and Charles’s law (V/T constant, in kelvin).
  • Pure substances have sharp melting and boiling points.
For teachers and parents

Suggested use: ask students to act out the three states in the classroom (tightly linked, sliding, moving freely), then record a cooling curve for water or stearic acid with a thermometer and sketch the plateau. Heating and any experiments with hot water or chemicals should be supervised. Parents can use kitchen examples such as steam, ice cubes and perfume. Please follow your own syllabus for required notation and gas-law content.
