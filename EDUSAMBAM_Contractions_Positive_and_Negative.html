Contractions in English: Positive and Negative Contractions

A complete guide to English contractions, including positive and negative forms, apostrophe rules, contractions with be, have, will and would, common confusing pairs, formal and informal use, examples, practice and a 15-question self-assessment.

Article purpose: Contractions are shortened forms of two words that are joined with an apostrophe. They are a normal and important part of spoken English and are also common in informal writing. This article teaches you how positive and negative contractions are formed, what they mean, how to recognise them in longer sentences, when to use full forms instead, and how to avoid the mistakes that commonly confuse learners.

1. What Is a Contraction?

A contraction is a shortened form of two words. One or more letters are omitted, and an apostrophe (’) shows where the missing letters are.

EDUSAMBAM principle

TWO WORDS → SHORTER FORM
I am → I’m
They are → They’re
Do not → Don’t

Contractions usually keep the same basic meaning as the full forms. The difference is mainly one of form, rhythm and register. Compare:

  • I am ready. → I’m ready.
  • We will leave soon. → We’ll leave soon.
  • She is not tired. → She isn’t tired.

English uses contractions very frequently in natural conversation. They help speech flow smoothly and often make writing sound more natural when the situation is informal.

2. Positive and Negative Contractions

For this lesson, it is useful to divide contractions into two broad groups.

Positive contractions

They do not contain not.
I’m, you’re, he’s, we’ve, they’ll, I’d

Negative contractions

They usually combine a verb with not.
isn’t, don’t, didn’t, can’t, won’t, shouldn’t

The word positive here does not mean “good”, and negative does not mean “bad”. It simply identifies whether the contraction contains a negative word such as not.

3. Positive Contractions with Be

Forms of be are among the most common words used in positive contractions.

Full formContractionExample
I amI’mI’m ready.
you areyou’reYou’re welcome.
he ishe’sHe’s tired.
she isshe’sShe’s a teacher.
it isit’sIt’s cold today.
we arewe’reWe’re ready.
they arethey’reThey’re outside.
Notice the apostrophe

The apostrophe is not decoration. It represents letters that have been left out. you’re contains an apostrophe because it is a contraction of you are.

4. Positive Contractions with Have and Has

When have or has is an auxiliary verb, it can form contractions with pronouns.

Full formContractionExample
I haveI’veI’ve finished my work.
you haveyou’veYou’ve done well.
we havewe’veWe’ve arrived.
they havethey’veThey’ve seen the film.
he hashe’sHe’s finished the task.
she hasshe’sShe’s gone home.
it hasit’sIt’s been a long day.

This creates an important ambiguity: he’s, she’s and it’s can represent either is or has. You must use the words around the contraction to determine the meaning.

Compare

He’s happy. = He is happy.
He’s finished. = He has finished.
She’s a doctor. = She is a doctor.
She’s left. = She has left.

5. Positive Contractions with Will

Future meaning is often expressed with contractions of a pronoun and will.

Full formContractionExample
I willI’llI’ll call you later.
you willyou’llYou’ll enjoy the lesson.
he willhe’llHe’ll help us.
she willshe’llShe’ll arrive soon.
it willit’llIt’ll be fine.
we willwe’llWe’ll start at six.
they willthey’llThey’ll come tomorrow.

These forms are particularly common in conversation, predictions, promises, offers and quick decisions.

6. Positive Contractions with Would and Had

The contraction ’d is especially important because it can represent either would or had.

ContractionPossible full formExample
I’dI would / I hadI’d like some tea. / I’d already eaten.
you’dyou would / you hadYou’d enjoy it. / You’d left before me.
he’dhe would / he hadHe’d help us. / He’d gone home.
she’dshe would / she hadShe’d agree. / She’d finished.
we’dwe would / we hadWe’d stay. / We’d arrived.
they’dthey would / they hadThey’d come. / They’d left.
A reliable clue

After ’d, a past participle often points to had: I’d finished = I had finished. A base verb often points to would: I’d finish = I would finish.

7. Negative Contractions: The Main Pattern

Negative contractions commonly combine an auxiliary or modal verb with not.

Full formContractionExample
is notisn’tHe isn’t ready.
are notaren’tThey aren’t here.
was notwasn’tShe wasn’t late.
were notweren’tWe weren’t tired.
do notdon’tI don’t know.
does notdoesn’tHe doesn’t agree.
did notdidn’tThey didn’t come.
have nothaven’tWe haven’t finished.
has nothasn’tShe hasn’t called.
had nothadn’tI hadn’t seen it.

8. Negative Contractions with Modal Verbs

Modal verbs also form very common negative contractions.

Full formContractionExample
will notwon’tWe won’t be late.
would notwouldn’tHe wouldn’t listen.
cannotcan’tI can’t swim.
could notcouldn’tShe couldn’t open it.
should notshouldn’tYou shouldn’t worry.
must notmustn’tYou mustn’t touch that.
need notneedn’tYou needn’t hurry.
might notmightn’tHe mightn’t come.

9. The Special Contraction Won’t

One contraction must be memorised because it does not look like the full form: will not → won’t.

Do not build it mechanically

Correct: will not → won’t
Incorrect: willn’t

Won’t is a fixed standard contraction. Learn it as a vocabulary item rather than trying to create it by simply attaching n’t to will.

10. Can’t, Cannot and Can not

The ordinary negative of can is written as cannot in the full form and can’t in the contraction.

Standard

I cannot drive.
I can’t drive.

Special construction

Can not may appear when not belongs to a separate contrast or choice, but it is not the normal spelling of the ordinary negative.

For ordinary school writing, the safe rule is: cannot or can’t.

11. Contractions in Questions

Contractions are extremely natural in questions, particularly in conversation.

  • Isn’t he coming?
  • Don’t you understand?
  • Haven’t they arrived?
  • Wouldn’t you like to come?
  • Can’t we try again?

There is one special question form worth remembering:

Aren’t I?

I’m late, aren’t I? is the standard form in ordinary English for Am I not? The form amn’t I? is not standard in most varieties of English.

12. Contractions in Longer Sentences

A contraction can occur anywhere a suitable full form could occur, including sentences with several clauses.

Example 1

If you’re ready, we’ll leave when everyone’s finished.
If you are ready, we will leave when everyone is finished.

Example 2

She hasn’t called because she’s been busy, but she’ll contact us tomorrow.
She has not called because she has been busy, but she will contact us tomorrow.

Example 3

I’d have helped you if I’d known you couldn’t come.
The first I’d means I would; the second I’d means I had.

13. Contractions in Direct and Indirect Speech

Contractions are especially common in direct speech because direct speech often represents natural spoken language. They can also remain in informal reported speech, although a formal style may prefer full forms.

Direct → Indirect

Direct: Ali said, “I’m tired, but I’ll finish it.”
Indirect: Ali said that he was tired but that he would finish it.

Another example

Direct: Sara said, “I haven’t seen him.”
Indirect: Sara said that she hadn’t seen him.

Remember that changing direct speech into indirect speech can require changes to pronouns, tense, time expressions and other words. A contraction is only one part of the sentence.

14. Formal and Informal English

Contractions are not incorrect English. They are a normal feature of the language. The important issue is whether they suit the audience, purpose and level of formality.

SituationTypical choiceExample
ConversationContractions are very naturalI’m sure you’ll understand.
Friendly messageContractions are commonWe’re waiting for you.
Dialogue in fictionContractions can make speech realistic“I can’t stay.”
Formal reportFull forms may sound more formalWe are pleased to announce…
Academic writingFollow the required styleUse the form expected by the institution.
EDUSAMBAM rule

Do not think “contraction = wrong”. Think: “Does this form fit my purpose and audience?”

15. Commonly Confused Contractions

Some errors occur because a contraction looks or sounds like another word.

ContractionMeaningDo not confuse it with
it’sit is / it hasits = possession
you’reyou areyour = possession
they’rethey aretheir = possession; there = place/existence
who’swho is / who haswhose = possession
we’rewe arewere = past form of be
they’dthey would / they hadthey did is not the same contraction

16. Common Mistakes and How to Correct Them

Common mistake
Mistake 1: “Your welcome.”
Correct: You’re welcome. = You are welcome.
Common mistake
Mistake 2: “Its raining.”
Correct: It’s raining. = It is raining.
Common mistake
Mistake 3: “They’re books are new.”
Correct: Their books are new. Here their shows possession.
Common mistake
Mistake 4: “I could of helped.”
Correct: I could have helped. In speech, could’ve is the contraction of could have.
Common mistake
Mistake 5: “Willn’t you come?”
Correct: Won’t you come?
Common mistake
Mistake 6: using an apostrophe for a plural.
Correct: Students means more than one student. Student’s normally means possession or is a contraction in a different context.

17. Contractions with Have: A Useful Pronunciation Warning

In natural speech, contractions such as could’ve, should’ve, would’ve may sound very different from their spelling. This can cause learners to write could of, should of or would of.

Remember the grammar

could’ve = could have
should’ve = should have
would’ve = would have
The word of is not the correct full form.

18. How to Decode Any Contraction

When you meet an unfamiliar contraction, use this four-step method.

  1. Look at the apostrophe. Identify the shortened part.
  2. Check the words after the contraction. They often reveal whether the missing word is is, has, would or had.
  3. Expand it mentally. Replace the contraction with the likely full form.
  4. Check the meaning. The expanded sentence must make grammatical and logical sense.
Try the method

She’d already left.
1. See ’d.
2. The next word is already and the verb is left (past participle).
3. Expand: She had already left.
4. The sentence makes sense. Therefore, she’d = she had.

21. Guided Practice

A. Expand the contractions
  1. I’m ready.
  2. They’ve finished.
  3. She won’t agree.
  4. We weren’t late.
  5. He’d already left.
  6. You shouldn’t worry.

Answers: 1. I am ready. 2. They have finished. 3. She will not agree. 4. We were not late. 5. He had already left. 6. You should not worry.

B. Contract the sentences
  1. I am certain.
  2. We are waiting.
  3. She has finished.
  4. They will come.
  5. He would help.
  6. Do not touch that.
  7. She cannot drive.
  8. They have not arrived.

Answers: 1. I’m certain. 2. We’re waiting. 3. She’s finished. 4. They’ll come. 5. He’d help. 6. Don’t touch that. 7. She can’t drive. 8. They haven’t arrived.

C. Choose the correct form
  1. Its / It’s raining heavily.
  2. Your / You’re very helpful.
  3. Their / They’re waiting outside.
  4. I would / had like some water. → I’d like some water.

Answers: 1. It’s 2. You’re 3. They’re 4. would.

22. Final Memory Map

Remember

CONTRACTION: a shorter form made from two words, marked by an apostrophe.

POSITIVE: I’m, you’re, he’s, we’ve, they’ll, I’d.

NEGATIVE: isn’t, don’t, didn’t, haven’t, won’t, can’t, shouldn’t.

AMBIGUOUS: he’s/she’s/it’s can mean is or has; I’d/you’d/he’d etc. can mean would or had.

SPECIAL: will not → won’t; cannot → can’t.

REGISTER: contractions are natural in speech and informal writing; full forms may be preferred in more formal contexts.

METHOD: expand → check grammar → check meaning → check context.
