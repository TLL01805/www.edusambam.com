The Importance of the English Language

A language can be important in many different ways at once — to a nation, to an individual, to a field of study, or to a single conversation between two strangers who share no other common tongue.

A language can be important in many different ways at once: to a nation, to an individual, to a field of study, or to a single conversation between two strangers who share no other tongue. English, more than almost any other language today, plays several of these roles at the same time.

This article looks at why English carries the weight it does, using evidence you can check, not slogans. It covers English as a gateway to education, employment and information, what the numbers actually say about the web, science and wages, and why a balanced view matters: English opens doors, but it is not a substitute for education, and it does not make any other language less valuable. It also shows how to turn "I need English" into a practical plan. It includes two worked examples, a checklist, common mistakes and ten practice questions.

It follows English as an International Language and Language and Its Need, and leads into How to Write a Perfect Paragraph.

1.A Gateway Rather Than a Destination

It is useful to think of English not as a subject to be mastered for its own sake, but as a gateway that opens access to things a person actually wants: a course of study, a job, a piece of research, a conversation with someone from another country. This is why English so often appears as a requirement in university admissions, job advertisements and visa applications. It works like a practical key.

Notice what this does not say. A key is valuable only if there is a door you want to open, and it does nothing without the rest of your preparation. The sections below show what the evidence says about each door.

2.What the Evidence Says

Strong claims about English are easy to find and often exaggerated. Here is what can be checked.

On the web: about half, and not growing

W3Techs, a company that measures web technologies, reports that in mid-August 2026 English was used by about 49.5 percent of the websites whose content language it could identify. Spanish came next at about 6.0 percent, followed by German (5.9), Japanese (4.9), French (4.5), Portuguese (4.1) and Russian (3.4). Their yearly table shows English was higher in earlier years (for example 63.6 percent in January 2022) and has been close to 49 to 50 percent since January 2025.

Content languages of websites
Share of sites with a known language, mid-August 2026 (W3Techs).

Diagram 1. English is the largest single language online, but about half of the web is in other languages. Source: W3Techs, 15 August 2026.

So the accurate statement is "about half", not "almost everything". It also matters that the measurement covers sites with an identifiable language, and counts sites, not how many people read them.

In science: overwhelmingly English, with real costs

In the databases that index research, English is dominant. A 2025 analysis of scholarly publishing found that English-language papers grew from about 877,000 in 1990 to about 5.0 million in 2023, and that around 98.9 percent of the references cited in its corpus point to English-language documents. Even in the social sciences and humanities, where English is lowest, about 91 percent of publications were in English. The same analysis notes that other languages are gaining visibility, so the picture is slowly becoming more multilingual.

For researchers whose first language is not English, this has a price. A 2020 study of Colombian biology researchers found that more than 90 percent of their published articles were in English, and that publishing in a second language created extra costs, reading and writing difficulties and anxiety. The author argues for keeping a common language while also making room for other languages. This is a balanced reading: English gives access to the world's research, and it also asks more of people who must learn it.

In pay: a real premium, linked to education

A well-known study of India (Azam, Chin and Prakash, 2013) used a national survey from 2005. After controlling for age, social group, schooling, geography and proxies for ability, it found that men who spoke fluent English earned about 34 percent more per hour than men who spoke none, and men who spoke a little English about 13 percent more. The authors note that the return to fluent English was about as large as the return to completing secondary school, and about half the return to a bachelor's degree.

English skills and hourly wages
Men in India, 2005 survey, compared with men with no English.

Diagram 2. Source: Azam, Chin and Prakash (2013), after controlling for other factors.

The details matter. The authors found that the premium grew with experience and education, and that among younger workers only the more educated earned a premium. A later summary of the study warned that teaching English to adults who lack education may not by itself raise wages. In other words, English pays most when it is added to education, not used instead of it. This is one study of one country, so treat it as strong evidence about that setting and a useful hint elsewhere.

In school: learn first in the language you understand

UNESCO's guidance is clear that children learn best when early schooling uses their mother tongue, and it recommends mother tongue-based bilingual or multilingual education: strong learning in the home language alongside additional languages. It also reports, citing UNESCO's 2016 monitoring report, that almost 40 percent of children do not have access to education in a language they understand. So the best case for English is not "English instead of your language" but "English as a strong addition to it".

3.The Five Doors, Revisited

Seen against that evidence, the common claims about English become more precise.

DoorWhat English can addHonest limit
EducationDirect access to textbooks, lectures and research written in English, and to study abroadMany courses and exams are in other languages; early learning is best in the home language
EmploymentAccess to multinational companies, remote and freelance work, tourism and tradeThe wage premium is larger with education and experience; English alone is not enough
InformationReading original sources, technical documentation and software without waiting for translationAbout half of the web is in other languages; English text is not automatically accurate
Independent thinkingComparing reports and arguments from several countries directlyIt also needs critical skills: check who wrote it, why and with what evidence
ConfidenceFewer barriers when travelling, reading manuals or speaking with visitorsYou do not need native-like fluency to benefit: reading, basic conversation and short writing already help

English at work: three common roles

  • Multinational companies often use English as an internal language when staff, clients and partners speak many different mother tongues.
  • Remote and freelance work frequently connects people who have no other language in common. A designer, programmer or writer can reach a wider set of clients.
  • Customer-facing industries such as tourism, hospitality and trade depend on staff who can communicate clearly with visitors and partners.

English and independent thinking: a caution

Being able to read sources from several countries directly is valuable, but a source is not reliable simply because it is in English, or because it is foreign. Good readers ask the same questions in any language: who wrote this, what is their evidence, and who might disagree? See Evidence, Citations & Plagiarism.

4.A Balanced Perspective

None of this suggests that English proficiency alone determines success, or that any native language is less valuable or less capable of expressing complex ideas. Every language carries the full capacity for precise thought, literature and human connection. Thoughtful people raise real concerns, and a fair account includes them.

What English offersWhat to keep in mind
Wider access to education, work and informationAccess to good English teaching is unequal, so English can widen gaps as well as narrow them
A shared tool for working across bordersNon-native speakers often face extra cost and effort, as the research example shows
Confidence in international situationsHome languages carry identity, knowledge and learning, and need protection (see Language and Its Need)

Researchers describe two possible paths. In additive bilingualism, a second language is added to a rich first language, which builds strong knowledge in both. In subtractive bilingualism, the second language begins to replace the first. The goal for learners, families and schools is the additive path: grow English and keep your own language strong.

5.From "I Need English" to a Plan

The word "English" is too broad to study. Start with the door you want to open.

6.Real-World Example

Consider an invented nursing graduate in a country where English is not the main language. With strong reading skills in English, she can follow current clinical guidelines and research as soon as they are published, apply for postgraduate study abroad, speak with international colleagues and visiting specialists, and use training materials before local translations appear. She keeps her own language for her patients, family and community. English gives her a second set of doors without taking away the first.

Now consider a second learner who learns English words but has not finished secondary school. The wage study suggests that his English alone may bring a smaller return than hers would, because the premium tends to be larger when English is combined with education. His best plan is to keep studying and improve English alongside, not instead.

7.Worked Examples

Example A: Turning a goal into a plan

Goal: apply for a master's course abroad in two years.

1Check the requirements. Find the university's English requirement and any accepted tests, and the date by which you must show it.
2List the skills. Reading academic articles, writing essays and reports, understanding lectures, speaking in seminars.
3Find your weakest skill. Try a sample task and note where you slow down or lose marks.
4Set a weekly routine. For example, three short reading sessions, two writing tasks, and ten minutes of listening and speaking on most days.
5Check progress monthly. Record a short talk, write a paragraph on the same topic, and compare with last month.

What this shows. A clear goal turns "learn English" into a few specific habits. It also reminds you that the plan is part of a bigger one, which includes your subject knowledge and application documents.

Example B: Fact-checking a claim about English

Claim: "Almost everything on the internet is in English."

1Say precisely what is claimed. That English is used by "almost all" websites.
2Find a measurement and its source. W3Techs, August 2026: English about 49.5 percent of sites with a known content language.
3Compare. "About half" is far from "almost everything".
4Note the limits. It counts websites, not readers, and only those with an identifiable language.

Better claim: "English is the largest single language on the web, used by about half of websites with an identifiable language, but many other languages are widely used."

Try it yourself. Fact-check: "English is the language with the most native speakers."

Sample answer: This is not accurate. English has the most speakers in total when second-language speakers are counted, but Mandarin Chinese has more first-language (native) speakers. See English as an International Language for the figures and their source.

10.Common Myths

MythLearning English means giving up your own language.
FactResearch and UNESCO guidance support learning in the home language alongside additional languages.
MythFluent English guarantees a higher income.
FactStudies find a premium in some settings, but it is larger with education and experience, and it is not guaranteed.
MythScience is only done in English.
FactMost indexed research is in English, but other languages are growing in visibility and local journals matter.
MythYou need a native-like accent to benefit.
FactClear, understandable English is the target; reading and basic conversation already open doors.

11.Key Terms

Gateway: something that gives access to other opportunities.
Content language: the natural language of the text on a website.
Wage premium: the extra pay linked with a skill, after other factors are controlled.
Mother tongue-based education: teaching in the learner's first language, with additional languages added.
Additive bilingualism: adding a second language to a strong first language.
Subtractive bilingualism: a second language replacing the first.
Multilingualism: using more than one language.
Primary source: original material, such as a research paper or official document.

12.Why This Matters

Understanding why English matters, and what the evidence says about each claim, helps you decide how much effort to give it, what to use it for and what not to expect from it. Treat English as a valuable skill you build on top of your education and alongside your own language. English is important not because it is the most beautiful, the oldest or the most logical of languages. It is important because of the doors it can open, the connections it enables and the knowledge it makes accessible. Continue with How to Write a Perfect Paragraph.

Key points to remember
  • English is best seen as a gateway to education, work and information, not a goal in itself.
  • About half of websites with a known language use English; the rest of the web is multilingual.
  • Most indexed research is in English, which helps access and adds costs for non-native researchers.
  • One major study in India linked fluent English with higher wages, mostly in combination with education.
  • UNESCO recommends learning in the home language alongside additional languages.
  • Start from a specific goal and practise the skills it needs.
  • Keep your home language strong, and check evidence in every language.
For teachers and parents

Activity: ask students to choose one future goal and list the exact English skills it needs, then compare with a partner. Follow with a fact-checking task: give them three claims about English and ask them to find a source, a date and a limit for each.

Please note: the statistics in this article come from named sources and years, and they change over time, so students should check the latest figures. The wage study is from one country and one year, and it describes an association controlled for other factors, not a guarantee. The nursing graduate and the second learner are invented examples.
