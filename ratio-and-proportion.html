Ratio & Proportion: Complete Guide with All Question Types

A ratio compares two quantities and a proportion says two ratios are equal. Between them they cover recipes, maps, speeds, mixing, sharing and scaling.

Ratios and proportions are how mathematics compares quantities without needing to know their exact sizes. A recipe, a map, a paint mix, a speed and a currency exchange are all ratio problems in disguise. This guide builds from the simplest ratio through sharing, direct and inverse proportion, and three-term ratios, with a worked example and a real-life use for every type.

1.What Is a Ratio?

A ratio compares two (or more) quantities of the same kind. It is written with a colon, a : b, and read “a to b”. A ratio tells you how many times bigger one quantity is than another, or how the quantities are shared.

Example 1

A fruit bowl has 4 apples and 6 oranges. The ratio of apples to oranges is 4 : 6. Order matters: the ratio of oranges to apples is 6 : 4, which is a different statement.

When the two quantities have different units (for example 150 km in 3 hours), the comparison is called a rate, such as 50 km per hour. Ratios compare like with like, and rates compare unlike things.

2.Simplifying Ratios

Just like fractions, ratios simplify by dividing every term by their common factors, ideally the HCF. A ratio is in its simplest form when the terms share no factor except 1.

Example 2

(a) 12 : 18. HCF(12, 18) = 6. Dividing both by 6 gives 2 : 3.

(b) Three terms: 24 : 36 : 60. HCF = 12, so the simplest form is 2 : 3 : 5.

Ratios with different units, fractions or decimals

  • Same units first. 50 cm : 2 m must become 50 cm : 200 cm = 50 : 200 = 1 : 4.
  • Fractions: multiply every term by the common denominator. 1/2 : 1/3, multiplied by 6, becomes 3 : 2.
  • Decimals: multiply by 10, 100 and so on to clear the point. 0.6 : 1.5 becomes 6 : 15, which simplifies to 2 : 5.

The form 1 : n

To compare ratios easily, it helps to make one term equal to 1. Divide both terms by the first: 4 : 10 = 1 : 2.5. Or by the second: 5 : 2 = 2.5 : 1.

3.Equivalent Ratios

Multiplying or dividing every term of a ratio by the same number never changes what it represents, exactly like equivalent fractions.

Example 3

2 : 3 = 4 : 6 = 6 : 9 = 20 : 30. All of these describe the same comparison. A table of equivalent ratios is a good way to solve many problems: write the ratio, then keep multiplying until you reach the number you need.

4.Part-to-Part vs. Part-to-Whole Ratios

This distinction trips up more students than any other ratio idea. A ratio can compare one part to another part, or one part to the whole total, and the two give different numbers.

Example 4

A class has 12 boys and 18 girls (30 students in all). Boys to girls (part to part) = 12 : 18 = 2 : 3. Boys to the whole class (part to whole) = 12 : 30 = 2 : 5.

“Boys are 2/3 of the class, since boys : girls = 2 : 3”

A part-to-part ratio is not the same as a part-to-whole fraction.

The fix: boys : girls = 2 : 3 means that for every 2 boys there are 3 girls, which makes 5 parts in total. Boys are 2 out of those 5 parts, so boys are 2/5 of the class (40%), not 2/3. The 3 in a part-to-part ratio refers to the other part, not to the whole.

Ratios, fractions and percentages are linked. A ratio of 3 : 7 splits the whole into 10 parts, so the two parts are 3/10 = 30% and 7/10 = 70% of the whole. (See Percentages.)

5.Dividing a Quantity in a Given Ratio

To share a total in a ratio: add up the parts, find the value of one part, then multiply back out.

A bar model makes the steps visible. The first two blocks glow gently.
Example 5: Two-Part Ratio

Divide Rs 60 between two people in the ratio 2 : 3. Total parts = 2 + 3 = 5. One part = 60 ÷ 5 = Rs 12. Shares: 2 × 12 = Rs 24 and 3 × 12 = Rs 36. Check: 24 + 36 = 60. ✓

Example 6: Three-Part Ratio

Divide 180 sweets among three children in the ratio 2 : 3 : 4. Total parts = 9. One part = 180 ÷ 9 = 20. Shares: 40, 60 and 80 sweets. Check: 40 + 60 + 80 = 180. ✓

When you are given a different clue

Example 7: Given the Difference

Two people share money in the ratio 7 : 4, and one gets Rs 3,600 more than the other. The difference is 7 − 4 = 3 parts, so 3 parts = Rs 3,600 and 1 part = Rs 1,200. The shares are 7 × 1,200 = Rs 8,400 and 4 × 1,200 = Rs 4,800.

Example 8: Given One Share

Money is shared in the ratio 3 : 5, and the larger share is Rs 4,000. The larger share is 5 parts, so 1 part = Rs 800. The smaller share is 3 × 800 = Rs 2,400, and the total is 8 × 800 = Rs 6,400.

Example 9: Changing a Ratio

In a class, boys : girls = 3 : 2, and there are 24 boys. One part = 24 ÷ 3 = 8, so there are 2 × 8 = 16 girls. If 8 more girls join, there are 24 girls and the ratio becomes 24 : 24 = 1 : 1.

Example 10: Combining Two Ratios

A : B = 2 : 3 and B : C = 4 : 5. Find A : B : C. Make the B terms equal using the LCM of 3 and 4, which is 12. A : B = 8 : 12 and B : C = 12 : 15. So A : B : C = 8 : 12 : 15.

6.What Is a Proportion?

A proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal: a : b = c : d. When four numbers are in proportion, cross-multiplying gives a × d = b × c. That is the basis for finding any missing value.

7.Solving Proportions by Cross-Multiplication

Example 11

Solve x : 5 = 12 : 15. Cross-multiply: x × 15 = 5 × 12 = 60. So x = 60 ÷ 15 = 4. Check: 4 : 5 and 12 : 15 both simplify to 4 : 5. ✓

8.Direct Proportion

Two quantities are in direct proportion when they increase or decrease together at the same rate. Double one and the other doubles too. Their ratio stays constant, and a graph of one against the other is a straight line through the origin (0, 0).

Example 12: The Unitary Method

5 pens cost Rs 20. How much do 8 pens cost? One pen costs 20 ÷ 5 = Rs 4. So 8 pens cost 4 × 8 = Rs 32.

Example 13: Eggs

12 eggs cost Rs 240. How much do 18 eggs cost? One egg costs 240 ÷ 12 = Rs 20, so 18 eggs cost 18 × 20 = Rs 360.

9.Inverse Proportion

Two quantities are in inverse proportion when one increases as the other decreases in such a way that their product stays constant. A graph of one against the other is a curve, not a straight line.

Example 14

4 workers build a wall in 12 days. How many days would 6 workers take? The total work stays constant: 4 × 12 = 48 “worker-days”. With 6 workers: 48 ÷ 6 = 8 days.

Example 15: Pipes and Speeds

(a) 2 identical pipes fill a tank in 6 hours. 3 pipes take 12 ÷ 3 = 4 hours, because 2 × 6 = 12 pipe-hours of work.

(b) A car covers 300 km at 60 km/h in 5 hours. At 75 km/h it takes 300 ÷ 75 = 4 hours.

The two lines draw themselves in turn. Direct proportion rises steadily. Inverse proportion falls away.
Using the direct proportion method (multiply up) for the wall-building problem

Assuming every scaling problem is direct proportion is one of the most common ratio mistakes.

The fix: more workers means less time needed, not more. That is the signal for inverse proportion. In direct proportion both quantities move the same way (more pens, more cost). In inverse proportion they move in opposite ways (more workers, fewer days). A quick test: does doubling one double the other (direct), or halve it (inverse)?

“A taxi costs Rs 100 plus Rs 50 per km, so 4 km costs twice as much as 2 km”

Not every “more gives more” situation is direct proportion.

The fix: 2 km costs 100 + 50 × 2 = Rs 200, and 4 km costs 100 + 50 × 4 = Rs 300. Doubling the distance did not double the cost, because of the fixed Rs 100 charge. Direct proportion needs the ratio to be the same every time, and here 200 ÷ 2 = 100 per km but 300 ÷ 4 = 75 per km. The graph would not pass through (0, 0).

10.Continued Proportion and Mean Proportional

Three numbers a, b, c are in continued proportion when a : b = b : c. The middle term b is called the mean proportional, and it equals √(a × c). (See Squares, Cubes & Roots.)

Example 16

Find the mean proportional between 4 and 16. b = √(4 × 16) = √64 = 8. Check: 4 : 8 = 8 : 16, since both simplify to 1 : 2. ✓

11.Choosing the Right Method

Splitting a total by a ratioAdd the parts, find one part, scale up
Both quantities increase togetherDirect proportion (unitary method)
One increases as the other decreasesInverse proportion (keep the product constant)
Solving for an unknown in a : b = c : dCross-multiplication
Finding the middle term of a : b = b : cMean proportional = √(a × c)
Two linked ratios sharing a termMake the shared term equal using the LCM

12.Ratio and Proportion in Real Life

The gold stretch is the 3.5 cm measured on the map. A scale is just a direct proportion.
Real-World Examples

Map scales use direct proportion: at a scale of 1 cm : 5 km, a 3.5 cm map distance represents 3.5 × 5 = 17.5 km. Speed and time for a fixed distance are in inverse proportion: a car covering 300 km at 60 km/h takes 5 hours, but at 75 km/h takes only 300 ÷ 75 = 4 hours. Mixing paint in a red : blue ratio of 3 : 5 means that if 15 L of blue is used, the red needed is 15 × 3/5 = 9 L.

Example 17: A Scale Written as 1 : 50,000

On a map with scale 1 : 50,000, 4.5 cm represents 4.5 × 50,000 = 225,000 cm. Since 100,000 cm = 1 km, that is 2.25 km.

Example 18: Mortar and Rice

(a) A mortar mix uses cement : sand = 1 : 4. If 5 bags of cement are used, the sand needed is 5 × 4 = 20 bags.

(b) Rice and water are cooked in the ratio 1 : 2. For 3 cups of rice, use 3 × 2 = 6 cups of water.

Did You Know?

The Golden Ratio, written φ (phi), is about 1.618 : 1. It is linked to the Fibonacci numbers: the ratio of each Fibonacci number to the one before it (8/5 = 1.6, 13/8 = 1.625, 21/13 ≈ 1.615) gets closer and closer to φ. Some plants show Fibonacci-type spiral counts. But many popular claims about the golden ratio, such as that it appears in hurricanes, in every shell, or was used deliberately in ancient buildings, are exaggerated or unproven. Check a claim before you repeat it.

13.Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up the order. Apples to oranges (4 : 6) is not oranges to apples (6 : 4).
  • Different units. Convert first: 50 cm : 2 m is 50 : 200, not 50 : 2.
  • Part-to-part versus part-to-whole. 2 : 3 means 2/5 and 3/5, not 2/3.
  • Forgetting to add the parts. To share in the ratio 2 : 3, divide by 5, not by 2 or 3.
  • Using direct proportion for an inverse problem. If more of one means less of the other, multiply to find the constant.
  • Assuming “more gives more” is always direct. A fixed charge breaks direct proportion.
  • Not checking. Shares should add up to the total, and a ratio answer should simplify back to the original.

14.Try It Yourself

  1. Simplify 18 : 24.
  2. Divide Rs 900 in the ratio 4 : 5.
  3. 4 pencils cost Rs 60. How much do 7 pencils cost?
Show answers

1. HCF = 6, so 18 : 24 = 3 : 4.

2. 4 + 5 = 9 parts. One part = 900 ÷ 9 = 100. Shares: Rs 400 and Rs 500.

3. One pencil costs 60 ÷ 4 = Rs 15. Seven cost 7 × 15 = Rs 105.

15.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] Write 24 : 36 : 60 in its simplest form.
  2. [3 marks] Share Rs 6,000 in the ratio 3 : 5 : 4.
  3. [3 marks] Find x if x : 9 = 8 : 12.
  4. [3 marks] 6 workers can build a wall in 10 days. How many workers are needed to build it in 4 days?
  5. [3 marks] A map has the scale 1 : 50,000. Two towns are 7.5 cm apart on the map. Find the real distance in kilometres.
Show answers

1. HCF = 12, so the ratio is 2 : 3 : 5.

2. 3 + 5 + 4 = 12 parts. One part = 6,000 ÷ 12 = 500. Shares: Rs 1,500, Rs 2,500 and Rs 2,000.

3. Cross-multiply: 12x = 9 × 8 = 72, so x = 6.

4. Inverse proportion: total = 6 × 10 = 60 worker-days. Workers needed = 60 ÷ 4 = 15.

5. 7.5 × 50,000 = 375,000 cm = 3,750 m = 3.75 km.

16.Key Points

  • A ratio compares quantities of the same kind. Simplify it by dividing by the HCF, after putting both in the same units.
  • To share in a ratio: add the parts, find one part, multiply out, and check the shares add to the total.
  • Part-to-part (2 : 3) is not part-to-whole (2/5).
  • Direct proportion: ratio constant, same direction. Inverse proportion: product constant, opposite directions.
  • A proportion a : b = c : d is solved by cross-multiplication: a × d = b × c.
  • Maps, speeds, recipes and mixes are all ratio problems.

17.For Teachers and Parents

Bar models are the most useful tool in this topic. Ask learners to draw the total as a bar and cut it into the right number of equal parts before they calculate anything. Practise deciding between direct and inverse proportion by asking “if one doubles, what happens to the other?”, and by using real contexts such as recipes, journeys and workers. Pay attention to units, and ask learners to check that their shares add up to the total. Recipes at home are a natural place to practise scaling.

18.Why This Topic Matters

Ratio and proportion are used constantly outside the classroom: scaling a recipe, reading a map, mixing chemicals or paint safely, converting currencies, and comparing prices of differently sized packages. They are also the direct foundation for percentages, since a percentage is simply a ratio to 100.
