English as an International Language

English is used by an estimated 1.5 billion people worldwide, yet only about a quarter of them speak it as a first language.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 18 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Global Communication

Roughly three out of four people who use English did not grow up speaking it. That single fact explains why English has become the world's most widely used bridge between people who share no other language, and why learning it is about far more than learning a school subject.

This guide explains what it means for a language to be "international", how English reached that position, how it is used in different countries, and why many linguists now talk about World Englishes in the plural. It looks at English in science, travel, business, diplomacy and the internet, offers practical tips for communicating clearly with people from other countries, and weighs the advantages and concerns fairly. It includes two worked examples, a checklist, common mistakes and ten practice questions.

It follows Language and Its Need, and leads into The Importance of the English Language.

1.What Does "International Language" Mean?

A language becomes international not simply because many people speak it, but because people use it to communicate across their different native languages. When a German engineer works with a Japanese supplier, or a Pakistani doctor reads research written by a Brazilian team, a shared language is needed, and in many such cases it is English.

Key term: lingua franca

A language used as a common means of communication between people whose native languages differ. The phrase comes from Italian and means "Frankish language". English is today the best-known example, but it is not the first: Latin, Arabic, Persian, French and Portuguese have all served this role in different places and periods.

Three terms are often mixed up:

  • International language: used for communication between nations and across borders.
  • Global language: a language with a recognised role in many countries. This is the term often used for English.
  • English as a Lingua Franca (ELF): English as it is used between people for whom it is an additional language. Researchers who study ELF point out that most conversations in English around the world now happen without a native speaker present.

2.How Many People Use English?

Counting speakers is difficult because there is no agreed rule for how well someone must speak a language to be counted. Estimates differ between sources and between years, so treat all numbers here as rough. The research organisation Ethnologue, in its 2025 estimates as widely reported, puts English at around 1.5 billion speakers in total, of whom roughly 380 to 390 million speak it as a first language. That is about a quarter. The rest, around 1.1 billion, speak it as a second or additional language.

Who speaks English, and who speaks Mandarin?

Diagram 1. English has the most speakers in total, but Mandarin has more first-language speakers.

This is a useful correction to a common belief: English is not the language with the most native speakers. Mandarin Chinese has more. What makes English unusual is the very large number of people who use it as an additional language. According to Ethnologue's May 2025 count, there are about seven thousand languages in the world, so English is one of a very small number that serve as bridges for millions of non-native speakers.

3.How English Became an International Language

English did not become international because it is easier or more beautiful than other languages. Linguists stress that no language is inherently better suited to the role. Its position came from a series of events in history, each building on the last.

Five stages in the spread of English
Each stage added new speakers and new uses.

Diagram 2. Dates are approximate, and the stages overlap.

  1. Trade, settlement and empire. From the 1600s, English-speaking traders and settlers travelled to North America, the Caribbean, Asia, Africa and the Pacific. By the early twentieth century, by many estimates, the British Empire covered about a quarter of the world's population. Wherever it governed, English was introduced in administration, law and education, and in many countries it stayed after independence.
  2. The United States. In the twentieth century, the United States became the world's largest economy. Its films, music, television, products and universities carried English into homes and classrooms around the world.
  3. International organisations. After the Second World War, new bodies such as the United Nations were created. English is one of the six official languages of the UN, and, with French, one of the two working languages of its Secretariat.
  4. Science, aviation and business. Research is now published, taught and shared internationally, much of it in English. Aviation adopted English widely for international radio communication (see section 6), and many multinational companies use English between staff from different countries.
  5. The internet. From the 1990s, much of the early content, software and technical documentation online was in English. Today the internet is multilingual, but English remains very prominent.

The history also has a painful side. English arrived in many places through colonisation, often alongside the suppression of local languages and cultures. Being honest about this is part of understanding why people feel strongly, in both positive and negative ways, about English today.

4.Three Circles: How Countries Use English

English plays different roles in different countries. A popular model by the linguist Braj Kachru, first described in the 1980s, groups countries into three "circles":

Kachru's three circles
A simplified map of English around the world.

Diagram 3. Real countries do not always fit neatly, and people and communities move between circles.

CircleRole of EnglishExamples
InnerThe main first language of most peopleUnited Kingdom, United States, Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand
OuterAn official or institutional language (government, courts, education) alongside local languages, usually from a colonial pastIndia, Nigeria, Pakistan, Singapore, Kenya, the Philippines
ExpandingA foreign language taught in schools and used for international communicationJapan, Germany, Brazil, China, Egypt

The model is a helpful starting point, but it is simplified. Some countries, such as South Africa and Singapore, do not fit neatly, many people in the "expanding circle" use English daily for work, and in some outer-circle countries English is the first language of a growing urban minority. Newer models try to describe how English is used in practice, not just which country a speaker lives in.

5.World Englishes: Many Varieties, Not One "Correct" English

Because English is used in so many places, it has developed many varieties, each with its own vocabulary, pronunciation and sometimes grammar patterns. Linguists use the term World Englishes for this idea. British, American, Australian, Indian, Nigerian, Singaporean and many other varieties are all real, rule-governed forms of English.

FeatureExamples
SpellingBritish: colour, centre, practise (verb). American: color, center, practice (verb).
VocabularyBritish: lift, flat, lorry. American: elevator, apartment, truck.
Local wordsSouth Asian English commonly uses lakh (100,000) and crore (10 million) in everyday numbers.
PronunciationStress, rhythm and vowel sounds differ between regions, and all are understood by competent speakers.

For learners, there are two practical lessons. First, you do not need to copy a British or American accent to be a good speaker of English. What matters is being clear and consistent. Second, choose one standard for writing, such as British spelling in this course, and stay with it. For school, exams and formal writing, follow the standard your syllabus expects. See also Formal vs. Informal English and Spoken English & Pronunciation.

6.English at Work in the World

Aviation. International pilots and air traffic controllers need a shared language for safety. Since 5 March 2008, the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) has required pilots and air traffic controllers involved in international operations to show at least Operational Level (Level 4) proficiency in the language used for radio communication. English is the most widely used language for this, and air traffic services must be able to provide English on request, but the requirement itself applies to whichever language is used. The purpose, ICAO explains, is to reduce miscommunication and to help pilots and controllers recognise and solve it when it happens.

Science and education. A very large share of international research papers and conference talks are in English, and many universities around the world teach some courses in English. This makes research easier to share across borders, but it also means that scientists who are not confident in English may face extra effort.

Diplomacy and organisations. English is one of the six official languages of the United Nations and is a working language of many international organisations.

Business and travel. Companies with staff in several countries often choose English for meetings and documents. Hotels, airports and tourist services in many countries use English to serve visitors from everywhere.

The internet. English is very prominent online, especially in technical documentation, software and international websites, but many other languages are widely used, and their share online has been growing.

A reminder. English is not used everywhere or by everyone. In most countries, local and national languages are the languages of home, daily life, and often of government and literature. English complements them rather than replacing them.

7.Speaking English Clearly with an International Audience

Studies of English as a lingua franca show that successful international communication does not depend on perfect native-like English. It depends on both people working together to be understood. Here are habits that help, whether you are a learner or a native speaker:

HabitWhy it helps
Speak at a steady pace and pause between ideasListeners have time to process
Use plain, common wordsRare words and slang cause confusion
Avoid idioms, jokes and sports metaphors"Touch base" or "a piece of cake" may not translate
Check understanding: "Does that make sense?"Problems are caught early
Paraphrase: "In other words…"A second way of saying it often works
Ask for repetition without embarrassmentEveryone needs it sometimes
Write important details (dates, numbers, names)Spoken numbers are easy to mishear

These habits also help you in professional emails, interviews and international exams.

8.Advantages and Concerns

Thoughtful people disagree about the effects of a dominant world language, and a balanced view includes both sides.

AdvantagesConcerns
A shared tool lets people from different countries cooperate without needing a translator each time.Native speakers may gain an advantage in business, publishing and academia, while others must spend years studying.
Knowing English opens access to education, research, jobs and information.Not everyone has equal access to good English teaching, so inequality can grow.
It supports safety in fields such as aviation.Local and minority languages may lose speakers when English dominates schooling and work.
It makes travel, study abroad and international friendship easier.Some critics argue English spreads one cultural viewpoint along with the language.

Many educators argue that the best response is multilingualism: learning English as an additional language while keeping and valuing your home language, rather than treating them as rivals. Research and policy in this area is an active, open field of discussion.

9.Will English Always Be International?

History suggests caution. Latin was the common language of scholarship in Europe for centuries. French held a leading role in diplomacy for a long period. Persian was widely used in the courts and administration of South Asia and Central Asia. In each case, the language's role changed when political, economic and technological conditions changed. Language status is not permanent.

Today, shifts in economic power, large populations learning other languages, and improving machine translation could all change the picture, but nobody can predict how. A reasonable expectation is that English will remain important for many years, while other languages also grow in importance, and that skill in more than one language will matter more, not less.

10.Worked Examples

Example A: Placing countries on the three circles

Decide which circle fits best for each country, and explain your reasoning.

1Canada. English is the first language of many citizens and a main language of government. Inner circle.
2Nigeria. English is the official language of government and education, used alongside hundreds of local languages. Outer circle.
3Japan. English is taught in schools and used with foreign partners, but Japanese is the language of government and daily life. Expanding circle.
4Singapore. English is one of four official languages and is widely used in government, law and schools. Outer circle, though many families now speak it at home.

Try it yourself. Place Pakistan and Brazil, and say what would make the choice difficult. Sample answer: Pakistan is usually placed in the outer circle, because English is used in government, law, higher education and business alongside Urdu and regional languages. Brazil is usually placed in the expanding circle, because Portuguese is the national language and English is a foreign language used for international contact. The difficulty is that the circles describe the country as a whole, while individual people and cities vary widely in how much English they use.

Example B: Making a message clear for an international reader

Original email: "Let's touch base ASAP. We're up against the clock, so it'd be a piece of cake if you could run this by your boss before the dust settles."

1Underline the idioms. touch base, up against the clock, piece of cake, run this by, before the dust settles.
2Replace each with plain meaning. talk soon; we have little time; easy; show this to; before things change.
3Replace "ASAP" with a clear time. Say "by Thursday at 12:00".
4Keep sentences short. One request per sentence.

Revised email: "Can we speak by Thursday at 12:00? We have very little time. Please show this document to your manager before then. If you need more time, please tell me."

Try it yourself. Rewrite this for an international audience: "Break a leg at your presentation, and don't beat around the bush." Sample answer: "I hope your presentation goes very well. Please speak directly and clearly."

12.Common Mistakes

MistakeBetter thinking
Saying English has the most native speakersMandarin has more first-language speakers; English has the most speakers in total
Treating one variety (British or American) as the only correct EnglishMany varieties are valid; choose a standard for writing and follow it
Assuming English is the official language everywhereIts role varies by country
Using idioms with international listenersUse plain, direct language
Saying English spread because it is "easier"History, not simplicity, explains its spread
Quoting exact speaker numbers as factSay "about" and name the source and year
Seeing English and home languages as rivalsValue both; multilingualism has advantages

13.Common Myths

MythEnglish is the most widely spoken first language.
FactMandarin Chinese has more first-language speakers. English leads when additional-language speakers are included.
MythEnglish is easier than other languages.
FactLinguists say no language is inherently easier. English became international because of history, economics and power.
MythOnly native speakers speak "real" English.
FactMost English conversations in the world now involve non-native speakers, and many varieties are fully legitimate.
MythEnglish will always be the global language.
FactHistory shows that international languages change over time.

14.Key Terms

International language: a language used for communication between nations and across borders.
Lingua franca: a common language between speakers of different native languages.
ELF: English as a Lingua Franca, used between speakers for whom it is an additional language.
First language (L1): the language a person learns from early childhood.
Second or additional language (L2): a language learned after the first.
Three circles: Kachru's model of inner, outer and expanding uses of English.
World Englishes: the idea that English has many valid varieties.
Multilingualism: the ability to use more than one language.
Colonial legacy: the long-term effects of colonial rule, including language.

15.Why This Matters

Understanding English as an international language helps you in three ways. It explains why learning English can open doors, it reminds you that English belongs to everyone who uses it and not only to native speakers, and it encourages you to communicate in ways that make others feel understood. Continue with The Importance of the English Language.

Key points to remember
  • A lingua franca connects speakers of different native languages.
  • English has about 1.5 billion speakers in total, most of them using it as an additional language. Mandarin has more first-language speakers.
  • English spread through empire, the rise of the United States, international organisations, science, aviation, business and the internet.
  • Kachru's three circles describe inner, outer and expanding uses of English.
  • World Englishes are legitimate varieties; choose one standard for writing.
  • Clear international communication uses plain words, exact details and checks for understanding.
  • Advantages and concerns both matter; multilingualism offers a balanced way forward.
  • Language status is not permanent.
For teachers and parents

Activity: ask students to list every place in their week where they meet English (school, signs, phones, videos, family). Then have them classify each use as local, national or international, and discuss why English appears there. Follow with the idiom-rewriting task in Example B.

Please note: speaker numbers are estimates that differ between sources and years, and the three-circle model is a simplification. Students should treat figures as approximate and name the source. Topics such as colonial history and language policy are discussed openly by scholars, so encourage students to consider more than one viewpoint.
