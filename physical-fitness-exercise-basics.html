Physical Fitness and Exercise Basics

Exercise doesn't just build muscle — it strengthens your heart, sharpens your brain, and improves your mood. Understanding the basics helps you build habits that last a lifetime.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 14 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Health

The human body evolved to move. For most of history, people walked, ran, carried and lifted as part of daily life. Today many of us sit for most of the day, which is why deliberately building physical activity into our lives has become essential for good health. This guide explains what fitness is, how much activity young people need, how to measure effort with your heart rate, and how to exercise safely and build a habit that lasts.

1.What Does "Fitness" Actually Mean?

Physical fitness is not just about how much weight you can lift or how fast you can run. It is made up of several parts, called components, and each one contributes to overall health.

ComponentWhat it measuresExample activity
Cardiovascular enduranceHow well the heart and lungs supply oxygen during activityRunning, swimming, cycling
Muscular strengthThe amount of force muscles can produceWeightlifting, push-ups
Muscular enduranceHow long muscles can keep workingPlanks, repeated squats
FlexibilityThe range of motion around jointsStretching, yoga
Body compositionThe proportion of fat to lean tissue in the bodyAffected by diet and all types of exercise

Sports also need skill-related fitness, such as balance, coordination, agility, speed and reaction time. A footballer, a cricketer and a gymnast train these skills along with the five health components above.

2.Types of Exercise

Exercise can be sorted into three broad categories. A balanced routine includes some of each.

Aerobic

Raises your heart rate and breathing for a longer time. It builds endurance.

Examples: jogging, brisk walking, cycling, swimming, football, skipping.

Strength

Makes muscles and bones stronger by working them against resistance.

Examples: push-ups, squats, resistance bands, carrying and lifting safely, climbing.

Flexibility

Keeps joints moving freely and muscles supple, and helps prevent stiffness.

Examples: stretching, yoga, and slow, controlled movements.

3.How Exercise Benefits the Body

  • Heart and lungs: regular activity strengthens the heart muscle and improves how well the lungs work.
  • Bones and muscles: weight-bearing exercise increases bone density and muscle strength, especially important during the growth years.
  • Weight management: exercise helps balance the energy you take in with the energy you use.
  • Brain function: physical activity increases blood flow to the brain, supporting concentration and memory.
  • Mental health: exercise triggers the release of endorphins, chemicals that improve mood and reduce stress.
  • Sleep: active people usually fall asleep more easily. See our article on sleep and rest.
Example

A brisk 30-minute walk raises your heart rate and improves blood circulation, and research shows it can lift mood for hours afterwards. This shows that you do not need intense gym workouts to gain real health benefits.

4.How Much Exercise Do You Need?

Health guidance from the World Health Organization gives the following targets. They are minimums for good health, and doing more is usually better.

GroupRecommended activity
Children and teenagers (5 to 17)At least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity every day, mostly aerobic. Include vigorous activity and activities that strengthen muscles and bones at least 3 days a week.
Adults (18 to 64)150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening activity on 2 or more days.

The guidance also advises everyone to sit less and to reduce long periods of sitting and screen time. Any movement is better than none, and starting small is fine.

5.Sit Less and Move More Through the Day

Exercise sessions are important, but so is the time between them. Sitting for many hours, in class, on a phone or in front of a screen, is linked to poorer health even in people who exercise. A few small habits help: stand up and move for a minute or two every 30 to 60 minutes, walk or cycle for short trips, take the stairs, and do active chores. These small movements add up across the day.

6.How Hard Should You Exercise?

The terms moderate and vigorous describe how hard your body is working. A simple test is the talk test. During moderate activity you can talk but not sing. During vigorous activity you can say only a few words before you need a breath.

You can also use your heart rate. A rough estimate of your maximum heart rate is 220 minus your age. Moderate activity is about 50 to 70 percent of that number, and vigorous activity is about 70 to 85 percent. To check your pulse, find it at your wrist or neck, count the beats for 15 seconds and multiply by 4.

Worked example: heart rate zones for a 15-year-old

1Estimate the maximum heart rate: 220 − 15 = 205 beats per minute.
2Moderate zone (50 to 70 percent): 205 × 0.50 = 102.5 and 205 × 0.70 = 143.5, so about 103 to 144 beats per minute.
3Vigorous zone (70 to 85 percent): 205 × 0.70 = 143.5 and 205 × 0.85 = 174.25, so about 144 to 174 beats per minute.
Heart rate zones for a 15-year-old
LightBelow 50%
Moderate50 to 70%
Vigorous70 to 85%
MaxAbove 85%
  • Light: Under 103 beats per minute
  • Moderate: 103 to 144 beats per minute
  • Vigorous: 144 to 174 beats per minute
  • Maximum: Above 174 beats per minute

This is only an estimate. Real maximum heart rates differ from person to person, so use the numbers as a guide together with how you feel.

Try it yourself: what is the moderate zone for a 12-year-old? Answer: 220 − 12 = 208. Then 208 × 0.50 = 104 and 208 × 0.70 ≈ 146, so about 104 to 146 beats per minute.

7.Plan Your Exercise: The FITT Principle

Coaches use the letters FITT to plan a routine:

F: Frequency

How often you exercise, for example most days of the week.

I: Intensity

How hard you work: light, moderate or vigorous.

T: Time

How long each session lasts, for example 30 to 60 minutes.

T: Type

The kind of activity: aerobic, strength or flexibility.

To improve, increase one thing at a time, and do it gradually. Doing too much too soon is a common cause of injury and quitting.

DayExample for a teenager
MondayWalk or cycle to school, plus 30 minutes of football with friends
TuesdayStrength: squats, push-ups and a plank, about 20 minutes, plus a walk
WednesdayPE lesson or a sport you enjoy
ThursdaySkipping or jogging for 30 minutes, then stretching
FridayActive play, a team game or swimming
WeekendA longer walk, cricket or cycling, and one rest day

8.A Simple Home Workout With No Equipment

You can build strength and fitness at home with a short circuit. After a warm-up, do each exercise for about 30 to 40 seconds, rest for 20 seconds, and repeat the circuit 2 or 3 times.

  • Squats: feet shoulder-width apart, bend the knees as if sitting on a chair, keep the back straight, then stand up.
  • Push-ups: hands under the shoulders, body in a straight line. You can do them on your knees at first.
  • Plank: rest on forearms and toes, keep the body straight and hold steady.
  • Marching or skipping on the spot: keeps the heart rate up between strength exercises.

Good technique matters more than speed. Stop if something hurts, and ask a teacher or coach to check your form.

9.Exercise Safely

  • Warm up for 5 to 10 minutes with gentle movement, such as walking and arm circles, before harder activity.
  • Cool down with a few minutes of slow movement and stretching afterwards.
  • Drink water before, during and after exercise, especially in hot weather.
  • Wear suitable shoes and clothes, and use protective equipment, such as a helmet for cycling.
  • Listen to your body. Mild muscle tiredness is normal, but sharp pain is a warning sign.
  • Stop and get help if you feel chest pain, severe breathlessness, dizziness or faintness.
  • Rest. Muscles grow stronger during recovery, so build rest days into every week.

If you have a medical condition, such as asthma, a heart condition or an injury, ask a doctor what exercise is suitable for you.

10.Fuel and Recovery

Exercise works best together with good food and sleep. Your body needs enough energy and nutrients to move, and it needs protein and rest to repair muscles. Eating from all the food groups, as explained in our guide to food groups and healthy eating, gives you the fuel you need, and water replaces what you lose through sweat. You can read more about energy in What Is Nutrition?.

11.Building a Sustainable Habit

The best exercise routine is the one you will actually keep. Enjoyment and variety matter much more than intensity for building a long-term habit. Choose activities you like, exercise with friends or family, set small goals, track your progress, and make activity part of your daily routine.

Real-World Example

Consider two students trying to become more active. One forces themselves into an intense daily gym routine and quits after two weeks, exhausted and unmotivated. The other joins a school sports team, walks to school, and plays with friends a few times a week. They enjoy it so much that it becomes a lasting part of their routine. Consistency beats intensity when building fitness habits.

12.Common Fitness Myths

MythYou need a gym to get fit.
FactWalking, running, skipping, cycling, sports and bodyweight exercises need little or no equipment.
MythNo pain, no gain.
FactMild tiredness is normal, but sharp or lasting pain means you should stop and rest.
MythYou can burn fat from one body part by exercising it.
FactYou cannot choose where the body loses fat. Overall activity and healthy eating matter.
MythStrength training will stunt your growth.
FactSafe strength exercise with good technique and supervision is healthy for young people and helps build strong bones.
Key points to remember
  • Fitness has several components: endurance, strength, flexibility and body composition.
  • A balanced routine includes aerobic, strength and flexibility exercise.
  • Young people need at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity every day.
  • Use the talk test or heart rate zones to judge effort, and warm up and cool down.
  • Enjoyment and consistency matter more than intensity.
For teachers and parents

Activity: ask students to measure their resting pulse, do 3 minutes of brisk movement, and measure it again. Discuss which heart rate zone they reached, and plan a weekly routine using the FITT principle.

Please note: this article is general education and not medical advice. Students with a health condition or injury should check with a doctor before starting a new exercise programme.
