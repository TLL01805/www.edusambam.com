EQ Explained: Goleman's Five Components of Emotional Intelligence

Go deeper into emotional intelligence: the five components one by one, what the research really shows, its limits, and practical exercises you can start today.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 23 min read | IQ & EQ

Last updated: 12 October 2026. Written by the EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team. This article is for general education and does not replace advice from a qualified professional.

Two friends hear that their exam results are lower than expected. One snaps at everyone, blames the teacher and refuses to talk for a week. The other feels just as upset, but she notices her disappointment, takes a walk, asks what went wrong and makes a plan. Same feeling, very different handling. The skill that explains the difference has a name: emotional intelligence, or EQ. In our first article we introduced it. In this guide we go much deeper into the five components described by the psychologist Daniel Goleman, look at what the research truly shows (including the criticisms), and give you tools to practise each part.

Key Takeaways

Goleman's five components are self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills. Strong research supports several of the skills inside them, such as naming emotions, rethinking a situation (reappraisal), and school programmes that teach social and emotional skills. But scientists also criticise the way EQ has been marketed: popular claims that EQ matters far more than IQ are not supported, and some tests measure personality more than a new kind of intelligence. Treat EQ as a useful set of learnable skills, not as a score that decides your future.

1.Three Families of Emotional Intelligence Models

When people say "EQ", they may mean quite different things. Researchers group the models into three families, and knowing the difference helps you read any article or test critically.

FamilyWhoWhat EI isHow it is measured
Ability modelPeter Salovey and John Mayer (1990, revised 1997)A set of mental abilities for reasoning with and about emotionsPerformance tests with better and worse answers, such as the MSCEIT
Mixed modelDaniel Goleman (1995 and later) and Reuven Bar-OnA mix of emotional abilities with personality traits, motivation and social skillsSelf-report questionnaires and ratings by others
Trait modelK. V. Petrides and colleaguesSelf-perceptions of one's own emotional abilities, treated as a personality traitSelf-report questionnaires

Goleman's five components belong to the mixed family. They are broader and easier to apply in daily life, which is why they are so popular in schools and workplaces, but for the same reason they overlap with personality. The ability model is narrower but easier to test scientifically. Salovey and Mayer describe four abilities, shown below, which are a useful companion to Goleman's list.

The Salovey and Mayer four-branch model: abilities build from simple (noticing feelings) to complex (managing them well).

2.Goleman's Model: Five Components and Their Skills

Daniel Goleman, a science journalist and psychologist, built on the work of Salovey and Mayer in his 1995 bestseller Emotional Intelligence. In his 1998 book Working with Emotional Intelligence he described five components and a list of around two dozen related competencies. In later work with Richard Boyatzis, the model was reorganised into four domains (self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and relationship management), but the five-component version remains the most widely taught.

ComponentTypeSkills listed under it (from Goleman's 1998 model)
1. Self-awarenessAbout yourselfEmotional awareness; accurate self-assessment; self-confidence
2. Self-regulationAbout yourselfSelf-control; trustworthiness; conscientiousness; adaptability; innovation
3. MotivationAbout yourselfAchievement drive; commitment; initiative; optimism
4. EmpathyAbout othersUnderstanding others; helping others develop; service orientation; valuing diversity; reading group dynamics
5. Social skillsAbout othersInfluence; communication; conflict management; leadership; building bonds; collaboration; teamwork

The following five sections take each component in turn. For each one you will find what it means, what research says, a worked example and a practice exercise.

3.Component 1: Self-Awareness

Self-awareness is knowing what you feel, why you feel it, how it affects your behaviour, and what your strengths and limits are. It is the foundation for every other component, because you cannot manage what you have not noticed.

What it looks like

  • Emotional awareness: noticing a feeling as it arises, including its signals in the body, such as a tight chest, a hot face or a stomach that "drops".
  • Accurate self-assessment: knowing what you do well and where you need to improve, without exaggerating or putting yourself down.
  • Self-confidence: a realistic belief in your own ability to handle things.

What research suggests

One of the best-tested self-awareness skills is affect labelling, which means putting a feeling into words. In a brain-imaging study by Lieberman and colleagues (2007), people who named the emotion shown in a picture had a weaker response in the amygdala, a brain region involved in fast emotional reactions, than people who did other tasks with the same pictures. Naming the emotion was also linked with more activity in a part of the right prefrontal cortex that helps with control. This helps to explain why saying "I feel embarrassed" can take some of the sting out of the feeling.

A second point: people are not always accurate judges of themselves. That is one reason why feedback from others, such as a trusted teacher, friend or colleague, is such a valuable tool.

Worked Example

Hamza feels irritable all afternoon and snaps at his sister. Instead of just reacting, he stops and asks himself three questions. What am I feeling? "Worried." About what? "Tomorrow's presentation." What do I need? "To practise once and to go to bed early." Naming the worry, instead of letting it appear as anger, lets him choose a useful action and apologise to his sister.

Practice

  • Body scan: three times a day, pause for 30 seconds and notice any tension, then guess the feeling behind it.
  • Name it precisely: choose a specific word (for example "disappointed" or "overwhelmed", not just "bad").
  • Ask for feedback: request one honest observation about your strengths and one area to improve, and say thank you.

4.Component 2: Self-Regulation

Self-regulation (also called self-management) is the ability to manage disruptive emotions and impulses, stay calm under pressure, keep your word, and adapt to change. It does not mean suppressing feelings. It means choosing how to respond.

The process model: where can you step in?

The psychologist James Gross describes emotion as a process that unfolds over time, with several points where you can step in.

James Gross's process model of emotion regulation: you can step in at different points. Strategies that act earlier tend to cost less and work better.
StrategyWhat it meansExample
Choose the situationApproach or avoid situations in advanceStudying in a quiet library instead of a noisy room before an exam
Change the situationAlter things to change their emotional impactAsking a friend to lower the music so you can talk calmly
Shift attentionFocus on something else, or on a different part of the situationConcentrating on your breathing during a stressful test
Rethink the meaning (reappraisal)Change how you interpret the situation"This feedback hurts, but it shows me how to improve"
Manage the responseChange how the emotion shows up in the body or behaviourSlow breathing; or hiding the feeling (suppression)

Reappraisal versus suppression

In five studies, Gross and John (2003) compared two common habits. People who often used reappraisal reported more positive emotion, less negative emotion, better relationships and higher well-being. People who often used suppression, that is, hiding the outward signs of what they feel, reported less positive emotion, more negative emotion, worse interpersonal functioning and lower well-being. Suppression feels like control, but it comes late in the process and does not remove the feeling.

Self-control over a lifetime

The Dunedin study in New Zealand followed about 1,000 children from birth to age 32. Children with better self-control, as rated by parents, teachers, observers and the children themselves, had better health, fewer money problems and fewer criminal convictions as adults, along a gradient from lowest to highest self-control, and this held after accounting for intelligence and family background (Moffitt and colleagues, 2011). A well-known related study, the "marshmallow test", has been revisited. A larger and more varied sample of 918 children (Watts, Duncan and Quan, 2018) found that the link between waiting for a reward at age four or five and later outcomes was much weaker than first reported, and that family background explained a lot of it. The fair conclusion is that self-control matters, that it is shaped by circumstances as well as by character, and that it can be taught and supported.

Worked Example

Sana receives a harsh comment on her essay. Suppression: she says "It's fine" but replays it all night and avoids the teacher. Reappraisal: she tells herself, "This hurts, but the comment is about the essay, not about me. If I ask what exactly needs fixing, I can improve." She books a short meeting, gets clear advice and feels better by the evening.

Practice

  • The 4-6 breath: inhale for four seconds and exhale slowly for six, repeated five times.
  • The reappraisal question: "Is there another way to see this that is also true?"
  • Delay rule: wait ten minutes, or sleep on it, before replying to something that made you angry.

5.Component 3: Motivation

In Goleman's model, motivation means an inner drive to pursue goals for their own sake, with energy, optimism and persistence, and not only for money or status. It includes the drive to improve, commitment to a group's goals, readiness to act on opportunities, and the ability to keep going after setbacks.

Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation

Self-determination theory (Ryan and Deci, 2000) separates intrinsic motivation, doing something because it is interesting or meaningful, from extrinsic motivation, doing it for a reward or to avoid a punishment. The theory proposes that people thrive when three basic needs are met: autonomy (a sense of choice), competence (a feeling of growing skill) and relatedness (connection with others).

NeedWhat helpsWhat harms
AutonomyOffering choices; explaining the reason for a taskConstant pressure and control
CompetenceTasks that are challenging but achievable; specific, constructive feedbackTasks that are far too hard or too easy; vague or harsh criticism
RelatednessTeamwork, belonging, support from peers and teachersIsolation; constant comparison with others

Goals that work

Decades of research on goal setting (Locke and Latham, 2002) found that specific and challenging goals lead to better performance than vague goals such as "do your best", as long as the person accepts the goal and receives feedback. A useful way to write a goal is to make it specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.

Optimism and resilience

Resilience is not the absence of setbacks. It is the ability to recover from them. Optimistic people tend to explain a failure as temporary and specific ("I did badly on this test") rather than permanent and general ("I'm hopeless"). That habit of explanation can be practised.

Worked Example

Ali wants to improve in mathematics. A vague plan: "I'll study more." A better plan: "I will solve 10 problems on fractions, check them against the answers, and redo the ones I missed, every weekday for three weeks. I will ask my teacher about any I still cannot do." The second plan gives him choice, a clear target, feedback and a visible sense of progress.

Practice

  • Write one goal for the week in the five-part form above.
  • At the end of each day, write one thing you improved at, however small.
  • When something goes wrong, finish this sentence: "This is temporary because...".

6.Component 4: Empathy

Empathy is understanding what other people feel and why, and responding in a way that fits. In Goleman's model it also includes helping others to develop, serving their needs and reading how a group is feeling.

Different kinds of empathy

Three related but different responses to another person's feelings. Researchers distinguish empathic distress from compassion.

Researchers separate cognitive empathy (working out what another person thinks or feels), emotional empathy (feeling some of what they feel) and compassion or empathic concern (caring and being motivated to help). Singer and Klimecki (2014) explain that sharing someone's suffering can lead either to empathic distress, an aversive, self-focused feeling that makes people want to withdraw, or to compassion, a warm, caring feeling that motivates help.

In a training study (Klimecki and colleagues, 2014), participants given empathy training that focused on resonating with suffering showed increased negative feelings and more activity in brain regions linked with empathy for pain. When the same people then received compassion training, the increase in negative feelings reversed and their positive feelings grew. The authors suggested that compassion training may be a way to cope with empathic distress and build resilience. This matters for people in caring roles such as teachers, nurses and doctors, who risk burnout if they only absorb others' distress.

Empathy is not the same as sympathy or agreement

ResponseExampleWhat is missing or present
Dismissing"Don't be silly, it's not a big deal."No attempt to understand
Fixing too fast"Just do this and you'll be fine."Advice before understanding
Sympathy"Poor you, I feel so sorry."Concern, but from a distance
Empathy"It sounds like you felt left out and embarrassed. Is that right?"Understanding the other person's view and checking it
Compassionate action"That sounds hard. What would help right now?"Understanding plus offering support

Some scholars warn that empathy can be biased: we tend to feel more for people who look or think like us, or whose stories we hear vividly. A mature EQ includes noticing this tendency and extending care to people outside your usual circle.

Practice

  • Perspective-taking: before judging someone, write down two reasons why a reasonable person might act that way.
  • Reflect and check: say "It sounds like you feel... Is that right?" and listen to the answer.
  • Cross-circle conversation: have one real conversation each week with someone whose life is different from yours.

7.Component 5: Social Skills

Social skills are the ability to build relationships, communicate clearly, influence and inspire, resolve conflict, and work in a team. They are where the other four components become visible to other people.

Active listening

  1. Give full attention: put away the phone and make eye contact where appropriate for your culture.
  2. Do not interrupt: let the speaker finish.
  3. Reflect back: summarise what you heard in your own words.
  4. Ask open questions: "What was that like?" or "What would you like to happen next?"
  5. Respond to feelings as well as facts.

Three communication styles

StyleWhat it sounds likeTypical result
Passive"It's fine, whatever you want."Needs go unmet; resentment builds
Aggressive"You always ruin everything!"The other person becomes defensive or afraid
Assertive"I felt left out when the plan changed. Can we agree to check with each other first?"Clear, respectful, and more likely to lead to a solution

A simple assertive format is the "I" statement: "I feel [feeling] when [specific event] because [reason]. I would like [request]." It describes your experience rather than attacking the other person's character.

Resolving a conflict in five steps

  1. Calm down first, using your self-regulation skills.
  2. Listen to the other side and repeat it back.
  3. State your own view with "I" statements.
  4. List options together that could meet both people's key needs.
  5. Agree on one action and a time to check how it is working.
Real-World Example

Four students share a project. One has not done his part, and the others are annoyed. Instead of public blame, one student speaks to him privately: "I'm worried we will not finish on time, and I want us all to feel good about it. Is something getting in the way?" He admits that he has a family problem and was embarrassed. The team redistributes tasks and sets a Friday check-in. The project is finished, and the friendship is intact.

8.The Five Components Working Together

In real life the components are not used one at a time. Consider this situation: a friend tells you she is angry that you forgot her birthday.

ComponentWhat you do
Self-awarenessNotice your guilt and defensiveness rising
Self-regulationTake a breath and decide not to make excuses or counter-attack
MotivationRemember that you value this friendship and want to repair it
EmpathyUnderstand that she felt unimportant and hurt
Social skillsSay "I'm sorry. I should have remembered. How can I make it up to you?" and then listen

9.How Is EQ Measured?

MethodHow it worksStrengthsWeaknesses
Ability tests (for example, the MSCEIT)The Mayer-Salovey-Caruso test has 141 items covering the four branches (perceiving, using, understanding and managing emotions); answers are scored against consensus or expert viewsTests performance rather than opinions about yourselfDeciding which emotional answers are "correct" is difficult and debated
Self-report questionnairesYou rate how well statements describe youQuick and cheapMeasures self-image; can be faked; overlaps strongly with personality
360-degree or other-ratingsPeople who know you rate your behaviourShows how you come acrossRaters may be biased or uncomfortable giving honest ratings
Observation and situational testsYou respond to realistic scenariosCloser to real behaviourTime-consuming; harder to standardise

Be careful with free online "EQ tests". Most are entertainment, not scientifically validated measures, and a single number cannot describe your emotional life. The self-reflection prompts in this article are not a test and give no score.

10.What Does the Research Say About Outcomes?

EvidenceFinding
School programmes in social and emotional learning (SEL): Durlak and colleagues, 2011A meta-analysis of 213 school-based programmes with 270,034 students found improved social and emotional skills, attitudes and behaviour, and a gain in academic achievement equivalent to 11 percentile points
SEL follow-up: Taylor and colleagues, 2017A review of 82 programmes involving 97,406 students found that benefits were still visible at follow-up, from six months to many years later
Childhood self-control: Moffitt and colleagues, 2011Better childhood self-control predicted better adult health, finances and fewer convictions in about 1,000 people followed to age 32
Emotion regulation: Gross and John, 2003Habitual reappraisal linked with better well-being and relationships; habitual suppression linked with worse
Ability EI and work: Joseph and Newman, 2010A cascading pattern (perceiving, then understanding, then regulating emotions) was supported. Ability-based EI predicted performance in jobs with high emotional demands, but not consistently across jobs

The SEL programmes studied are structured, taught by trained staff and repeated over time, so their results should not be taken to mean that reading about EQ will have the same effect.

11.Criticisms and Limits: An Honest Look

Some of the strongest criticism of EQ comes from psychologists who accept that emotional skills matter. Their main points are as follows.

CriticismEvidence and explanation
Exaggerated claimsGoleman's 1998 book reported that emotional competencies matter about twice as much as purely cognitive abilities for outstanding performance. Later reviews found no solid support for such precise ratios, and popular "80% EQ" figures have no reliable source
Overlap with personalityA 2015 meta-analysis (Joseph and colleagues) found that the content of self-reported "mixed" EI measures overlaps strongly with personality traits, self-efficacy, ability EI and general mental ability. Its link with job performance (about .29) disappeared after controlling for these
Small extra value over IQ and personalityMeta-analyses found that ability-based EI adds only a very small amount to predicting job performance beyond personality and IQ, though slightly more in jobs that involve a lot of emotional labour
Measurement problemsIt is hard to say which answers on an emotion test are objectively correct, and self-report can be inflated
Definition problemsThe term covers quite different ideas, so studies are not always comparing the same thing
Dark sideEmotional skill can be used to manipulate others. Reading people well does not by itself make someone kind or honest

These criticisms do not mean that emotional skills are unimportant. They mean that specific skills such as regulating emotion, listening and handling conflict are better supported by evidence than broad claims that one "EQ score" predicts success.

12.EQ at Home, School and Work

SettingHow the components appearHelpful habits
At homeCalm responses to sibling conflict; listening to parents; expressing needs respectfullyFamily check-ins; naming feelings; apologising and forgiving
At schoolHandling exam stress; working in groups; asking for help; supporting classmatesFeelings vocabulary; reflection journals; structured group roles
For teachersNoticing student distress; staying calm in class; giving feedback with careGreeting students; naming expectations; repairing relationships after conflict
At workTeamwork; receiving feedback; leading with fairness; serving customersActive listening; clear requests; managing stress; seeking honest feedback

Culture matters. Rules about when and how to show feelings vary between families, cultures and workplaces. In some settings, open expression of emotion is welcomed, and in others, calm restraint is a sign of respect. Emotional intelligence includes reading the situation and the people in it, rather than following one fixed style.

13.Myths and Facts About EQ

MythFact
EQ matters more than IQBoth matter, in different ways; claims of fixed percentages are not supported
You are born with a fixed EQEmotional skills develop through experience and practice, and school programmes can improve them
High EQ means always being calm and cheerfulIt means understanding and handling all feelings, including anger and sadness, in useful ways
Empathy means taking on everyone's painToo much shared distress can lead to burnout; compassion and boundaries help
Hiding your feelings is good self-controlHabitual suppression is linked with worse well-being; reappraisal and naming feelings work better
An online quiz can give your exact EQMost quizzes are unvalidated and measure self-image
High EQ people are always good peopleEmotional skill is a tool; values and character decide how it is used

14.Your Eight-Week Practice Plan

Skills grow by repetition. Spend about ten minutes a day, and choose one focus every fortnight or so.

WeeksFocusDaily action
1 to 2Self-awarenessEvening journal: name three feelings from the day, with the cause and a precise word for each
3 to 4Self-regulationPractise the 4-6 breath once a day, and use the reappraisal question whenever something upsets you
5MotivationWrite one specific goal each Monday and record one small win each evening
6EmpathyHave one conversation where you only listen, reflect and ask open questions
7Social skillsUse one "I" statement and one clear, kind request every day
8ReviewRead your journal, note what changed, ask a trusted person for feedback, and choose your next focus

If strong feelings such as sadness, anxiety or anger stay with you for weeks or interfere with daily life, speak to a parent, teacher, school counsellor or doctor. That is a sign of good emotional judgement, not of weakness.

A Closing Thought

Emotional intelligence is not about being soft, or about always being calm and kind. It is about noticing what is happening inside you and between you and other people, and choosing responses you will not regret. The five components give you a map: know yourself, steer yourself, keep going, understand others and work well with them. Be wary of big promises, enjoy the real benefits, and practise one small skill each day.
