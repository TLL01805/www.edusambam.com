Operations of Integers: Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication & Division

Four operations, a few sign rules, and one skill that unlocks all of it: knowing exactly when the answer is positive or negative, and why.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|16 min read|Last updated: November 2026|Mathematics

A night temperature of −3°C. A bank account that is Rs 500 overdrawn. A lift that goes down to the second basement. Each of these uses a negative number. Almost every mistake with integers comes from one thing: losing track of signs. This guide explains the four operations (add, subtract, multiply, divide) in plain words first, then gives the exact rule, then shows why the rule is true, so you can remember it instead of guessing.

1.Integers, Opposites and the Number Line

Integers are whole numbers and their negatives: …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, … There are no fractions or decimals. Zero is an integer and is neither positive nor negative. (For where integers sit among other kinds of number, see Number Systems.)

On a number line, numbers to the right of zero are positive and numbers to the left are negative. A number further right is always greater. That means −2 is greater than −7, even though 7 looks “bigger”. So −7 < −2 < 0 < 3.

Two numbers that are the same distance from zero on opposite sides are opposites: 5 and −5, or −12 and 12. The opposite of 0 is 0. The absolute value of a number is its distance from zero, ignoring the sign. It is written with bars: |7| = 7 and |−7| = 7. Absolute value is never negative. We will use it because every sign rule below compares distances from zero.

A minus sign can mean two different things. In 8 − 3 it is an operation (take away). In −3 it is part of the number (negative three). Putting a negative number in brackets, as in 8 − (−3), keeps the two jobs apart.

2.Adding Integers

Think of adding as moving along the number line. Adding a positive number moves you right. Adding a negative number moves you left. The number you start at is where you stand, and the answer is where you land.

Figure 1. Start at 3 and add −7. Move 7 steps left and land on −4.

The number line works for small numbers. For bigger numbers, use the rule:

CaseRule
Same signsAdd the absolute values and keep the shared sign.
Different signsSubtract the smaller absolute value from the larger. Keep the sign of the number with the larger absolute value.

Why the rule works: zero pairs

Picture a positive chip (+) and a negative chip (−). One of each cancels out and makes zero, like earning Rs 1 and then owing Rs 1. We call this a zero pair. To add (−5) + 3, lay out five negative chips and three positive chips. Three zero pairs cancel, and two negative chips are left. So (−5) + 3 = −2.

Figure 2. Each positive chip cancels one negative chip. The two chips that are left give the answer.
Example 1 · Same signs

(−4) + (−7). Both are negative, so add the absolute values: 4 + 7 = 11. Keep the negative sign: −11. (Owe Rs 4, then owe Rs 7 more: you owe Rs 11.)

Example 2 · Different signs, negative answer

9 + (−15). The signs differ. The absolute values are 9 and 15. Subtract: 15 − 9 = 6. The larger absolute value belongs to −15, so the answer is negative: −6. Check on the number line: start at 9 and move 15 left to land on −6.

Example 3 · Different signs, positive answer

(−8) + 12. The absolute values are 8 and 12. Subtract: 12 − 8 = 4. The larger absolute value belongs to +12, so the answer is 4.

Example 4 · A real situation

At night a hill station is at −3°C. By afternoon the temperature has risen by 11 degrees. The new temperature is (−3) + 11 = 8°C. Another check: (−47) + 38. The absolute values are 47 and 38, the difference is 9, and 47 is the larger and is negative, so the answer is −9.

“9 + (−15) = 24, because 9 + 15 = 24”

This adds the absolute values as if both signs matched. They do not.

The fix: when the signs differ you subtract. The answer is 9 + (−15) = −6. A quick check: adding a negative number can never make the total bigger, so an answer of 24 (bigger than 9) cannot be right.

3.Subtracting Integers

There is no separate subtraction rule to memorise. Subtracting a number is the same as adding its opposite. Rewrite the subtraction as an addition, then use the addition rules.

Why is this true? Look at this pattern, where each line takes away one less than the line before:

CalculationAnswer
5 − 32
5 − 23
5 − 14
5 − 05
5 − (−1)6
5 − (−2)7

The answers go up by 1 each time, so the pattern must continue: taking away −1 gives 6, which is the same as 5 + 1. Taking away a debt makes you richer.

Example 5 · Subtracting a negative

7 − (−5) = 7 + 5 = 12.

Example 6 · Subtracting a positive

(−10) − 6 = (−10) + (−6) = −16. Also 5 − 12 = 5 + (−12) = −7.

Example 7 · Negative minus negative

(−3) − (−9) = (−3) + 9 = 6. And (−15) − (−15) = (−15) + 15 = 0, because anything minus itself is zero.

Example 8 · Finding a difference

The highest temperature in a week is 14°C and the lowest is −6°C. The range is 14 − (−6) = 14 + 6 = 20°C. In the same way, a bird at +30 m above sea level and a diver at −12 m are 30 − (−12) = 42 m apart. A difference is always “larger minus smaller”.

“5 − (−3) = 2”

The two minus signs were treated as if they were one.

The fix: the first minus is the operation (take away) and the second is the sign of the number. “Take away negative 3” means “add the opposite”: 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8. Test it with a pattern: 5 − 3 = 2, 5 − 2 = 3, 5 − 1 = 4 and so on, so 5 − (−3) must be bigger than 5, not smaller.

4.Multiplying Integers

Multiplication is repeated addition. 3 × (−2) means three lots of −2: (−2) + (−2) + (−2) = −6. So a positive times a negative is negative. What about a negative times a negative? Use a pattern again. Keep the second number as −2 and lower the first number by 1 each line:

CalculationAnswer
3 × (−2)−6
2 × (−2)−4
1 × (−2)−2
0 × (−2)0
(−1) × (−2)2
(−2) × (−2)4

The answers rise by 2 each time, so (−1) × (−2) must be +2 and (−2) × (−2) must be +4. That is why two negatives make a positive. The rule depends only on whether the two signs match:

Figure 3. Same signs give a positive answer. Different signs give a negative answer. The grid pulses cell by cell.
Example 9 · Two numbers

(a) (−6) × (−7): same signs, so positive: 42. (b) 8 × (−9): different signs, so negative: −72. First multiply the absolute values (6 × 7 and 8 × 9), then decide the sign.

More than two numbers

Count the negative signs. An even number of negatives gives a positive answer. An odd number gives a negative answer. Each negative flips the sign once, like pressing a light switch: two presses return it to how it began, three presses leave it the other way.

Example 10 · Counting negatives

(a) (−3) × (−2) × (−4) has three negatives (odd). 3 × 2 × 4 = 24, so the answer is −24.

(b) (−2) × (−2) × (−2) × (−2) has four negatives (even). 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16, so the answer is 16.

(c) Multiplying −1 by itself five times gives −1 (five negatives, odd).

“(−3) × (−2) × (−4) = 24, because negatives cancel”

Negatives cancel in pairs. A leftover one stays.

The fix: work in pairs. (−3) × (−2) = 6 (a pair cancels). Then 6 × (−4) = −24 (one negative is left over). Three negatives is an odd number, so the final sign is negative.

5.Dividing Integers

Division is the opposite of multiplication, so it follows the same sign rule: same signs give a positive answer, different signs give a negative answer. A useful check is to multiply your answer by the divisor and see if you get back to the start.

Example 11 · Division

(a) (−36) ÷ 6 = −6 (different signs). Check: (−6) × 6 = −36 ✓.

(b) (−48) ÷ (−8) = 6 (same signs). Check: 6 × (−8) = −48 ✓.

(c) 72 ÷ (−8) = −9. (d) 0 ÷ (−5) = 0.

Division by zero is not allowed. There is no number that, multiplied by 0, gives 7, so 7 ÷ 0 has no answer. Also, dividing integers does not always give an integer: (−7) ÷ 2 = −3.5, which is not an integer (it is still a correct answer).

6.All the Sign Rules in One Table

OperationSame signsDifferent signs
AdditionAdd the absolute values, keep the signSubtract, keep the sign of the larger absolute value
SubtractionAdd the opposite, then use the addition rule
MultiplicationPositiveNegative
DivisionPositiveNegative

7.Brackets, Powers and Order of Operations

When an expression has several operations, follow the order BODMAS: Brackets, Orders (powers and roots), Division and Multiplication (left to right), then Addition and Subtraction (left to right). With negative numbers one trap catches nearly everyone.

“−3² = 9”

This is one of the most common traps in school mathematics.

The fix: without brackets, the power applies only to the 3. So −3² means −(3²) = −9. To square negative three itself you need brackets: (−3)² = (−3) × (−3) = 9. Brackets change the answer, so always notice them.

Powers of negative numbers follow the sign-counting rule: an even power gives a positive answer and an odd power gives a negative answer. (−2)³ = −8, (−2)⁴ = 16, but −2⁴ = −(2⁴) = −16. Also (−1)¹⁰⁰ = 1 and (−1)¹⁰¹ = −1.

A minus sign in front of a bracket changes every sign inside it, or, equivalently, it finds the opposite of the result: −(3 − 8) = −(−5) = 5.

Example 12 · Multi-step

Work out −8 + 3 × (−2) − (−6) ÷ 2.

Step 1 (× and ÷ first): 3 × (−2) = −6 and (−6) ÷ 2 = −3.

Step 2 (rewrite): −8 + (−6) − (−3).

Step 3 (left to right): −8 + (−6) = −14, then −14 − (−3) = −14 + 3 = −11.

Example 13 · Brackets first

Work out (−5 + 2) × (−3) − 4. Brackets: −5 + 2 = −3. Multiply: (−3) × (−3) = 9. Subtract: 9 − 4 = 5.

Example 14 · Powers and mixed signs

(a) 12 − 3 × (−4) + (−18) ÷ (−3). Multiplication and division first: 3 × (−4) = −12 and (−18) ÷ (−3) = 6. Then 12 − (−12) + 6 = 12 + 12 + 6 = 30.

(b) (−2)³ + 5 × (−4) − (−7). Power first: (−2)³ = −8. Then 5 × (−4) = −20. So −8 + (−20) − (−7) = −28 + 7 = −21.

(c) Compare 4 − (−3)² = 4 − 9 = −5 with (4 − (−3))² = 7² = 49. Same digits, different brackets, very different answers.

Calculator tip. To enter a negative number, use the (−) key, not the subtraction key, and put negative numbers in brackets when you raise them to a power. If you type −3² and the calculator shows −9, it is following the same BODMAS rule as above.

8.Integers in Real Life

  • Money. A bank balance of Rs −2 000 (overdrawn) plus a deposit of Rs 5 000 and a payment of Rs 3 500 gives −2 000 + 5 000 − 3 500 = Rs −500. You still owe Rs 500.
  • Temperature. If the temperature starts at 6°C and drops 4°C every hour for 5 hours, it becomes 6 + 5 × (−4) = −14°C.
  • Depth and height. A submarine at −40 m rises 25 m and then dives 18 m: −40 + 25 − 18 = −33 m.
  • Lifts. A lift starts at floor 2, goes down 7 floors, up 4 and down 6. The final floor is 2 − 7 + 4 − 6 = −7, which is the seventh basement.
  • Science and computing. Electric charge, elevation below sea level, and the coordinates used in graphs and games all use negative numbers.

9.Common Mistakes

  • Adding when the signs differ. 9 + (−15) is not 24. When signs differ, subtract.
  • Reading − as one thing. In 5 − (−3) there are two jobs for minus signs: take away, and negative three.
  • Thinking all negatives cancel. Only pairs cancel. Count them.
  • −3² versus (−3)². The power only reaches what is directly next to it.
  • Using the multiplication rule for addition. (−4) + (−7) is −11, not 28. The “two negatives make a positive” rule belongs to multiplying and dividing only.
  • Ignoring the order of operations. −8 + 3 × (−2) is −14, not −10 (multiply before you add).
  • Dividing by zero. This is never allowed.

11.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] Work out (a) (−9) + 4 and (b) 3 − (−8).
  2. [2 marks] Work out (a) (−7) × (−6) and (b) 56 ÷ (−8).
  3. [2 marks] At 6 a.m. the temperature was −7°C. At 2 p.m. it was 9°C. Find the rise in temperature.
  4. [3 marks] Calculate 5 − 3 × (−2) + (−12) ÷ 4.
  5. [3 marks] Write down the value of (a) (−4)², (b) −4², (c) (−4)³.
  6. [3 marks] The product of three integers is −30. Two of the integers are −2 and 3. Find the third integer.
  7. [3 marks] A lift is at floor 2. It goes down 7 floors, then up 4 floors, then down 6 floors. Which floor is it at now?
Show answers

1. (a) −5 (b) 3 + 8 = 11.

2. (a) 42 (b) −7.

3. 9 − (−7) = 9 + 7 = 16°C.

4. Multiply and divide first: 3 × (−2) = −6 and (−12) ÷ 4 = −3. Then 5 − (−6) + (−3) = 5 + 6 − 3 = 8.

5. (a) 16 (b) −16 (c) −64.

6. (−2) × 3 = −6, so the third integer is −30 ÷ (−6) = 5. Check: (−2) × 3 × 5 = −30 ✓.

7. 2 − 7 + 4 − 6 = −7, the seventh basement.

12.Key Points

  • A number further right on the number line is greater. |x| is the distance from zero.
  • Adding a negative moves left. Same signs: add and keep the sign. Different signs: subtract and keep the sign of the larger absolute value.
  • Subtracting a number is adding its opposite: 7 − (−5) = 7 + 5.
  • Multiplying or dividing: same signs give positive, different signs give negative. With many factors, an even number of negatives gives a positive.
  • Division by zero is not allowed. 0 ÷ n = 0.
  • BODMAS applies. −3² = −9 but (−3)² = 9.

13.For Teachers and Parents

Negative numbers feel abstract until they are attached to something physical. Start with a floor-marked number line (a strip of tape on the floor works well) and let learners walk the additions: face the positive direction, step forwards for a positive number and backwards for a negative one. Use the context of temperature, floors in a building or money owed, and move to chips for zero pairs when the numbers get bigger. For multiplication, patterns (like the tables above) give a reason for the rule, which sticks longer than a chant. Insist on brackets around negative numbers while learners are still building the habit, and encourage a quick estimate of the sign of the answer before any calculation.
