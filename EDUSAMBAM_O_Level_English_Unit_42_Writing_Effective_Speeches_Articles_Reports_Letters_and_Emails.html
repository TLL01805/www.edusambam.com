Writing Effective Speeches, Articles, Reports, Letters & Emails

A comprehensive Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 unit on directed writing: analysing purpose and audience, selecting an appropriate form, controlling register, organising information and producing effective speeches, articles, reports, letters and emails.

Unit 42 focuses on the directed-writing forms identified for Cambridge O Level English Language 1123: speeches, articles, reports, letters and emails. The central principle is simple: the writer must use, develop and evaluate the information provided in the task, while making the response appropriate to its purpose, audience, form and context.

1. What This Unit Covers

Cambridge's current syllabus states that Paper 2 Section A is a compulsory directed-writing task. Candidates read one or two source texts and use, develop and evaluate the information in them to create a discursive, argumentative or persuasive speech, email, report, letter or article. The syllabus specifies a response of about 250–350 words and assesses both writing and reading skills.

This means directed writing is not simply a test of whether you can produce grammatically correct English. You must make decisions about what to say, why you are saying it, who will receive it and which form will communicate it effectively.

Cambridge assessment focus
Paper 2 Section A combines W1–W5 writing objectives with R3 and R5. In practical terms, you must communicate ideas, organise them deliberately, choose appropriate vocabulary and sentence structures, use an appropriate register, write accurately, evaluate source information and select information for a specific purpose.

2. Directed Writing Is a Reading-and-Writing Task

A common mistake is to treat the source text as something to read once and then ignore. Cambridge explicitly requires candidates to use, develop and evaluate information from the source text or texts. Therefore, strong directed writing begins with careful reading.

Before drafting, identify the main facts, viewpoints, reasons, examples and implications in the source. Then decide which information is useful for your particular audience and purpose. You should not copy the source mechanically. You need to transform relevant information into a new response suited to the task.

3. Start With Purpose

Purpose answers the question: What am I trying to achieve? A speech may aim to persuade or inform. An article may inform, explain, entertain or argue. A report may present findings and recommendations. A letter or email may request action, explain a situation, complain, advise or communicate information.

The purpose affects the content and language. If the purpose is persuasion, reasons and supporting evidence need to lead the reader or listener towards a position. If the purpose is to report findings, information must be organised clearly and separated from unsupported opinion.

Purpose test
Complete this sentence before writing: “When my reader/listener finishes this response, I want them to understand, believe, decide or do ______.” If you cannot complete it clearly, your writing plan is probably not yet focused.

4. Identify the Audience

Audience affects content, explanation, tone and register. British Council TeachingEnglish identifies audience as a major factor influencing register, while Cambridge expects candidates to use an appropriate register for context.

Ask who will receive the response and what that person or group already knows. A school principal, a group of students, members of the public, a friend and a local organisation may all require different choices. The same information can be expressed in different ways without changing its factual basis.

Do not write for an imaginary examiner instead of the audience named in the task. The examiner is assessing whether your response behaves like the requested form and addresses the intended reader or listener.

5. Purpose, Audience and Form Work Together

Purpose, audience and form should not be treated as three unrelated boxes. They interact. A formal report for a school committee, a speech to students and an email to a friend might concern exactly the same event, yet the organisation, vocabulary, tone and conventions would differ.

British Council guidance on genre similarly emphasises purpose, audience, content and form. This is why memorising one generic “good English” style is not enough for directed writing.

6. Understand What Cambridge Means by Form

Cambridge lists speech, email, report, letter and article among the possible directed-writing forms, with the task also specifying whether the writing should be discursive, argumentative or persuasive. The form tells you what kind of communication you are producing.

Form does not mean decoration. A heading, greeting, sign-off or other feature should be used when it belongs naturally to the requested genre. Cambridge's guidance specifically notes that examiners look for an understanding of the conventions and features of the required text type.

7. Speech Writing: Write for the Ear

A speech is intended to be heard. The writer therefore needs a clear progression that an audience can follow in real time. British Council guidance on public speaking stresses the importance of considering the audience and the purpose of the speech.

A practical structure is:

  • opening that establishes the subject and engages the audience;
  • clear development of the main points;
  • supporting examples, reasons or source-based information;
  • a strong final message, recommendation or call to action when appropriate.

Direct address can be useful when it suits the context: “Many of us have experienced…” or “Consider what would happen if…”. Questions can also focus attention, but they should serve the argument rather than become empty decoration.

8. Speech Tone and Register

A speech is not automatically informal. Its register depends on the audience and context. A school speech to classmates may be more conversational than a formal speech to a governing body. The writer should therefore ask: Who is listening? What relationship exists between speaker and audience? What does the speaker need the audience to think or do?

Use spoken-friendly sentence structures without sacrificing accuracy. Avoid writing every sentence as though it were a private conversation unless the context requires that level of informality.

9. Article Writing: Give the Reader a Reason to Continue

An article needs a clear subject and a reader-centred structure. Depending on the task, an article may explain, inform, discuss, argue or persuade. The exact purpose comes from the prompt.

A useful structure is an engaging opening, focused development of the central ideas, and a conclusion that leaves the reader with a clear final impression. A title or heading may be appropriate when the context suggests one. Paragraphs should have distinct jobs rather than becoming a sequence of disconnected points.

Article test
After each paragraph ask: “Why does the reader need this paragraph?” If the answer is unclear, the paragraph may need a stronger purpose or a clearer connection to the task.

10. Reports: Inform Clearly and Selectively

Reports are normally organised to help a reader locate information efficiently. Cambridge's directed-writing guidance specifically gives reports as one of the required forms and notes that a report might use headings.

A report may contain a clear title and headings when appropriate to the task. Information should be grouped logically: for example, findings, problems, evidence and recommendations may need separate treatment when the task calls for them.

Do not confuse a report with a stream of personal impressions. If the source provides facts and views, distinguish between what is established, what people said and what you recommend.

11. Reports: Findings and Recommendations

When a task asks for recommendations, they should follow logically from the information presented. A recommendation is stronger when the preceding discussion establishes the problem and provides a reason for the proposed action.

A useful chain is finding → significance → recommendation. For example, if a source indicates that students avoid a facility because it is difficult to access, a recommendation should address access rather than introduce an unrelated improvement.

12. Letter Writing: Relationship Matters

A letter's register depends heavily on the relationship between writer and recipient. Cambridge's guidance gives the letter as one of the possible forms and specifically notes that a letter should use appropriate conventions such as a salutation and correct form of valediction.

A formal letter may require a more controlled register, while a personal letter may allow a more conversational style. The correct choice comes from the situation in the task, not from a universal rule that every letter must sound formal.

13. Formal Letters: Purpose and Convention

For a formal letter, the opening should establish the reason for writing. The body should develop the required information in a logical sequence, and the ending should make the intended action or outcome clear when relevant.

Do not spend most of the response on polite introductory phrases. The content marks depend on what you communicate and how effectively you develop it. Convention supports the communication; it does not replace it.

14. Informal and Semi-Formal Letters

Not every letter has the same level of formality. A letter to a close friend may use a personal voice and contractions, while a letter to someone known but not closely familiar may require a more neutral register.

The safest approach is to identify the relationship described in the prompt and choose language that fits it. Register should remain consistent throughout the response.

15. Email Writing: Context Before Decoration

Email is a communication channel, not a single fixed register. An email to a friend and an email to an organisation can differ greatly in tone. Cambridge includes email among the possible directed-writing forms, so the candidate must respond to the context given in the question.

Use a clear opening, organise the main information logically and finish appropriately. If the task context makes a subject line or greeting natural, use it. Do not add invented technical features simply to make the response look like a modern email.

16. Register: Formal, Neutral or Informal?

Register is the degree and type of formality appropriate to a context. British Council TeachingEnglish commonly describes formal, neutral and informal choices as useful working categories.

Register affects vocabulary, sentence patterns, contractions, greetings, requests and degree of directness. A formal request might use “I would be grateful if…”, while a message to a close friend could be much more direct. The important point is appropriacy, not artificial difficulty.

17. Avoid “Fancy English”

W3 rewards vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context. A difficult word is not automatically a better word. If a supposedly sophisticated expression is inaccurate, unnatural or unsuitable for the audience, it can weaken communication.

Choose words for precision. Use a verb that communicates the action accurately, a noun that identifies the idea clearly and modifiers only when they add meaning. Controlled vocabulary is more valuable than decoration.

18. Use the Source — But Do Not Copy It

The directed-writing task gives you source material for a reason. Cambridge requires candidates to use, develop and evaluate the information. This means the source should influence the content of your response, but the response must still be your own organised communication.

A strong method is select → transform → develop → connect. Select information relevant to the task, transform it into the requested form, develop important points with explanation or evaluation, and connect the material into a coherent response.

19. Develop Information Instead of Listing It

Listing source points can show selection, but development requires more. If a source states that a facility is underused, a developed response might explain why that matters, connect it with another relevant point, or use it as a basis for a recommendation, depending on the task.

Development should remain faithful to the source. Do not invent statistics, quotations, events or claims and present them as though they came from the source.

20. Evaluate Information

Cambridge's R3 objective requires candidates to analyse, evaluate and develop facts, ideas and opinions, using appropriate support from the text. Evaluation means going beyond repetition.

You may need to consider importance, reliability, consequences, strengths, weaknesses, priorities or competing viewpoints, depending on the task. Evaluation should be connected to the information provided rather than based on unsupported assumptions.

21. Organise for Deliberate Effect

W2 concerns organising and structuring ideas and opinions for deliberate effect. In directed writing, organisation should help the reader or listener understand the message without having to reconstruct it.

A simple planning framework is:

  • Opening: establish purpose and context.
  • Development: group related information and ideas.
  • Evaluation: explain significance, consequences or competing views where required.
  • Final section: give the appropriate conclusion, recommendation or call to action.

22. Paragraphs Should Have a Job

Each paragraph should contribute something identifiable. A paragraph may introduce a problem, explain evidence, present a reason, evaluate an idea, provide a recommendation or move the argument forward.

One practical test is to write a three-to-five-word label beside each paragraph during planning. If two paragraphs have exactly the same job, combine or differentiate them.

23. Linking Ideas

Organisation does not require a linking word at the beginning of every paragraph. Ideas can be connected through repetition of key concepts, logical sequence, pronoun reference, contrast, cause and effect, and carefully chosen transitions.

Use linking expressions because the relationship between ideas requires them. Avoid mechanical chains such as Firstly, secondly, thirdly, finally when the ideas would be clearer through a more natural structure.

24. Persuasion in Speeches, Articles and Letters

When a task is persuasive, identify the position you want the audience to consider and build reasons that support it. Evidence from the source can strengthen the case. Anticipating a reasonable opposing view can also demonstrate evaluation when relevant.

Persuasion is not the same as shouting or repeating “I strongly believe”. A controlled persuasive response connects claims to reasons and evidence and maintains an appropriate register.

25. Discursive Writing: Consider More Than One Side

A discursive response considers ideas or viewpoints rather than simply asserting one position. Where the task invites discussion, organise relevant advantages, disadvantages, arguments or perspectives and reach an appropriate conclusion.

Balance does not necessarily mean giving every side exactly the same number of words. The response should reflect the task and the importance of the evidence available.

26. Accuracy Still Matters

W5 requires accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar. This remains important even when the main task is about source use and form.

Leave time to check sentence boundaries, verb forms, agreement, spelling, punctuation, paragraphing and confusing word choices. Do not spend the entire final minute searching for one advanced word while obvious errors remain.

27. A Reliable Examination Planning Method

Use a short planning sequence before writing:

  1. Read the task twice.
  2. Underline the purpose, audience, form and required content.
  3. Read the source text carefully.
  4. Mark the information you need.
  5. Group the selected points into two to four main sections.
  6. Decide where evaluation, development or recommendations belong.
  7. Choose the register and overall tone.
  8. Begin writing with a clear sense of destination.

The plan should be brief. Its purpose is to improve organisation, not to become a second essay.

28. A Form-by-Form Quick Guide

Speech: audience-aware opening, clear spoken progression, purposeful address, appropriate persuasive/informative tone, strong ending.

Article: reader-focused opening, clear topic, developed paragraphs, appropriate heading/title when suitable, coherent conclusion.

Report: clear purpose, organised findings, headings where useful, precise information, recommendations when requested.

Letter: appropriate salutation and ending, relationship-sensitive register, clear purpose, logically developed body.

Email: context-appropriate opening and closing, clear communication, suitable register and efficient organisation.

29. Common Examination Mistakes

  • Writing a generic essay instead of the requested form.
  • Ignoring the named audience.
  • Copying source sentences instead of transforming the information.
  • Using every source point whether relevant or not.
  • Adding invented facts and presenting them as source information.
  • Using a register that is too formal or too casual for the context.
  • Writing a report as one unstructured block.
  • Writing a speech like a silent essay with no audience awareness.
  • Spending too many words on the opening.
  • Failing to leave time for accuracy checking.

30. Final Examination Checklist

Before submitting, ask:
□ Did I answer the exact task?
□ Did I use, develop and evaluate relevant source information?
□ Did I address the intended audience?
□ Is the requested form recognisable?
□ Is my register appropriate and consistent?
□ Are ideas logically organised?
□ Have I developed rather than merely listed points?
□ Is the conclusion appropriate to the purpose?
□ Have I checked spelling, punctuation and grammar?

31. What High-Quality Directed Writing Looks Like

High-quality directed writing is controlled communication. It does not depend on memorising a model response because the source material and task will change. Instead, the writer transfers a reliable process from one context to another.

The strongest response therefore combines accurate reading, purposeful selection, thoughtful development, appropriate form, audience awareness, deliberate organisation, suitable register and accurate language.

32. Source Basis and Authenticity

This unit is grounded primarily in official Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 materials and supporting British Council TeachingEnglish guidance on genre, audience, register, public speaking and communicative writing. The Cambridge syllabus is the authority for examination requirements; the supporting teaching resources are used only to clarify general writing principles and genre awareness.

Key takeaway
In directed writing, do not ask only, “Can I write good English?” Ask: “What is my purpose, who is my audience, what form am I producing, what source information must I use and how can I organise it for deliberate effect?”
