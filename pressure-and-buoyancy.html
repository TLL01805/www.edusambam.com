Pressure and Buoyancy: Why Some Things Float and Others Sink

Why skis stop you sinking, why divers feel more pressure with depth, and why steel ships float: pressure, hydraulics and Archimedes' principle, explained with diagrams and worked examples.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 15 min read | Science | Last updated: October 2026

A person can stand on thin ice if they lie flat, but may fall through if they stand up. A sharp knife cuts bread easily while a blunt one only squashes it. A huge steel ship floats while a small steel nail sinks. All three puzzles are answered by one idea: what matters is not just the size of a force, but how that force is spread out. This article explains pressure in solids, liquids and gases, shows why the sea gets more crushing with depth, and uses Archimedes' principle to explain why things float or sink.

1.What Pressure Is

Pressure is the force acting on each unit of area, perpendicular to a surface.

Formula

p = F ÷ A  (pressure = force ÷ area)

The SI unit is the pascal (Pa): 1 Pa = 1 N/m². Because this is a very small pressure, kilopascals are common: 1 kPa = 1000 Pa. Another unit you may meet is the bar (1 bar = 100 000 Pa).

Worked example 1: standing on the ground

A student of mass 60 kg stands on both feet; the soles of her shoes touch the ground over a total area of 0.040 m². Find the pressure on the ground. (g = 9.8 N/kg)

Step 1. Weight (the force on the ground) = mg = 60 × 9.8 = 588 N.

Step 2. p = F ÷ A = 588 ÷ 0.040 = 14 700 Pa ≈ 15 kPa.

Check: on one foot (half the area) the pressure doubles, to about 29 kPa.

2.Changing Pressure by Changing Area

For a fixed force, a smaller area gives a larger pressure, and a larger area gives a smaller pressure.

  • High pressure (small area): knife edges, nails, needles, drawing pins and teeth concentrate force on a tiny area so they penetrate easily.
  • Low pressure (large area): snowshoes, skis, camel feet, tractor tyres, tank tracks and wide school-bag straps spread the force so the object does not sink or dig in. Lying flat on thin ice spreads your weight over a much larger area.
Worked example 2: a sharp knife

You press down on a knife with 20 N. The cutting edge touches the food over an area of 1.0 mm² (1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m²). Find the pressure.

p = F ÷ A = 20 ÷ (1.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.0 × 10⁷ Pa (20 MPa).

This is more than a thousand times the pressure of the student standing on the ground, from a force of only 20 N. A blunt edge has a much larger area, so the same force gives far less pressure.

3.Pressure in Liquids

A liquid exerts pressure because of its weight and because its particles collide with surfaces. Several facts hold for a liquid at rest:

  • Pressure increases with depth: there is more liquid above pushing down.
  • Pressure at a point acts equally in all directions.
  • At the same depth in the same liquid, the pressure is the same, whatever the shape of the container. A narrow tube and a wide tank give the same pressure at equal depth.
  • A denser liquid exerts more pressure at the same depth.
Formula

p = ρ × g × h

ρ = density of the liquid (kg/m³), g = gravitational field strength (9.8 N/kg), h = depth below the surface (m). This gives the pressure due to the liquid alone (called gauge pressure). Fresh water: ρ ≈ 1000 kg/m³; sea water: ρ ≈ 1025 kg/m³.

Figure 1. Pressure in a liquid grows with depth: three times the depth means three times the pressure. This is why dam walls are thicker at the bottom. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)
Worked example 3: a diver

(a) Find the pressure due to the water on a swimmer 10 m below the surface of a freshwater lake. (b) Find the pressure on a diver 30 m deep in the sea. (g = 9.8 N/kg)

(a) p = ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 10 = 98 000 Pa ≈ 98 kPa. The air above adds about 101 kPa, so the total (absolute) pressure is about 199 kPa, nearly twice normal air pressure.

(b) p = 1025 × 9.8 × 30 = 301 350 Pa ≈ 301 kPa, roughly three atmospheres from the water alone.

As a useful rule, every 10 m of depth in water adds about one atmosphere of pressure. This is why deep divers need special training and equipment, and why submarines have strong hulls.

4.Atmospheric Pressure

We live at the bottom of an "ocean" of air. The weight of the air above us creates atmospheric pressure. At sea level the standard value is 101 325 Pa (about 101 kPa, or 1 atmosphere). It does not crush us because the fluids inside our bodies push outwards with the same pressure.

In 1643 the Italian scientist Evangelista Torricelli filled a glass tube with mercury and inverted it in a dish of mercury. The mercury fell to a height of about 760 mm and stayed there, leaving an almost empty space above it. The weight of the column of mercury is balanced by the atmospheric pressure pushing on the surface of the dish. This is the first mercury barometer.

Worked example 4: the barometer

Mercury has a density of about 13 600 kg/m³. Calculate the pressure of a 0.760 m column of mercury.

p = ρgh = 13 600 × 9.8 × 0.760 ≈ 101 300 Pa, matching atmospheric pressure at sea level.

A water barometer would need a tube over 10 m tall, which is why mercury is used.

Atmospheric pressure explains many everyday things. When you drink through a straw, you lower the pressure inside the straw, and the higher pressure of the atmosphere pushes the liquid up. A rubber suction cup sticks because the air is pushed out and the outside air presses it on. In the 1650s Otto von Guericke showed the strength of atmospheric pressure with his famous Magdeburg hemispheres: after the air was pumped out of two joined metal hemispheres, teams of horses could not pull them apart.

Atmospheric pressure decreases with height, because there is less air above. It falls to about half of its sea-level value at around 5.5 km, and to roughly a third at the summit of Mount Everest (8.85 km). This is why mountaineers need extra oxygen at great heights, why aircraft cabins are pressurised, and why your ears "pop" when you climb or descend. Weather forecasters also watch changes in air pressure: falling pressure often brings clouds and rain, while rising pressure often brings fair weather.

Figure 2. Air pressure falls with height, quickly at first and then more slowly. Values follow the International Standard Atmosphere and are approximate; real pressure varies with weather. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)

5.Pascal's Principle and Hydraulics

Liquids are almost incompressible. Pascal's principle says that a pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the walls of its container. A hydraulic system uses this to multiply force. A small force on a small piston creates a pressure; the same pressure acts on a large piston and gives a large force.

Formula

F₁ ÷ A₁ = F₂ ÷ A₂  (same pressure in both cylinders)

Figure 3. A hydraulic press. The pressure is the same in both cylinders, so a larger piston area gives a larger force. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram, not to scale.)
Worked example 5: a hydraulic jack

A force of 50 N pushes a small piston of area 4.0 cm². The large piston has an area of 200 cm². What force acts on the large piston, and how far does it move when the small piston moves 25 cm?

Step 1. F₂ = F₁ × (A₂ ÷ A₁) = 50 × (200 ÷ 4.0) = 2500 N.

Step 2. The liquid volume moved is the same on both sides, so the large piston moves 25 cm ÷ 50 = 0.50 cm.

The force is multiplied by 50, but the distance is divided by 50. No energy is created: work in equals work out (50 N × 0.25 m = 12.5 J; 2500 N × 0.0050 m = 12.5 J). Car brakes, garage jacks, diggers and aircraft control systems all use hydraulics.

6.Upthrust and Archimedes' Principle

When an object is put in a fluid, the fluid pushes it upwards with a force called upthrust (or buoyant force). It exists because pressure increases with depth: the bottom of the object is deeper than the top, so the upward push on the bottom is bigger than the downward push on the top.

Archimedes' principle states that the upthrust on an object in a fluid equals the weight of the fluid it displaces. The ancient Greek scientist Archimedes of Syracuse (about 287–212 BCE) studied this idea. A popular story, recorded centuries later by the Roman writer Vitruvius, says he discovered it in a bath while checking whether a crown was pure gold. Historians treat the tale with caution, but the principle itself is correct and is used every day.

Formula

upthrust = ρfluid × g × Vdisplaced

Vdisplaced is the volume of fluid pushed out of the way, which equals the volume of the object that is under the surface.

Worked example 6: upthrust on a block

A block measures 0.20 m × 0.10 m × 0.050 m and is fully under water. Find the upthrust. If the block has a mass of 0.50 kg, will it float? (ρwater = 1000 kg/m³)

Step 1. Volume = 0.20 × 0.10 × 0.050 = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³.

Step 2. Upthrust = 1000 × 9.8 × 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 9.8 N.

Step 3. Weight = mg = 0.50 × 9.8 = 4.9 N. The upthrust (9.8 N) is greater than the weight (4.9 N), so the block floats.

Step 4. Its density is 0.50 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 500 kg/m³, which is half the density of water, so it floats with half of its volume under the surface.

7.Floating and Sinking

Compare the weight W of the object with the largest upthrust U it could get (when fully under the surface):

  • Sinks: W is greater than U, so there is a net downward force. The object's average density is greater than the fluid's.
  • Floats: the object rises until the displaced fluid weighs exactly the same as the object. Then U = W, the forces balance, and the object's average density is less than the fluid's.
  • Neutrally buoyant (suspended): U = W when fully under, and the average densities are equal. The object stays at any depth.
Figure 4. Three cases for an object in water. The floating block bobs gently to show a balanced state. The red arrow is weight; the blue arrow is upthrust. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)

This explains why a steel ship floats: its hull is hollow, so it displaces a huge volume of water. The ship's average density, counting all the air inside, is less than that of water. A solid steel nail, with an average density about eight times that of water, sinks.

Worked example 7: a ship in fresh and salt water

A ship has a total mass of 5.0 × 10⁶ kg. What volume of water must it displace to float in (a) fresh water (1000 kg/m³) and (b) sea water (1025 kg/m³)?

A floating ship's weight equals the weight of the water it displaces, so the displaced water has mass 5.0 × 10⁶ kg.

(a) V = m ÷ ρ = 5.0 × 10⁶ ÷ 1000 = 5000 m³.

(b) V = 5.0 × 10⁶ ÷ 1025 ≈ 4880 m³.

The ship needs to displace less in the denser sea water, so it floats slightly higher there.

8.Applications of Buoyancy

  • Ships and the load line: a mark on the hull, often called the Plimsoll line after the 19th-century British politician Samuel Plimsoll, shows how deep a ship may sit in different waters (fresh, salt, warm, cold) so that it does not become overloaded.
  • Submarines: they have ballast tanks. Flooding them with water increases the weight so the submarine dives; pumping the water out with compressed air makes it lighter so it rises. Its hull must resist enormous water pressure at depth.
  • Hot-air balloons and airships: air is a fluid too. A balloon rises when the weight of the air it displaces is greater than the weight of the balloon and its load. Heating the air inside makes it less dense.
  • Hydrometers: a floating glass tube sinks lower in a less dense liquid. It is used to test battery acid, milk and antifreeze.
  • Fish: many bony fish control their depth using a gas-filled swim bladder, adjusting their average density.
  • Salty water: the Dead Sea is so rich in dissolved salts (density about 1.24 g/cm³) that people float very easily on its surface.

9.Common Mistakes to Avoid

Watch out
  • Mixing up force and pressure. Pressure is force per area; the same force can give very different pressures.
  • Using cm² or mm² without converting. 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m² and 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m².
  • Thinking liquid pressure depends on the amount of liquid. It depends on depth, density and g, not on container shape or total volume.
  • "Heavy things sink; light things float." It is density that matters: a huge ship is heavy but floats; a small pebble sinks.
  • "Upthrust depends on the object's weight." It depends on the weight of the fluid displaced.
  • Forgetting atmospheric pressure. The p = ρgh equation gives the pressure from the liquid alone; add atmospheric pressure for the total.
  • "Suction pulls." A straw or suction cup works because the higher outside pressure pushes.

10.Check Your Understanding

Try each question before opening the answer.

1. A force of 500 N acts on an area of 0.25 m². Find the pressure.

p = F ÷ A = 500 ÷ 0.25 = 2000 Pa.

2. Why do skis stop a skier from sinking into deep snow?

Skis have a large area, so the skier's weight is spread out and the pressure on the snow is small.

3. Find the pressure due to water at a depth of 5.0 m. (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.8 N/kg)

p = ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 5.0 = 49 000 Pa.

4. Why are dam walls thicker at the bottom?

Pressure in water increases with depth, so the water pushes harder on the lower part of the wall, which needs to be stronger.

5. A hydraulic system has pistons of 2.0 cm² and 100 cm². What force on the large piston does a 30 N force on the small piston give?

F₂ = 30 × (100 ÷ 2.0) = 1500 N.

6. What is the upthrust on an object that displaces 0.0030 m³ of water? (g = 9.8 N/kg)

U = ρgV = 1000 × 9.8 × 0.0030 = 29.4 N.

7. Why does a steel ship float but a steel nail sink?

The ship's hull encloses a large volume of air, so it displaces a large volume of water and its average density is below that of water. The nail is solid steel with a density far greater than water.

8. Why does a mercury barometer read lower on a mountain than at sea level?

There is less air above, so atmospheric pressure is lower and supports a shorter column of mercury.

Summary

  • Pressure p = F ÷ A, measured in pascals (N/m²). A smaller area gives a larger pressure.
  • Liquid pressure p = ρgh increases with depth and acts equally in all directions.
  • Atmospheric pressure is about 101 kPa at sea level and falls with height; the barometer measures it.
  • Pascal's principle: pressure in an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally, so hydraulic systems multiply force.
  • Archimedes' principle: upthrust = weight of fluid displaced. An object floats if its average density is less than the fluid's.
For teachers and parents

Safe classroom activities: press a pencil against the skin with the point and the blunt end to feel the difference in pressure; use a plastic bottle with three holes at different heights (cover them first, then uncover) to show the water jets leaving with different strengths; use a syringe to show how hydraulic systems transmit a push; weigh an object in air and then in water with a newton meter and compare the loss with the weight of displaced water in a eureka can; test floating with a lump of plasticine formed into a ball and into a boat. Keep water spills away from electrical sockets. Numerical values are rounded; please check that they match the data booklet used by your examination board.
