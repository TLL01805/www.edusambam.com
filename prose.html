Prose: The Complete Guide

Almost everything you read — novels, news articles, emails, this very sentence — is prose. Here is what actually defines it, its major forms, and the elements that make it work.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 18 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Literature

Prose is language in its most natural shape: ordinary grammar, ordinary sentences, flowing the way we actually speak and think. It is so common that most readers never stop to ask what it actually is, until they compare it with poetry.

This guide defines prose precisely, and then looks at it from every useful angle: how it differs from poetry (and where the line blurs), the two great categories of fiction and nonfiction, the types of fiction by length and by genre, the types of nonfiction, the core elements of prose fiction, point of view, the styles prose can take, and the language techniques that give prose its rhythm and force. It ends with a method for analysing a prose passage, two worked examples, a checklist, common mistakes and ten practice questions.

It opens the Literary Forms series and leads into Poetry: The Complete Guide. It also connects with Story Structure, Show, Don't Tell and Types of Essays.

1.What Is Prose?

Prose is written or spoken language in its ordinary form, organised into sentences and paragraphs and following the natural grammatical flow of the language. It does not rely on the deliberate line breaks, regular metre or stanza structure that define most poetry.

Example

The rain had not stopped for three days. Sarah watched it from the kitchen window, wondering whether the roads would still be passable by morning.

These are ordinary sentences in ordinary grammar, arranged into a paragraph. That is prose. Novels, news reports, textbooks, emails, speeches and diaries are all prose. Because it is the default shape of written language, prose is the form you will read and write most often.

2.Prose vs. Poetry: The Core Difference, and Where It Blurs

The clearest way to understand prose is by contrast with poetry.

FeatureProsePoetry
StructureSentences and paragraphsLines and stanzas
RhythmNatural, shaped by sentence length and punctuationOften deliberate, in a regular metre
Line breaksDecided by the margin or page widthChosen by the poet for effect
RhymeRare, and usually avoidedOften present, though not required
Typical aimTo tell, explain or argue clearlyTo compress feeling and image into intense language

The two forms overlap more than this table suggests. A prose poem is written in sentences without line breaks, yet uses the heightened imagery and sound of poetry. Great prose writers use rhythm, imagery and repetition with a poet's care. And drama can be written in either prose or verse. Treat the table as a guide to the typical case, not a wall. Our guide to poetry explores the other side of this comparison in detail.

3.The Two Major Categories: Fiction and Nonfiction

All prose falls into one of two broad categories, according to a single question: is it imagined, or is it real?

CategoryMeaningExamples
FictionComes from the imagination: invented characters, events and settings, even when inspired by real lifeNovels, short stories, novellas
NonfictionBased on real people, events, ideas and factsEssays, biographies, journalism, speeches

The boundary can be blurred. A novel may be set in a real city and a real war. A work labelled "based on a true story" mixes the two. Creative nonfiction uses the techniques of storytelling (scenes, dialogue, vivid description) to tell true stories. Even so, the distinction matters, because readers expect different promises: fiction promises a convincing story, nonfiction promises that what it states as fact is true.

4.Types of Fiction by Length

Fiction is often classified by length. There is no single official definition, which is worth knowing because many websites state one set of figures as if it were a law. The Science Fiction and Fantasy Writers of America (SFWA), whose Nebula Awards are one of the best-known references, define the categories this way:

Fiction by length
One common set of definitions. Bars are not to scale.

Diagram 1. Source: the SFWA Nebula Awards rules. Flash fiction has no single agreed limit.

  • Novel: a long, fully developed fictional narrative with a complex plot and several characters.
  • Novella: shorter than a novel but longer than a short story, often focused on one tightly developed idea.
  • Short story: a compact, complete narrative built around a single conflict or moment.
  • Flash fiction: an extremely short story that captures a complete arc in a tiny space. Different magazines set different limits, often in the hundreds of words.

Other organisations use other figures. One fantasy-awards body, for example, treats a novella as between 10,000 and 40,000 words and a short story as anything under 10,000. So when you see a word count in a textbook or website, treat it as a rule of thumb, and use the guidelines of the competition, exam or publisher you are working for.

5.Fiction Genres

Length is only one way to classify fiction. Genre classifies by content and conventions: what kind of story it is and what readers expect from it.

GenreTypical features
Realistic fictionInvented people in believable, everyday settings
Historical fictionA story set in a real past period
Mystery and crimeA puzzle or crime to be solved, clues and suspects
Science fictionImagined science, technology, space or future societies
FantasyMagic, invented worlds and creatures
RomanceA central love story
Horror and thrillerFear, suspense and danger
AdventureJourneys, risks and action

A single work can be several at once, such as a historical mystery or a science-fiction romance. Genre tells the reader what promise the story is making.

6.Types of Nonfiction Prose

TypeDescription
EssayA focused piece exploring one idea, argument or reflection (see Types of Essays)
BiographyThe account of a person's life, written by someone else
AutobiographyThe account of a person's whole life, written by that person
MemoirA personal account of specific memories or one period, rather than a whole life
JournalismFactual reporting of current events, aiming at clarity and accuracy
SpeechProse written to be spoken aloud, often to persuade or mark an occasion
Letters and diariesPersonal records of events and thoughts, written in the moment
Travel writing and reviewsAccounts of places, or evaluations of books, films or products
Academic and technical proseResearch reports, textbooks and manuals that explain with precision

Nonfiction is not automatically neutral. A memoir records one person's memory, and journalism involves choices about what to include. Responsible readers ask who is writing, why, and how they know what they claim. See Evidence, Citations & Plagiarism.

7.The Core Elements of Prose Fiction

Strong fictional prose is built from five connected elements. Each is explored further in the Creative Writing series.

ElementWhat it does
PlotThe sequence of events, often built on a beginning, middle and end (see Story Structure)
CharacterThe people who drive and experience the events
SettingThe time and place the story unfolds in, and the mood it creates
Point of viewWho tells the story, and from what position
ThemeThe underlying idea the story explores (not the same as the plot)

Two further ingredients bind them together: conflict, the struggle that gives the plot its energy, and voice, the distinctive personality of the narration.

8.Point of View in Prose

Point of view decides how much the reader is allowed to know, and through whose eyes. The same moment can feel entirely different depending on the choice.

How much does the narrator know?
Longer bar means more access to minds.

Diagram 2. Second person ("you") addresses the reader as a character. It is rarer.

Point of viewExample
First personI opened the door slowly, unsure of what I would find.
Second personYou open the door slowly, unsure of what you will find.
Third person limitedShe opened the door slowly, unsure of what she would find.
Third person omniscientShe opened the door slowly. Behind it, her brother held his breath, terrified she would notice him.
Third person objectiveShe opened the door slowly. Her brother stood very still behind it.

A first-person narrator can also be an unreliable narrator, someone whose account the reader gradually learns not to trust, because they are mistaken, biased or hiding something. Choosing the point of view is one of the most important decisions a prose writer makes.

9.Styles and Purposes of Prose

Beyond fiction and nonfiction, prose can be classified by its underlying purpose, which is the same idea as the essay modes (see Types of Essays).

  • Narrative prose tells a story, with events unfolding over time (most fiction, memoirs).
  • Descriptive prose paints a vivid picture using sensory detail, often slowing the pace.
  • Expository prose explains or informs, aiming for clarity above style (textbooks, news reports, how-to guides).
  • Persuasive prose aims to convince, using argument and evidence (opinion pieces, speeches).
StyleSame idea, four ways
NarrativeI walked into the storm without a coat, and by the time I reached home, I was soaked through.
DescriptiveRain hammered the pavement in cold, relentless sheets, soaking through fabric within seconds.
ExpositoryHeavy rainfall can rapidly saturate clothing, causing a significant drop in body temperature.
PersuasiveNo one should have to walk home in weather this severe; better shelters are clearly needed.

10.The Language of Prose: Rhythm, Diction and Dialogue

Prose has no metre, yet skilled writers shape its rhythm on purpose, through sentence length, punctuation and word choice, to control pace and emotion.

Sentence length controls pace
Bar length shows the number of words.

Diagram 3. Short sentences quicken the pace and raise tension; long sentences slow it and create calm.

  • Diction (word choice) sets the tone: glanced, stared and glared all mean "looked", but feel very different.
  • Figurative language such as simile and metaphor works in prose just as in poetry.
  • Dialogue brings characters to life. By convention, a new speaker starts a new paragraph, and the spoken words are placed inside quotation marks.
  • Paragraphing controls the reader's rest and emphasis. A very short paragraph can land a point with force.

11.How to Analyse a Prose Passage

  1. Read it twice. Once for what happens, once for how it is written.
  2. Identify the type and purpose. Fiction or nonfiction? Narrative, descriptive, expository or persuasive?
  3. Find the point of view. Who is telling it, and how much do they know?
  4. Notice the sentences. Are they short or long? What does that do to the pace?
  5. Notice the words. Which words carry feeling? Are there images or comparisons?
  6. State the effect. What does the passage make you feel or understand, and which techniques cause it? Use point, evidence, effect (see How to Write a Perfect Paragraph).

12.Worked Examples

Example A: Analysing a short passage

Read this original opening, then follow the steps.

The house had been empty for eleven years, but Maya still remembered which floorboard creaked, which door stuck in humid weather, which window let in the morning light exactly the way she liked it. Some places you never really leave, even after you have gone.
1Type and purpose. Fiction (narrative prose): a story opening.
2Point of view. Third person limited: we are inside Maya's memory, using "she".
3Sentences. One long sentence with a list of three remembered details, then a short final sentence.
4Words. Specific, ordinary details (floorboard, door, window) rather than big abstract words.
5Effect. The long list shows how deeply Maya knows the house; the short last sentence states the theme plainly.

Sample paragraph. The writer makes Maya's attachment to the house feel real through precise detail. By listing "which floorboard creaked, which door stuck", the long first sentence shows that she remembers the house through her body, not only her mind. The short final sentence then steps back to state the idea quietly: "Some places you never really leave." This contrast in sentence length gives the theme emotional weight without lecturing the reader.

Example B: One moment, four points of view

Moment: a girl opens a door and finds her brother hiding behind it.

Point of viewVersion
First personI opened the door and froze. Tom was crouched behind it, holding his breath.
Third limitedNadia opened the door and froze. Tom was crouched behind it. Had he been there all afternoon?
OmniscientNadia opened the door and froze. Tom, crouched behind it, wished the floor would swallow him.
ObjectiveNadia opened the door. Tom was crouched behind it. Neither of them spoke.

What changes. First person gives us the narrator's shock directly; third limited lets us hear Nadia's question; omniscient also enters Tom's mind; objective shows only what a camera sees, leaving the reader to infer the feelings.

Try it yourself. Rewrite the moment in second person ("you").

Sample answer: "You open the door and freeze. Tom is crouched behind it, holding his breath."

14.Common Mistakes

MistakeBetter habit
Saying prose is "plain" and poetry is "special"Great prose uses rhythm, imagery and structure with care
Treating word counts as official lawsTreat them as rules of thumb; follow the guideline of your exam or publisher
Confusing memoir and autobiographyMemoir: selected memories; autobiography: a whole life
Mixing up the narrator and the authorSay "the narrator" unless you know the work is autobiographical
Switching point of view without a reasonKeep one viewpoint within a scene
Confusing plot and themePlot is what happens; theme is what it means
Writing dialogue for several speakers in one paragraphStart a new paragraph for each new speaker
Assuming nonfiction is always neutralAsk who wrote it, why, and how they know

15.Common Myths

MythA novel must be at least 60,000 words.
FactThere is no single rule. One major awards body counts 40,000 words or more as a novel, and many novels are longer.
MythProse never uses poetic techniques.
FactProse often uses imagery, metaphor and rhythm, and prose poems blur the line.
MythFiction is "untrue", so it has no value as truth.
FactFiction invents events but can reveal real human experience and ideas.
MythNonfiction always means objective.
FactNonfiction aims at truth, but writers choose what to include and from what viewpoint.

16.Key Terms

Prose: language in ordinary sentences and paragraphs, without the line structure of poetry.
Prose poem: a poem written in prose form, with poetic imagery and rhythm.
Fiction / nonfiction: imagined / real prose.
Novel, novella, short story, flash fiction: fiction classified by length.
Genre: a category by content and conventions, such as fantasy or mystery.
Memoir / autobiography: selected memories / a whole life, told by the person.
Point of view: who tells the story and how much they know.
Omniscient narrator: one who knows all characters' thoughts.
Unreliable narrator: a narrator whose account cannot be fully trusted.
Diction: the writer's choice of words.
Voice: the distinctive personality of the writing.

17.Why This Matters

Recognising prose, with its categories, elements and styles, is the foundation for reading almost everything you will meet and for writing almost everything you will produce, from a novel to a work email. Learning to notice sentence length, word choice and point of view makes you a sharper reader and a more deliberate writer. Understanding how prose differs from poetry, covered next in this series, sharpens your sense of both. Continue with Poetry: The Complete Guide.

Key points to remember
  • Prose is ordinary language in sentences and paragraphs; poetry uses lines. The line can blur (prose poems).
  • Fiction is imagined; nonfiction is real, though the boundary can be blurred.
  • Fiction is classified by length (with definitions that vary) and by genre.
  • Nonfiction includes essays, biography, autobiography, memoir, journalism, speeches and more.
  • Prose fiction rests on plot, character, setting, point of view and theme.
  • Point of view controls how much the reader knows.
  • Sentence length, diction and paragraphing shape rhythm and effect.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students a short moment (such as a character finding a closed door) and ask them to write it in two different points of view, then explain how the effect changes. Follow with a "sentence-length" exercise: rewrite a tense moment using only short sentences, then a calm moment using one long sentence.

Please note: word-count definitions for fiction types differ between awards, publishers and teachers, so students should use the guidelines for their own task. The passages in this guide are original examples written for teaching.
