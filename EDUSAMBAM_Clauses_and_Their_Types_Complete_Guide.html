Clauses and Their Types: A Complete Guide

Understand clauses, independent and dependent clauses, noun clauses, adjective (relative) clauses, adverb clauses, finite and non-finite clauses, coordination, subordination and common clause errors—with clear examples.

Article purpose: A clause is a group of words built around a verb. Clauses are the building blocks of sentences. Learning to recognise their boundaries and functions makes it easier to understand sentence structure, punctuation, meaning and the way writers combine ideas.

1. What Is a Clause?

A clause is a group of words that contains a verb and its associated elements. A clause may be able to stand alone as a complete sentence, or it may depend on another clause.

Examples

The children laughed. → one independent clause.

because the joke was funny → a dependent clause.

The children laughed because the joke was funny. → two clauses combined into one sentence.

A useful first step is therefore to find the verbs and then ask which words belong with each verb.

2. Clause vs Phrase

A phrase is a group of words that does not contain a complete subject–verb relationship of its own. A clause contains a verb and its related elements.

ExpressionWhat it isExample
in the morningPhraseNo finite verb.
after the rainPhraseNo finite verb.
after the rain stoppedClausestopped is the verb.
the girl in the blue dressPhraseNo finite verb in the group.
the girl who won the raceClause-containing groupwon introduces a relative clause.

3. Independent (Main) Clauses

An independent clause, also called a main clause, can normally stand alone as a complete sentence because it expresses a complete thought.

Examples

The students completed the exercise.

The sun disappeared behind the clouds.

We arrived before noon.

Each can function as a sentence on its own.

4. Dependent (Subordinate) Clauses

A dependent clause, also called a subordinate clause, cannot normally stand alone as a complete sentence in the intended meaning. It depends on another clause.

Examples

because the road was closed

when the lesson ended

although she was tired

who lives next door

These clauses need a main clause to complete the larger sentence:

We took another route because the road was closed.

5. The Three Major Functional Types of Dependent Clauses

For school grammar, dependent clauses are commonly grouped according to the job they perform:

TypeMain functionExample
Noun clauseActs like a nounI know what she wants.
Adjective / relative clauseDescribes a noun or pronounThe book that you recommended was useful.
Adverb clauseModifies a verb, adjective or whole clause by showing relationships such as time, reason, condition or contrastWe waited until the rain stopped.

6. Noun Clauses

A noun clause is a dependent clause that functions in a noun-like way. It can act as a subject, object or complement.

As an object

I understand what you mean.

She explained why she was late.

As a subject

What he decided surprised everyone.

Whether they will agree is uncertain.

As a complement

The truth is that nobody checked the figures.

7. Common Words Introducing Noun Clauses

Noun clauses may begin with words such as that, whether, if, what, who, whom, whose, which, when, where, why and how, depending on the meaning and structure.

Examples

We know that the plan changed.

I wonder whether she will come.

Tell me where you found it.

I understand why they objected.

Show us how the machine works.

8. Adjective / Relative Clauses

An adjective clause, commonly called a relative clause, gives information about a noun or pronoun.

Examples

The student who won the prize thanked the judges.

I read the book that you recommended.

The house which stands on the hill is very old.

The clause tells us which student, which book or which house is being discussed.

9. Relative Pronouns

WordTypical referenceExample
whoPeopleThe teacher who helped me was patient.
whomPeople as an object, especially in formal EnglishThe person whom we invited arrived early.
whosePossessionThe student whose bag was missing reported it.
whichThings / animalsThe car which broke down was repaired.
thatPeople or things in many defining relative clausesThe book that I borrowed was excellent.

10. Defining and Non-Defining Relative Clauses

A defining relative clause identifies which person or thing we mean. It is essential to the intended reference.

The students who submitted the work early received feedback.

A non-defining relative clause adds extra information about a person or thing already identified. It is normally separated by commas.

Mr Khan, who teaches English, prepared the lesson.

The punctuation matters because it can affect meaning.

11. Adverb Clauses

An adverb clause functions like an adverb by giving information about circumstances such as time, place, reason, purpose, condition, contrast, result or manner.

RelationshipCommon introducersExample
Timewhen, while, before, after, until, as soon asWe left when the lesson ended.
Reasonbecause, since, asWe stayed inside because it was raining.
Conditionif, unless, provided thatWe will go if the weather improves.
Contrastalthough, though, even thoughAlthough he was tired, he continued.
Purposeso that, in order thatShe spoke slowly so that everyone could understand.
Resultso...that, such...thatIt was so cold that we stayed inside.
Manneras, as if, as thoughHe spoke as though he knew the answer.

12. Finite and Non-Finite Clauses

A finite clause contains a finite verb that carries tense or agrees with the subject. A non-finite clause is built around a non-finite verb form such as an infinitive, -ing form or participle.

Compare

Because she was tired, she went home. → finite clause.

To save time, she took a taxi. → non-finite clause.

Feeling tired, she went home. → non-finite -ing clause.

Exhausted by the journey, she went straight to bed. → non-finite participial clause.

Non-finite clauses are particularly useful in compact, sophisticated writing, but their subjects must be clear.

13. Coordinate Clauses

Two independent clauses can be joined by a coordinating conjunction. The clauses have equal grammatical status.

FANBOYS

for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so

The rain stopped, and the match resumed.

She was tired, but she continued working.

We could leave now, or we could wait.

14. Subordination

Subordination makes one clause dependent on another. Subordinating conjunctions and relative words help show the relationship between ideas.

Examples

Although the task was difficult, the students completed it.

We waited until the teacher arrived.

She revised carefully because she wanted to improve.

Subordination allows writers to show which information is central and which information is supporting or dependent.

15. Clause Combinations and Sentence Types

Sentence typeClause structureExample
SimpleOne independent clauseThe students worked.
CompoundTwo or more independent clausesThe students worked, and the teacher observed.
ComplexOne independent clause + at least one dependent clauseThe students worked while the teacher observed.
Compound-complexTwo or more independent clauses + at least one dependent clauseThe students worked while the teacher observed, and the lesson continued.

16. How to Find Clauses in a Sentence

  1. Underline or identify every verb.
  2. Group each verb with its subject and related words.
  3. Look for conjunctions and relative words.
  4. Decide which groups can stand independently.
  5. Label the dependent clauses according to their function.
Worked example

When the bell rang, the students left the room because they had finished the test.

When the bell rang → dependent adverb clause of time.

the students left the room → independent/main clause.

because they had finished the test → dependent adverb clause of reason.

17. Another Worked Example

Sentence: “The book that you gave me was useful because it explained what I needed to know.”

Main clause: The book was useful.

Relative clause: that you gave me → describes book.

Adverb clause: because it explained what I needed to know → gives the reason.

Noun clause: what I needed to know → functions as the object of explained.

18. Common Clause Errors

Fragments

❌ Because the road was closed.

✅ We took another route because the road was closed.

Comma splice

❌ The rain stopped, the match resumed.

✅ The rain stopped, and the match resumed.

✅ The rain stopped; the match resumed.

Run-on sentence

❌ The lesson ended the students left the room.

✅ When the lesson ended, the students left the room.

19. Dangling and Misplaced Modifiers

A dependent or non-finite clause should clearly relate to the intended subject. An unclear relationship can produce a humorous or confusing meaning.

❌ Walking through the park, the trees looked beautiful.

The trees are grammatically presented as if they were walking.

✅ Walking through the park, I thought the trees looked beautiful.

20. Clauses and Punctuation

Punctuation often reflects the relationship between clauses.

  • Use a comma after a long introductory dependent clause when appropriate: Although the task was difficult, we completed it.
  • Do not normally use a comma between a subject and its essential verb: The student who won the prize thanked the judges.
  • Use a comma with a non-defining relative clause: My brother, who lives abroad, is visiting.
  • Use a suitable joining method between independent clauses: conjunction, semicolon or another appropriate construction.

21. Clauses and Meaning

Clause structure is not only a grammar exercise. It can change how information is presented.

Compare

Although he was tired, he continued. → contrast is foregrounded.

He was tired, but he continued. → the two ideas are coordinated.

Tired, he continued. → the idea is compressed into a non-finite construction.

In O Level reading and writing, noticing these choices can help you explain how a writer organises and emphasises ideas.

22. Clauses in O Level English

Strong knowledge of clauses supports several skills required in English Language: understanding complex sentences, following relationships between ideas, controlling sentence structure, using punctuation accurately and writing with variety.

When analysing a passage, ask:

  • Where does one clause end and another begin?
  • Which clause contains the main idea?
  • Which information is subordinate or supporting?
  • Does a relative clause add essential or extra information?
  • Does an adverb clause show cause, contrast, condition, time or another relationship?
  • How does the writer's choice of clause structure affect emphasis, pace or clarity?

23. Quick Classification Practice

A. “I know what you mean.” → what you mean = noun clause.

B. “The girl who won the race smiled.” → who won the race = relative clause.

C. “We stayed inside because it was raining.” → because it was raining = adverb clause of reason.

D. “If you practise, you will improve.” → If you practise = adverb clause of condition.

E. “When the bell rang, the students left.” → When the bell rang = adverb clause of time.

24. Final Memory Map

Remember

CLAUSE: a word group built around a verb.

MAIN / INDEPENDENT: can normally stand alone.

SUBORDINATE / DEPENDENT: depends on another clause.

NOUN CLAUSE: performs a noun-like function.

RELATIVE CLAUSE: describes a noun or pronoun.

ADVERB CLAUSE: expresses a circumstance or relationship such as time, reason, condition or contrast.

COORDINATION: joins clauses of equal status.

SUBORDINATION: makes one clause dependent on another.
