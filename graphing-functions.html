Graphing Functions: Quadratics, Cubics & Graphical Inequalities

Plot quadratic, cubic and reciprocal graphs from a table of values, find turning points, roots and intercepts, solve equations from a graph, estimate gradients, and shade inequality regions, with worked examples and tools.

Straight-line graphs are only the beginning. Many real quantities do not change at a steady rate: a ball thrown upwards rises, slows, stops and falls; the area of a field changes faster than its side; profit rises and then falls as prices climb. Graphs of quadratic and cubic functions show these curved patterns. This guide shows you how to plot them from a table of values, read their key features (turning point, roots, intercepts and symmetry), use a graph to solve equations, and show inequalities as shaded regions.

1.What a Function Graph Shows

A function is a rule that gives exactly one output for each input. We write y = f(x), for example y = x² − 2x − 3. Every pair (x, y) that fits the rule is a point on the graph. If you could plot all of them, they would join into one continuous line or curve. In practice you plot a few points and join them with a smooth curve.

For straight lines (y = mx + c) the graph is a line, and you will find that work in Algebra: Basic, Intermediate & Advanced. Here we study curves. The highest power of x decides the family:

2.Plotting a Quadratic from a Table of Values

Follow the same four steps every time.

  1. Choose a range of x-values that is wide enough to show the whole shape (for a quadratic, the curve must turn and start rising or falling again).
  2. Substitute each x into the rule and write the y-value in a table. Use brackets for negative numbers.
  3. Plot the points carefully on a grid with a sensible scale.
  4. Join them with one smooth curve, drawn freehand in one flowing line. Never use a ruler, and never join the points with short straight pieces.
Example 1 · Table of values

Complete a table for y = x² − 2x − 3 for x from −2 to 4.

For x = −2: y = (−2)² − 2(−2) − 3 = 4 + 4 − 3 = 5. For x = 3: y = 9 − 6 − 3 = 0.

x−2−101234
y50−3−4−305

Notice that the y-values are symmetrical: 5, 0, −3, then −4 at the middle, then −3, 0, 5 again. The lowest point is at x = 1. That symmetry is a useful check on your arithmetic.

Tip: a wrong y-value stands out as a point that does not lie on the smooth curve. If one point is off, recalculate it before you decide the curve is wrong.

3.Key Features of a Parabola

Every quadratic y = ax² + bx + c has a graph called a parabola. It is symmetrical, and these features describe it fully.

  • Shape. If a > 0 the curve opens upwards (a U shape) and the turning point is a minimum. If a < 0 it opens downwards (an ∩ shape) and the turning point is a maximum.
  • y-intercept. Put x = 0: y = c. This is where the curve crosses the y-axis.
  • Roots (x-intercepts). Put y = 0 and solve ax² + bx + c = 0. These are the x-values where the curve crosses the x-axis. There can be two, one or none.
  • Axis of symmetry. A vertical line through the turning point, with equation x = −b ÷ (2a). If there are two roots, the axis is halfway between them.
  • Turning point. Its x-value is on the axis of symmetry. Put that x into the equation to get y.
Figure 1. The features of y = x² − 2x − 3. The gold dashed line is the axis of symmetry and the gold dot is the turning point.

How many roots? The discriminant

The number b² − 4ac is called the discriminant. It tells you how many times the parabola meets the x-axis, without drawing it.

b² − 4acRootsPicture
PositiveTwo different rootsCurve crosses the x-axis twice
ZeroOne repeated rootCurve just touches the x-axis
NegativeNo real rootsCurve never reaches the x-axis

For y = x² − 2x − 3, the discriminant is (−2)² − 4(1)(−3) = 4 + 12 = 16, which is positive. This matches the two crossing points at x = −1 and x = 3.

4.Finding the Turning Point

There are two reliable methods. Learn both, and use one to check the other.

Method A: complete the square

Write the quadratic as y = (x − p)² + q. A square is never negative, so the smallest y can be is q, and this happens when x = p. The turning point is (p, q).

Example 2 · Complete the square

(a) y = x² − 6x + 5. Half of −6 is −3, so y = (x − 3)² − 9 + 5 = (x − 3)² − 4. Turning point (3, −4), a minimum.

(b) y = x² + 8x + 10. Half of 8 is 4, so y = (x + 4)² − 16 + 10 = (x + 4)² − 6. Turning point (−4, −6). Careful: the sign inside the bracket is the opposite of the x-coordinate.

(c) y = −x² + 4x + 1. Take out the minus sign from the first two terms: y = −(x² − 4x) + 1 = −[(x − 2)² − 4] + 1 = −(x − 2)² + 5. Turning point (2, 5), a maximum, because the squared term is subtracted.

Method B: use the axis of symmetry

Find x = −b ÷ (2a) and then substitute it into the equation.

Example 3 · Using x = −b ÷ 2a

y = 2x² − 12x + 7. Here a = 2 and b = −12, so x = 12 ÷ 4 = 3. Then y = 2(9) − 12(3) + 7 = 18 − 36 + 7 = −11. The turning point is (3, −11).

“y = (x + 3)² − 5 has its turning point at (3, −5).”

One sign is wrong.

The fix: the bracket (x + 3) is zero when x = −3, so the turning point is (−3, −5). In y = (x − p)² + q the x-coordinate is p. When you see (x + 3), p is −3. Check by substituting x = −3: (−3 + 3)² − 5 = −5.

5.How a, p and q Move the Curve

Once you know the family, you can predict a graph before you plot it.

Figure 2. The navy curve y = x² is the basic shape. The red curve is the same shape moved 2 right and 3 down. The gold curve is flipped upside down and moved up 4.
  • y = (x − p)² + q is y = x² moved p to the right and q up (negative values move left or down).
  • A larger a, as in y = 3x², makes the curve narrower. A smaller a, such as y = 0.5x², makes it wider.
  • A negative a turns the curve upside down.

6.Sketching from the Roots

A sketch does not need a table of values. It must show the correct shape and label the important points. If the quadratic is in the form y = (x − a)(x − b), the roots are at x = a and x = b, and the axis of symmetry is halfway between them.

Example 4 · Sketch from the factorised form

Sketch y = (x − 2)(x − 8).

Roots: x = 2 and x = 8. Axis of symmetry: x = (2 + 8) ÷ 2 = 5. Turning point: y = (5 − 2)(5 − 8) = 3 × (−3) = −9, so (5, −9) and it is a minimum because the x² coefficient is positive. y-intercept: (0 − 2)(0 − 8) = 16, so (0, 16).

Draw a U-shaped curve through (2, 0) and (8, 0), with its lowest point at (5, −9), and crossing the y-axis at (0, 16). Label all four values.

7.Cubic Graphs

A cubic has the form y = ax³ + bx² + cx + d. Its main features are these.

  • If a > 0 the curve rises from the bottom left to the top right. If a < 0 it falls from the top left to the bottom right.
  • It has at most two turning points: a local maximum and a local minimum. Some cubics have none.
  • It crosses the x-axis one, two or three times. (It must cross at least once.)
  • The y-intercept is the constant term d.

A local maximum is the highest point in its neighbourhood, not necessarily the highest point on the whole graph, because a cubic goes on rising forever to the right.

Figure 3. The cubic y = x³ − 3x. Its two turning points (gold) lie between three roots (navy).
Example 5 · Table for a cubic

Plot y = x³ − 3x for x from −2 to 2.

y−21.12521.3750−1.375−2−1.1252

For x = −1.5: (−1.5)³ − 3(−1.5) = −3.375 + 4.5 = 1.125. The extra points near the turns (x = ±0.5 and ±1.5) are essential: with only whole-number x-values you would miss the shape of the bends. The curve rises to a local maximum at (−1, 2), falls to a local minimum at (1, −2), then rises again. The roots are where y = 0: x = 0 and x = ±√3, about ±1.73.

Example 6 · A cubic in factorised form

Sketch y = (x + 2)(x − 1)(x − 3).

Roots: x = −2, 1 and 3. The y-intercept is (2)(−1)(−3) = 6. The x³ coefficient is positive (the brackets multiply to x³ + …), so the curve starts low on the left and finishes high on the right. It passes through the roots in order, with a hump between −2 and 1 and a dip between 1 and 3.

8.The Reciprocal Graph y = 1/x

You may also meet y = k/x. It has two separate branches. For y = 1/x, positive x gives positive y and negative x gives negative y, so the branches are in opposite corners of the grid. The value x = 0 is not allowed, because you cannot divide by zero, so the curve never touches the y-axis. It also never touches the x-axis, because 1 ÷ x is never zero.

Do not join the two branches. Plot extra points close to x = 0 to show the curve climbing steeply.

9.Solving Equations from Graphs

The solutions of f(x) = 0 are the x-values where the graph of y = f(x) crosses the x-axis. You can also solve other equations by drawing a second graph and reading where it meets the first.

Example 7 · Roots, a horizontal line and a sloping line

The graph of y = x² − 2x − 3 is drawn.

(a) Solve x² − 2x − 3 = 0. Read where the curve meets the x-axis: x = −1 and x = 3.

(b) Solve x² − 2x − 3 = 2. Draw the horizontal line y = 2 and read where it meets the curve. The readings are about x = −1.4 and x = 3.4. (The exact solutions are x = 1 ± √6, which are −1.45 and 3.45 to two decimal places.)

(c) Solve x² − 2x − 3 = x + 1. Draw the line y = x + 1. It meets the curve at (−1, 0) and (4, 5), so x = −1 and x = 4. Check: x² − 3x − 4 = (x − 4)(x + 1) = 0.

Notice the rule: to solve f(x) = something, draw y = something on the same axes. Graphical answers are readings, so they are only as accurate as your drawing. Examiners accept answers within a small tolerance.

“To solve x² − 2x − 3 = 2, I read where the curve crosses the x-axis.”

That answers a different equation.

The fix: the x-axis is the line y = 0, so crossing it solves x² − 2x − 3 = 0. For “= 2” you need the line y = 2. Always ask: what number is on the right-hand side? That is the height of the line to draw.

Estimating the gradient of a curve

On a curve the steepness changes from point to point. The gradient at a point is the gradient of the tangent, the straight line that just touches the curve there. Draw the tangent with a ruler, choose two points far apart on it, and calculate (change in y) ÷ (change in x).

Example 8 · Gradient at x = 3

On the graph of y = x² − 2x − 3, a tangent drawn at (3, 0) rises about 4 units for each 1 unit to the right. For instance, it passes close to (2, −4) and (4, 4). The gradient is (4 − (−4)) ÷ (4 − 2) = 4. A hand-drawn tangent gives an estimate, so an answer between about 3.5 and 4.5 is reasonable.

This gradient is a rate of change. If y were distance in metres and x were time in seconds, a gradient of 4 would be a speed of 4 m/s at that moment.

10.Quadratic Inequalities from a Graph

For y = x² − 2x − 3, the curve is below the x-axis between the roots and above it outside the roots.

InequalityMeaningSolution
x² − 2x − 3 < 0Curve below the x-axis−1 < x < 3
x² − 2x − 3 > 0Curve above the x-axisx < −1 or x > 3

Use ≤ or ≥ when the question includes the roots themselves. Two separate pieces (“x < −1 or x > 3”) must be written with the word or. You cannot write this as a single chain like 3 < x < −1, which is impossible.

11.Shading Regions for Linear Inequalities

An inequality such as y > x + 1 describes a whole region of the plane. Use these steps.

  1. Draw the boundary line (replace the inequality sign with =). Use a solid line for ≤ or ≥ and a dashed line for < or >. A dashed line means points on the line are not included.
  2. Choose a test point not on the line. (0, 0) is easiest, unless the line passes through it.
  3. Substitute the point. If the inequality is true, the region containing the test point is the answer. If it is false, the other side is.
Example 9 · Which side?

(a) y > x + 1. Test (0, 0): is 0 > 0 + 1? No. So the answer is the side not containing the origin, which is the region above the dashed line.

(b) 2x + 3y ≤ 12. The boundary passes through (0, 4) and (6, 0) and is solid. Test (0, 0): 0 ≤ 12 is true, so shade the side containing the origin.

(c) x < 3. The boundary x = 3 is a vertical dashed line. Points with x smaller than 3 lie to its left.

“For y > 2x − 1, I drew a solid line because the line is part of the graph.”

Which sign decides the line style?

The fix: a strict sign (> or <) excludes the boundary, so the line must be dashed. A solid line is only for ≤ or ≥. Check with a point on the line, such as (1, 1): is 1 > 2(1) − 1 = 1? No, because 1 is not greater than 1. So points on the line do not satisfy the inequality.

12.Several Inequalities Together

When several inequalities are given, draw all the boundary lines on one pair of axes. The answer is the region where all the inequalities are true at once. Read the question to see which convention it uses: some ask you to shade the wanted region and label it R, while others ask you to shade the unwanted regions and leave R unshaded. Both give the same R.

Figure 4. Region R for y > 1 (dashed), y ≤ x and x + y ≤ 8 (solid). The corners are where two boundary lines meet.
Example 10 · Corners and whole-number points

Find the corners of R and count the points with whole-number coordinates inside R (including points on solid edges).

The corners are where two boundaries cross. y = 1 and y = x meet at (1, 1). y = x and x + y = 8 meet where 2x = 8, so at (4, 4). y = 1 and x + y = 8 meet at (7, 1).

Because y > 1, the whole numbers for y start at 2. For y = 2: x runs from 2 to 6, which is 5 points. For y = 3: x from 3 to 5, which is 3 points. For y = 4: only x = 4, which is 1 point. The total is 5 + 3 + 1 = 9 points. The corners (1, 1) and (7, 1) are on the dashed line, so they are not included, but (4, 4) is included.

13.Real-Life Uses of Curved Graphs

  • Throwing a ball. A simple model for the height in metres after t seconds is h = 20t − 5t². The ball is on the ground when h = 0, which gives t = 0 and t = 4. The turning point is at t = 2, where h = 20 × 2 − 5 × 4 = 20 m, so the greatest height is 20 m. (This model uses 10 m/s² for gravity, a rounded value. The accepted figure is about 9.8 m/s².)
  • Fencing a field. A farmer has 40 m of fence for a rectangle. If one side is x metres, the other is 20 − x, and the area is A = x(20 − x). The roots are x = 0 and x = 20, so the maximum is halfway at x = 10, giving a square with area 100 m².
  • Speed and stopping distance, profit and cost, bridge arches and satellite dishes all use parabolas, while cubics appear in volume formulas and in some engineering curves.
  • Inequalities in planning. A shop that can buy at most 8 items of two kinds, and needs at least one of one kind, is describing a region like Figure 4.

14.Common Mistakes

  • Joining points with straight pieces. A curve must be smooth. Pointed bottoms and flat tops are marked wrong.
  • Stopping the graph at the plotted points. Extend the curve a little past the first and last point.
  • Using too few x-values. The turning point can fall between two plotted points. Add half-values if the curve looks uncertain.
  • Sign errors with negatives. Write (−2)² as 4, not −4, and use brackets when you substitute.
  • Mixing up roots and the y-intercept. The y-intercept has x = 0. The roots have y = 0.
  • Sign error in the turning point when reading y = (x − p)² + q.
  • Using the wrong line style or the wrong side for an inequality. Always run the test point.
  • Writing x < −1 or x > 3 as one chain, or giving only one of two solutions.
“The roots of y = x² − 2x − 3 are −3 and 1, because the numbers in the equation are −2 and −3.”

Do the numbers in the equation tell you the roots directly?

The fix: the roots are the x-values that make y = 0. Factorise: x² − 2x − 3 = (x + 1)(x − 3), so the roots are −1 and 3. Check: (−1)² − 2(−1) − 3 = 1 + 2 − 3 = 0. The number −3 is the y-intercept, not a root.

15.Try It Yourself

  1. Find the turning point and the roots of y = x² + 10x + 21.
  2. Does the point (3, 2) satisfy 2x + y < 8? What does this tell you about the line?
  3. For y = x³ − x, find y when x = −1, 0 and 1. What do you notice?
Show answers

1. y = (x + 5)² − 25 + 21 = (x + 5)² − 4, so the turning point is (−5, −4). Factorising gives (x + 3)(x + 7), so the roots are x = −3 and x = −7. The axis x = −5 is halfway between them.

2. 2(3) + 2 = 8, and 8 < 8 is false, so the point does not satisfy the inequality. The point is exactly on the boundary line 2x + y = 8, and because the sign is strict the line is dashed.

3. y = −1 + 1 = 0 at x = −1, y = 0 at x = 0, and y = 1 − 1 = 0 at x = 1. The curve crosses the x-axis at all three points. These are its three roots, because x³ − x = x(x − 1)(x + 1).

16.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [3 marks] For y = x² − 4x − 5, find y when x = −2, x = 1 and x = 4.
  2. [3 marks] Write y = x² + 6x + 4 in the form (x + p)² + q. State the turning point and the y-intercept.
  3. [4 marks] For y = x³ − 4x, (a) factorise and write down the three roots, and (b) find y when x = 1.5.
  4. [4 marks] The graph of y = x² − 2x − 3 is drawn. Explain how you would use it to solve x² − 2x − 3 = 5, and then solve the equation by algebra.
  5. [4 marks] A ball’s height is h = 20t − 5t² metres after t seconds. Find (a) when it lands, (b) its greatest height and the time it happens, (c) its height after 3 seconds.
  6. [5 marks] Draw the lines y = 1, y = x and x + y = 8. Shade the region R where y > 1, y ≤ x and x + y ≤ 8. Give the coordinates of the three corners and say which edge is dashed.
  7. [3 marks] y = x² − 6x + k. Find the value of k for which the graph touches the x-axis, and the values of k for which it does not meet the x-axis.
Show answers

1. x = −2: 4 + 8 − 5 = 7. x = 1: 1 − 4 − 5 = −8. x = 4: 16 − 16 − 5 = −5.

2. Half of 6 is 3, so y = (x + 3)² − 9 + 4 = (x + 3)² − 5. Turning point (−3, −5). The y-intercept is the constant term, 4.

3. (a) x³ − 4x = x(x² − 4) = x(x − 2)(x + 2), so the roots are x = 0, 2 and −2. (b) 1.5³ − 4(1.5) = 3.375 − 6 = −2.625.

4. Draw the horizontal line y = 5 and read the x-values where it meets the curve. By algebra: x² − 2x − 8 = 0, so (x − 4)(x + 2) = 0, giving x = 4 or x = −2.

5. (a) 20t − 5t² = 0, so 5t(4 − t) = 0 and the ball lands at t = 4 s. (b) The axis is halfway between t = 0 and t = 4, so t = 2 and h = 40 − 20 = 20 m. (c) h = 60 − 45 = 15 m.

6. The corners are (1, 1), (4, 4) and (7, 1). The line y = 1 is dashed because of the strict sign y > 1. The other two edges are solid.

7. The graph touches the axis when the discriminant is zero: 36 − 4k = 0, so k = 9 (y = (x − 3)²). It does not meet the axis when 36 − 4k < 0, so k > 9.

17.Key Points

  • Plot from a table of values, then join with one smooth curve. Check that the y-values are symmetrical for a quadratic.
  • A parabola has an axis of symmetry x = −b ÷ 2a. The turning point lies on it.
  • y = (x − p)² + q has its turning point at (p, q). Positive a gives a minimum, negative a gives a maximum.
  • The discriminant b² − 4ac tells you whether there are two, one or no roots.
  • A cubic has at most two turning points and between one and three roots. Its y-intercept is the constant term.
  • To solve f(x) = k from a graph, draw the line y = k. For f(x) = 0 read the x-axis crossings.
  • For an inequality: dashed line for < or >, solid for ≤ or ≥. Use a test point to choose the side.
  • For several inequalities, the answer is where all of them are true together.

18.For Teachers and Parents

The biggest barrier with curved graphs is that learners treat plotting as joining dots. Ask them to say what the curve must do between two points before they draw it. Use the symmetry of the y-values as a self-check, and have learners hold a flexible piece of wire or a strip of card against the points so that they feel what “smooth” means. The Parabola Explorer on this page lets a learner change a, b and c and watch the roots and turning point move, which builds the link between the algebra and the picture. Encourage learners to predict first (“will this open up or down?”) and then test. For inequalities, always insist on the test-point step and on a spoken reason, such as “the origin does not satisfy the inequality, so I shade the other side.” Graphing software is helpful for checking work, but learners should still be able to produce a table and a hand-drawn curve, because that is what examinations ask for.
