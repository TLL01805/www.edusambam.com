Singular and Plural Nouns: Rules, Irregular Plurals and Examples

One book, two books; one child, two children. Learn every rule for forming plurals, from regular -s and -es endings to irregular and foreign plurals, with tables of examples.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 25 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Vocabulary

One book or two books? One child or two childs? Choosing between singular and plural looks simple until English gives you children, mice, sheep, criteria and information. Knowing how plurals are formed, and which nouns break the rules, is essential for correct spelling, grammar and verb agreement.

This guide explains singular and plural nouns, every regular spelling rule, and all the main groups of irregular plurals. It gives over 360 nouns and noun types in tables, with a "how it is formed" column for each. It also covers plural-only and uncountable nouns, compound nouns, collective nouns, verb agreement, common mistakes, key points, ten practice questions and a teacher activity.

It builds on Parts of Speech and Sentence Structure, and connects with Tenses & Verb Forms for verb agreement and with Common Errors in English. Spelling in this guide follows British English.

1.What Are Singular and Plural Nouns?

A noun names a person, place, thing or idea. English nouns have two numbers:

  • Singular means one: a book, one child, a city.
  • Plural means more than one: books, children, cities.

The number of the noun controls the verb: The book is on the table but The books are on the table. Nouns that can be counted are countable and have both forms. Nouns that cannot be counted are uncountable and usually have only a singular form (information, advice).

2.The Regular Plural Rules at a Glance

Ending of the singularRule for the pluralExamples
Most nounsadd -sbook → books, river → rivers
-s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -zadd -esbus → buses, dish → dishes, church → churches, box → boxes
consonant + ychange y to i, add -esbaby → babies, city → cities
vowel + yadd -sboy → boys, key → keys
-f or -fe (some nouns)change to -vesleaf → leaves, knife → knives
-f or -ff (other nouns)add -sroof → roofs, cliff → cliffs
consonant + o (some nouns)add -eshero → heroes, potato → potatoes
vowel + o, and most shortened wordsadd -szoo → zoos, photo → photos
Single-syllable words ending in one s or zdouble the letter, add -esquiz → quizzes, bus → buses or busses

The ending -es is used when -s alone would be hard to say: bus-es, church-es.

16.Collective Nouns and Verb Agreement

A collective noun names a group: team, committee, family, class, staff, government, audience. In British English, you can use a singular verb when the group acts as one (The team is ready) or a plural verb when you think of the members separately (The team are arguing).

NounRuleExample
police, people, cattle, clergyalways take a plural verbThe police are investigating.
news, mathematics, physics, economics, measleslook plural but take a singular verbThe news is good. Mathematics is difficult.
scissors, trouserstake a plural verbThe scissors are sharp.
a pair of scissorstakes a singular verbA pair of scissors is on the desk.
amounts of money, time and distancetake a singular verb when seen as one amountTen pounds is enough.
each, every, either, neither + nountake a singular verbEach of the students has a pen.

17.Plurals of Letters, Numbers and Abbreviations

  • Abbreviations: add -s with no apostrophe: MPs, CDs, PhDs.
  • Decades: the 1990s, not the 1990's.
  • Surnames: the Smiths, not the Smith's.
  • Single letters: an apostrophe may be used for clarity: mind your p's and q's, two A's.
  • Words as words: the dos and don'ts, the ifs and buts.

18.Common Mistakes with Singular and Plural

MistakeCorrectWhy
informations, advices, furnituresinformation, advice, furnitureThese are uncountable.
childs, mans, foots, toothschildren, men, feet, teethThese are irregular plurals.
sheeps, deers, fishssheep, deer, fishThe plural has no change.
babys, citys, storysbabies, cities, storiesConsonant + y changes to -ies.
tomatos, potatos, herostomatoes, potatoes, heroesThese nouns add -es.
a criteria, a phenomenaa criterion, a phenomenonThe singular forms differ.
The apple's are fresh.The apples are fresh.Never use an apostrophe for a simple plural.
a news, an equipmenta piece of news, a piece of equipmentThese are uncountable.
The police is here.The police are here.Police always takes a plural verb.
Key points to remember
  • Most nouns add -s; nouns ending in s, ss, sh, ch, x, z add -es.
  • Consonant + y becomes -ies; vowel + y just adds -s.
  • Some nouns ending in -f or -fe change to -ves (leaves, knives); others do not (roofs, chiefs).
  • Learn the irregular plurals: men, women, children, feet, teeth, geese, mice, oxen, people.
  • Some nouns do not change: sheep, deer, species, series, aircraft.
  • Scientific words keep Latin and Greek plurals: criteria, phenomena, analyses, bacteria.
  • Uncountable nouns have no plural: information, advice, furniture, luggage, news.
  • Never use an apostrophe to make a plural.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give students twenty singular nouns on cards and ask them to sort them into groups by plural rule (-s, -es, -ies, -ves, irregular, no change). Then ask each student to write one sentence for each group, checking that the verb agrees. For younger learners, use objects in the classroom and ask, "How many?"

Please note: this guide uses British spelling. Some nouns have two accepted plurals, and usage varies between countries. Students should follow the preference of their syllabus when it differs.
