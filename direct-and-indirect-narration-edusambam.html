Direct and Indirect Narration: The Complete Guide

Master direct and indirect speech, reporting verbs, tense changes, pronoun and time-word changes, questions, commands, requests, exclamations, suggestions, universal truths, and advanced transformations.

Narration, also called reported speech, is the way we tell another person what someone said. In direct speech, we reproduce the speaker's exact words inside quotation marks. In indirect speech, we report the meaning of those words without repeating the original sentence exactly. The skill is not simply a matter of changing a few verbs: accurate narration requires attention to the reporting verb, the relationship between the original speaker and the reporter, pronouns, tense, time and place expressions, sentence type, punctuation, and meaning.

1. What Is Direct Speech?

Direct speech gives the speaker's actual words as a quotation. The quoted words are treated as a separate utterance and are normally enclosed in quotation marks.

Examples

Ali said, “I am tired.”

Sarah said, “I will call you tomorrow.”

The teacher said, “Complete your work.”

The reporting clause may come before, after, or sometimes within the quotation. Punctuation depends on the position and style used.

2. What Is Indirect Speech?

Indirect speech reports what someone said without reproducing the exact wording. Quotation marks are removed, and the reported clause is grammatically integrated into the reporting sentence.

Direct → Indirect

Ali said, “I am tired.”

Ali said that he was tired.

Sarah said, “I will call you tomorrow.”

Sarah said that she would call me the next day.

Notice that several elements may change at once: I → he/she, am → was, will → would, and tomorrow → the next day. These changes are made only when the context requires them.

3. The Core Structure of Narration

A reported sentence commonly contains a reporting clause and a reported clause.

The meaning is reported, but the grammatical form is adapted to the new reporting context.

4. The Main Steps in Changing Direct to Indirect Speech

  1. Identify the reporting verb and the type of sentence being reported.
  2. Remove quotation marks and adjust punctuation.
  3. Choose the appropriate connector, such as that, if/whether, or a wh-word.
  4. Change pronouns according to who said the words and who is being addressed.
  5. Change tense where the reporting context requires backshift.
  6. Change time and place expressions when the reference point has changed.
  7. Preserve the original meaning rather than mechanically changing every word.

5. Pronoun Changes

Pronoun changes depend on person and meaning. A useful starting principle is that the first person generally changes according to the speaker, while the second person generally changes according to the person addressed.

Think About the Context

John said to Mary, “I will help you.” → John told Mary that he would help her.

Mary said to John, “You have helped me.” → Mary told John that he had helped her.

6. Tense Backshift: The Main Pattern

When the reporting verb is in the past, English often shifts the tense of the reported clause one step backwards. This is called backshift or sequence of tenses. It is a common pattern, not an automatic law in every context.

7. Present Simple → Past Simple

Examples

He said, “I work hard.”

He said that he worked hard.

She said, “I like this book.”

She said that she liked that book.

Do not change the tense merely because you see quotation marks. The reporting context determines whether backshift is appropriate.

8. Present Continuous → Past Continuous

Examples

Tom said, “I am studying.”

Tom said that he was studying.

They said, “We are waiting for the bus.”

They said that they were waiting for the bus.

9. Present Perfect → Past Perfect

Examples

She said, “I have finished the task.”

She said that she had finished the task.

10. Past Simple → Past Perfect

Examples

He said, “I saw the film.”

He said that he had seen the film.

This change is especially useful when the reported event is presented as earlier than the reporting time.

11. Past Continuous → Past Perfect Continuous

Example

She said, “I was waiting for an hour.”

She said that she had been waiting for an hour.

12. Future Forms

DirectIndirect
willwould
shallwould / should, depending on meaning
cancould
maymight
Examples

He said, “I will return soon.” → He said that he would return soon.

She said, “I can swim.” → She said that she could swim.

He said, “I may come.” → He said that he might come.

13. When Tense Backshift Is Not Necessary

Backshift is not an excuse to change a statement that is still true. If the reported information is a universal truth, scientific fact, permanent fact, or still-current situation, the original tense may remain unchanged.

Examples

The teacher said, “The Earth moves around the Sun.”

The teacher said that the Earth moves around the Sun.

He said, “Water boils at 100°C.”

He said that water boils at 100°C.

In some contexts, both forms may be grammatically possible, but the version that best expresses the intended meaning should be preferred.

14. Time Expressions

Time words are changed only when the time reference has changed.

Example

On Monday, Ali said, “I will finish it tomorrow.”

Later, we report: Ali said that he would finish it the next day.

15. Place and Demonstrative Changes

Words such as here, this, and these may change when the reporting location or viewpoint changes.

DirectPossible indirect form
herethere
thisthat
thesethose

Again, these are context-dependent rather than mechanical substitutions.

16. Statements: Using “That”

Statements are commonly introduced with that, although that may often be omitted in informal English after verbs such as say and think.

Examples

She said, “I am busy.”

She said that she was busy.

She said she was busy. (also possible)

After tell, an object is normally required: She told me that she was busy. We do not normally say She told that she was busy.

17. Say vs Tell

VerbPatternExample
saysay + statement / say to + personHe said that he was ready.
telltell + person + statementHe told me that he was ready.
Common Error

✗ He told that he was tired.

✓ He said that he was tired.

✓ He told me that he was tired.

18. Reporting Questions: Yes/No Questions

A yes/no question is commonly introduced by if or whether. The reported clause uses statement word order, not question word order.

Examples

He asked, “Are you ready?”

He asked if I was ready.

She asked, “Did they arrive?”

She asked whether they had arrived.

Do not retain the auxiliary inversion from the direct question.

Avoid This

✗ He asked if was I ready.

✓ He asked if I was ready.

19. Whether vs If

Both if and whether can introduce many reported yes/no questions. Whether is especially useful in more formal contexts and is required in some structures, such as before an infinitive.

Examples

She asked if I was coming.

She asked whether I was coming.

We must decide whether to stay.

20. Reporting Wh-Questions

Wh-questions retain the question word: what, who, whom, whose, which, when, where, why, how. The reported clause has normal statement order.

Examples

He asked, “Where do you live?” → He asked where I lived.

She asked, “Why are you crying?” → She asked why I was crying.

They asked, “When will he arrive?” → They asked when he would arrive.

21. Questions Whose Question Word Is Also the Subject

Some questions do not contain the usual auxiliary inversion because the wh-word itself is the subject.

Example

Direct: “Who broke the window?”

Indirect: He asked who had broken the window.

The transformation must preserve the grammatical role of who; do not insert an unnecessary subject.

22. Commands and Orders

Commands are commonly reported with structures such as tell + object + to-infinitive. Negative commands use not to.

Examples

The teacher said to Ali, “Open the book.”

The teacher told Ali to open the book.

The guard said, “Do not enter.”

The guard told us not to enter.

23. Requests

Requests can be reported with verbs such as ask, request, beg, urge and others. The reporting verb should reflect the force of the original utterance.

Examples

She said to me, “Please help me.”

She asked me to help her.

He said, “Please wait here.”

He asked us to wait there.

24. Advice

Advice can be reported with advise + object + to-infinitive.

Examples

The doctor said to him, “Take some rest.”

The doctor advised him to take some rest.

Mother said, “Do not waste your time.”

Mother advised me not to waste my time.

25. Invitations and Offers

Choose a reporting verb that captures the original meaning rather than forcing every utterance into said that.

Examples

He said, “Would you like to join us?” → He invited me to join them.

She said, “Shall I carry your bag?” → She offered to carry my bag.

26. Suggestions

Suggestions are especially important because their grammar changes according to the original wording. Common reported structures include suggest + gerund, suggest that + clause, and, in appropriate contexts, suggest that + should.

Examples

He said, “Let's take a break.”

He suggested taking a break.

He suggested that we should take a break.

Common Error

✗ He suggested me to take a break.

✓ He suggested taking a break.

✓ He suggested that I should take a break.

27. Exclamations

Exclamatory sentences express strong emotion. In reported speech, the reporting verb may change to exclaimed, exclaimed with joy, exclaimed with sorrow, exclaimed with surprise, or another suitable verb.

Examples

She said, “What a beautiful view!”

She exclaimed that it was a beautiful view.

He said, “How foolish I have been!”

He exclaimed that he had been very foolish.

28. Wishes and Prayers

Wishes and prayers require attention to the original emotional or optative meaning. Reporting verbs such as wish, pray, bless or expressions like expressed the hope may be appropriate depending on context.

Examples

He said, “May you succeed!”

He wished me success / wished that I might succeed.

She said, “May God help us!”

She prayed that God might help them.

29. Imperative Sentences: Choosing the Right Reporting Verb

The original command does not always need to become told. Meaning matters.

Original forceUseful reporting verbPattern
Commandtell / order / commandverb + object + to-infinitive
Requestask / request / begverb + object + to-infinitive
Adviceadviseverb + object + to-infinitive
Warningwarnwarn + object + to / against
Invitationinviteinvite + object + to

30. Reporting “Let”

Let can express permission, suggestion, or an appeal for an action. The reporting structure therefore depends on meaning.

Examples

Father said, “Let him go.” → Father told us to let him go.

He said, “Let's go for a walk.” → He suggested going for a walk.

She said, “Let me help you.” → She offered to help me.

31. Modals That Usually Do Not Backshift

Some modal forms do not normally change simply because the reporting verb is past. Examples include could, would, should, might, ought to. Their meaning and context still need to be considered.

Examples

He said, “I could help.” → He said that he could help.

She said, “You should study.” → She said that I should study.

32. Must: A Context-Sensitive Case

Must can express obligation or logical deduction. Its reported form depends on meaning and time reference. In many contexts, had to reports a past obligation, while must may remain when the obligation is still current.

Examples

He said, “I must leave now.” → He said that he had to leave then. (past obligation)

The teacher said, “You must wear a helmet.” → The teacher said that we must wear helmets. (rule still in force)

33. Reporting Verbs Beyond “Said”

Strong narration uses reporting verbs that communicate the speaker's intention accurately.

34. Reporting Verbs and Their Grammar Patterns

A reporting verb is not interchangeable with every other reporting verb. Each verb has its own grammatical pattern.

VerbTypical patternExample
telltell + object + that-clauseShe told me that she was busy.
askask + object + if/whether/wh-clauseHe asked me whether I agreed.
adviseadvise + object + to-infinitiveShe advised me to wait.
suggestsuggest + gerund / that-clauseHe suggested leaving early.
offeroffer + to-infinitiveShe offered to help.
refuserefuse + to-infinitiveHe refused to answer.

35. Direct → Indirect: A Full Worked Example

Step-by-Step

Direct: Ahmed said to Sara, “I am preparing for my examination now, and I will finish this work tomorrow.”

Step 1 — Reporting verb: said to → told.

Step 2 — Pronouns: I → he; my → his.

Step 3 — Tenses: am preparing → was preparing; will finish → would finish.

Step 4 — Time: now → then; tomorrow → the next day.

Final: Ahmed told Sara that he was preparing for his examination then and that he would finish that work the next day.

36. Direct → Indirect: A Question Worked Example

Step-by-Step

Direct: Maria said to John, “Where are you going tomorrow?”

Reporting verb: said to → asked.

Question word: where is retained.

Word order: you are → you were.

Time: tomorrow may become the next day if the reference point has changed.

Final: Maria asked John where he was going the next day.

37. Indirect → Direct Speech

Changing indirect speech back into direct speech is not simply reversing every word. The original wording is often unknown, so more than one direct sentence may be possible. The task is to reconstruct a natural direct utterance consistent with the reported meaning.

Examples

Indirect: Ali said that he was tired.

Possible direct: Ali said, “I am tired.”

Indirect: She told me that she would visit the following day.

Possible direct: She said to me, “I will visit you tomorrow.”

38. Indirect → Direct: Questions

When reconstructing a direct question, restore question word order and the appropriate punctuation.

Examples

Indirect: He asked me whether I was ready.

Direct: He said to me, “Are you ready?”

Indirect: She asked where I lived.

Direct: She said to me, “Where do you live?”

39. Indirect → Direct: Commands and Requests

Examples

Indirect: The teacher told the students to sit down.

Direct: The teacher said to the students, “Sit down.”

Indirect: She asked me to wait outside.

Direct: She said to me, “Please wait outside.”

40. Narration with Two Reporting Clauses

A sentence may contain more than one reporting clause. Each clause must be interpreted in its own context.

Example

Direct: He said, “I am tired,” and she replied, “You should rest.”

Indirect: He said that he was tired, and she replied that he should rest.

41. Narration with Multiple Speakers

When several speakers appear, identify each speaker before changing pronouns. Do not apply one pronoun change to the entire paragraph.

Example

Direct: Ali said, “I am ready.” Sara replied, “I am not.”

Indirect: Ali said that he was ready, but Sara replied that she was not.

42. Narration in the Present

If the reporting verb is in the present, backshift is usually unnecessary.

Examples

He says, “I am busy.” → He says that he is busy.

She asks, “Where do you live?” → She asks where I live.

43. Narration When the Reporting Verb Is Past but the Fact Remains Current

English allows the speaker to preserve a present tense when the information is still true.

Example

Yesterday, the scientist said, “The experiment is safe.”

Today, we may report: The scientist said that the experiment is safe.

Or, if we are simply reporting the past statement without emphasizing present truth: The scientist said that the experiment was safe.

The choice can therefore communicate a difference in viewpoint and current validity.

44. Reporting Facts, Beliefs and Opinions

Reported speech does not guarantee that the reported statement is true. The reporting verb can preserve the distinction between what someone said and what the reporter believes.

Example

He said that the plan was successful.

This sentence reports his statement; it does not necessarily assert that the plan really was successful.

45. Common Difficulties and Traps

  • Changing every present tense into a past tense even when the fact remains true.
  • Changing you without identifying whom the speaker addressed.
  • Keeping question word order after if, whether, or a wh-word.
  • Using tell without an object.
  • Using suggest + object + to-infinitive.
  • Changing every time expression regardless of context.
  • Using by or that mechanically instead of selecting a suitable reporting structure.
  • Forgetting that commands, requests, advice, offers, warnings and suggestions have different reporting patterns.
  • Assuming that an indirect sentence has only one possible direct reconstruction.

46. A Master Transformation Checklist

CheckQuestion to ask yourself
SpeakerWho originally said the words?
ListenerWho was being addressed?
Reporting verbIs this a statement, question, command, request, advice, suggestion, offer or another function?
PronounsWho does I, we, you, my, your, etc. refer to?

EDUSAMBAM Learning Principle

Good narration is not a word-swapping exercise. First understand who said what, to whom, when, where, and for what purpose. Then rebuild the sentence so that the grammar expresses the same meaning from the new reporting viewpoint.
