Informal Letter Writing

Personal letters follow looser rules than formal ones — but they still have a shape. Learn how to write to friends and family in a way that actually sounds like you.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 12 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Letter & Email Writing

An informal letter is a conversation on paper. Its structure exists to give the letter shape, not to make it sound stiff.

Informal letters are written to people you know personally: friends, family and close acquaintances. Unlike formal letters, they welcome contractions, personal opinions, humour and a warm, conversational tone. The structure is relaxed, but a few habits still make a letter easier and more pleasant to read. Informal does not mean careless. In school exams, spelling, punctuation and clear paragraphs still earn marks.

This guide covers the layout of an informal letter, how to open and close warmly, how to adjust your tone for the reader, and how to write one in an exam. It ends with worked examples, a checklist, a glossary and ten practice questions.

1.The Layout of an Informal Letter

Most informal letters have six parts. The layout is shorter than a formal letter's, because you do not write the recipient's address or a subject heading.

An informal letter, part by part
Numbers appear one after another. Match each to the list below.
  1. Address and date. Top right. In school exams, include your address and the date unless told otherwise.
  2. Greeting. "Dear Sana," or "Hi Sana,". A comma is the usual choice.
  3. Warm opening. Ask how they are, or mention something recent.
  4. The news. Short paragraphs, one topic in each. Share details and feelings.
  5. Closing line. Ask a question, make a plan, or ask for a reply.
  6. Sign-off and name. Write "Love", "Best wishes" or "Take care", then your first name.

Diagram 1. The six parts of an informal letter.

In school exams, an informal letter usually includes your address and the date at the top, but layouts vary, so follow your teacher's guide. In a real personal letter, many people write only the date. For the stricter layout used in official letters, see Formal Letter Writing.

2.The Greeting

Use the reader's first name, or the name you normally call them, such as "Grandma". A comma after the greeting is the usual choice. An exclamation mark ("Hey Sam!") is a style you may use with a very close friend, but it is not needed.

Example

Dear Priya,
Hi Grandma,
Hey Sam!

3.The Opening: Warm Up First

Unlike a formal letter, an informal letter does not need to state its purpose in the first sentence. It is natural to begin with a friendly line, such as asking how the person is, or mentioning something recent, before you come to your news. But do not wait too long. The reader should understand why you are writing within the first few lines.

Example

I hope you are doing well! It has been far too long since we last caught up, so I thought I would sit down and write.

4.The Body: Conversational, Not Rigid

The body can move between topics as a real conversation does: news, questions, small stories and opinions. Group related ideas into short paragraphs, so that the letter is easy to follow. Good informal letters often include:

  • Specific details. "I came second in the science fair" is better than "school was fine".
  • Feelings. Say how you felt, not only what happened.
  • Questions for the reader. "How did your match go?" makes the letter feel like a two-way conversation.
  • Natural linking words and phrases such as "Anyway," "By the way," and "Guess what?".
Formal letterInformal letter
I am writing to inform you...Just wanted to let you know...
I would be grateful if...Could you maybe...?
Please do not hesitate to contact me.Let me know if you need anything!
I apologise for the delay.Sorry it has taken me so long to write!

Contractions ("I'm", "we've", "can't"), phrasal verbs ("catch up", "find out") and idioms are natural here. You can find more in Idioms and Phrases. Humour is welcome if it suits your relationship with the reader. Be careful with sarcasm, because it is easily misunderstood on the page.

5.Informal, But Not Careless

Informal language has limits, especially in an exam. Avoid text-message spellings such as "u", "gr8" and "thx". Use capital letters and full stops properly. Use slang sparingly, because the examiner or an older reader may not know it. Write mostly in complete sentences, with an occasional short fragment for effect. Overusing exclamation marks can make a letter sound breathless, so save them for real excitement. The advice in Formal vs. Informal English explains the difference in more detail.

6.Adjusting Tone for the Reader

"Informal" is not a single fixed tone. A letter to a grandparent, a cousin and a new pen pal will all sound different, even though none follows the rules of a formal letter. The closer you are to the reader, the more relaxed you can be.

How relaxed should you be?
Tone changes with the reader.

Diagram 2. A best friend allows the most relaxed tone. A grandparent or a new pen pal deserves a little more care.

Same news, three readers
The news: you won second place in the science fair.
To your best friend
"Guess what?! I came second in the science fair! My volcano project went really well. You would have loved it."
To your grandmother
"I have some good news. My volcano project won second place at the science fair, and I was very proud."
To a new pen pal
"Last month my school held a science fair. My project about volcanoes came second, which made me very happy."

7.The Closing

End with a closing line that looks ahead: a question, a plan or a request for a reply. Then choose a sign-off that matches the relationship.

ReaderSuitable sign-off
Family and very close friendsLove, With love, Lots of love,
FriendsTake care, Best wishes, Your friend,
A new pen palBest wishes, Kind regards, Yours,

Do not use "Yours faithfully" or "Yours sincerely" in an informal letter. Those belong to formal letters.

8.Common Reasons for Writing

PurposeWhat to include
Sharing newsWhat happened, how you felt, and a question for the reader
InvitingThe event, date, time, place, and how to reply
ThankingWhat you are thanking them for, and how you used or enjoyed it
Describing a trip or eventWhere, when, who, a few vivid details, and your feelings
Giving adviceShow you understand, give two or three ideas, and offer support
ApologisingSay sorry plainly, explain briefly, and say how you will make it right

9.Informal Letters in Exams

An exam task will usually give you a situation, a reader and several points to cover. Read it twice and plan for a few minutes.

  • Cover every point in the task, each in its own paragraph.
  • Keep the register informal but correct, with a friendly tone throughout.
  • Include the address and date if your teacher expects them.
  • Add real detail. Examiners reward specific, personal touches.
  • Finish with a closing line and a sign-off, and leave a minute to check spelling and punctuation.

10.Worked Examples

Example A: From a quick text to a warm letter

The text message. "hey whats up, i got into the football team, u should come see the match sat. bring ur sis. c ya"

1Add the layout. Address and date, then "Dear Sana,".
2Add a warm opening. Ask how she is.
3Share the news with feeling. Say how it happened and how you felt.
4Make the invitation specific. Say when and where.
5Ask a question and close. Then sign off with "Take care,".

The letter.

15 Rose Avenue
Greenfield
17 October 2026

Dear Sana,

I hope you are well! I have not heard from you since the holidays, so I wanted to share some big news.

I have been chosen for the school football team! I could hardly believe it when the coach read out my name. I have practised every day after school for months, so it felt amazing.

Our first match is this Saturday at 10 o'clock on the school field. Would you like to come and watch? Please bring your sister too. It would be great to see you both.

Let me know if you can make it, and tell me how your own match went!

Take care,
Ali

Try it yourself: write the opening and closing lines of a thank-you letter to an aunt who sent you a book. Sample answer: Opening: Thank you so much for the book! It arrived this morning, and I started reading it straight away. Closing: Thank you again for thinking of me. Write back soon and tell me what you are reading. With love, (your name).

Example B: Planning an exam letter

The task. "Write a letter to a friend who lives in another country. Describe a festival in your town and say why you enjoyed it."

1Find the points. Describe the festival, and say why you enjoyed it.
2Plan the paragraphs. Warm opening, what the festival is, what you saw and did, why you enjoyed it, and an invitation.
3Choose vivid details. The colours, sounds, food and one small moment.
4Write and check. Keep the tone friendly, then check spelling and punctuation.

A possible middle paragraph: "The best part was the evening parade. Hundreds of lanterns glowed along the river, and a band played so loudly that I could feel the drums in my chest. I wished you were there to see it!"

13.Common Myths About Informal Letters

MythInformal means there are no rules.
FactInformal letters are relaxed, but they still need clear paragraphs, correct spelling and a suitable tone.
MythYou should use as much slang as possible.
FactUse slang sparingly. Readers of other ages or places may not know it.
MythEvery informal letter sounds the same.
FactThe tone changes with the reader, from a best friend to a grandparent.
MythLetters are outdated, so the skill is not useful.
FactThe same warm, personal style is used in friendly emails and messages. A handwritten letter can also mean a great deal to the person who receives it.

14.Key Terms

Informal letter: a personal letter to a friend or family member, in a relaxed tone.
Greeting: the opening words, such as "Dear Sana,".
Warm opening: a friendly first line before the main news.
Contraction: a short form such as "I'm" or "can't".
Tone: how the writing sounds to the reader.
Register: the level of formality in writing or speech.
Slang: very informal words used by particular groups.
Sign-off: the closing words before your name, such as "Love,".

15.Why This Matters

Knowing when to relax the rules of Formal Letter Writing, and by how much, is a skill of its own. Informal letters teach the flexible side of written communication, and this flexibility is useful again in friendly emails and in the series' later topics, such as Professional Email Etiquette and Job Applications and Cover Letters.

Key points to remember
  • An informal letter has six parts: address and date, greeting, warm opening, news, closing line, and sign-off.
  • Use a first-name greeting and a sign-off that matches the relationship.
  • Write short paragraphs with details, feelings and questions for the reader.
  • Informal is not careless. Avoid text-message spellings and check your spelling.
  • Adjust your tone to the reader.
  • In an exam, cover every point and keep the tone friendly throughout.
For teachers and parents

Activity: ask learners to write the same news to three readers (a best friend, a grandparent and a new pen pal) and compare how the tone changes. Then ask them to swap letters and check them with the checklist.

Please note: all examples here are original, written for teaching. The addresses are made up. Layouts differ between countries and syllabuses, so follow the style your course expects.
