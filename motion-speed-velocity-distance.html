Motion: Speed, Velocity, and Distance

From a walking student to a falling skydiver: distance, speed, velocity and acceleration, motion graphs and the equations of motion, explained with diagrams and worked examples.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 15 min read | Science | Last updated: October 2026

Sitting still in a train, you are not moving relative to your seat, yet you are moving at over a hundred kilometres per hour relative to the ground. Even then, the Earth carries both you and the ground around the Sun at about 30 kilometres every second. Motion is always described relative to something, and to describe it clearly physicists use a small set of precise ideas: distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration. Once these are clear, you can analyse everything from a walking student to a falling raindrop and understand why speed limits exist.

What you will be able to do
  • Distinguish distance from displacement, and speed from velocity.
  • Calculate speed, acceleration and distance with correct units and unit conversions.
  • Read distance–time and velocity–time graphs, using slopes and areas.
  • Use v = u + at, s = ut + ½at² and v² = u² + 2as, and explain stopping distances.

1.Distance and Displacement

Distance is the total length of the path an object travels. It is a scalar quantity: it has size only and is never negative. Displacement is the straight-line change in position from start to finish, together with its direction. It is a vector quantity: it has both size and direction.

Suppose a student walks 40 m east along a corridor and then 40 m back west. The distance travelled is 80 m, but the displacement is zero, because she finished where she started. Both are measured in metres (m).

2.Speed

Speed is how fast an object covers distance.

Formula

speed = distance ÷ time  (v = s ÷ t)

The SI unit is metres per second (m/s). Road speeds are often given in kilometres per hour (km/h).

km/h → m/s: divide by 3.6.   m/s → km/h: multiply by 3.6.

Where does 3.6 come from? One kilometre is 1000 m and one hour is 3600 s, so 1 km/h = 1000 ÷ 3600 = 1 ÷ 3.6 m/s.

Most real journeys do not happen at one steady speed. The average speed is the total distance divided by the total time, including any stops. The instantaneous speed is the speed at one moment, which is what a car's speedometer shows.

Worked example 1: average speed

A bus travels 150 km in 2.5 hours, including a short break. Find its average speed in km/h and in m/s.

Step 1. v = s ÷ t = 150 km ÷ 2.5 h = 60 km/h.

Step 2. Convert: 60 ÷ 3.6 ≈ 16.7 m/s.

3.Velocity

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement: velocity = displacement ÷ time. It tells you both how fast and in which direction. Two cars travelling at 20 m/s in opposite directions have the same speed but opposite velocities, which we show with signs: +20 m/s and −20 m/s.

The student in the corridor walked for 80 s in total, so her average speed was 80 m ÷ 80 s = 1 m/s, but her average velocity was 0 ÷ 80 = 0 m/s. An object moving at a constant speed around a circle has a velocity that keeps changing, because its direction keeps changing.

Relative velocity: if a car travelling at 80 km/h overtakes another travelling at 60 km/h in the same direction, the faster car moves at 20 km/h relative to the slower one. For two vehicles approaching each other at 80 km/h and 60 km/h, the closing speed is 140 km/h.

4.Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes. A car speeding up, slowing down or turning a corner is accelerating.

Formula

a = (v − u) ÷ t

a = acceleration (m/s²), v = final velocity (m/s), u = initial velocity (m/s), t = time taken (s).

A negative acceleration (slowing down) is often called deceleration or retardation.

The unit m/s² means "metres per second, per second": each second the velocity changes by that many metres per second. An acceleration of 3 m/s² means that the velocity increases by 3 m/s every second.

Worked example 2: acceleration

A cyclist speeds up from 2.0 m/s to 8.0 m/s in 4.0 s. Find the acceleration.

a = (v − u) ÷ t = (8.0 − 2.0) ÷ 4.0 = 6.0 ÷ 4.0 = 1.5 m/s².

5.Distance–Time Graphs

Graphs make motion easy to see. On a distance–time graph, time is on the horizontal axis and distance on the vertical axis.

  • A straight sloping line means constant speed. The gradient (slope) equals the speed.
  • A horizontal line means the object is stationary.
  • A curve that gets steeper means the object is speeding up.
Figure 1. A distance–time graph. In stage A the gradient is 40 m ÷ 20 s = 2 m/s. Stage B is flat, so the object is stationary. In stage C the object returns to the start at the same speed. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)

6.Velocity–Time Graphs

On a velocity–time graph, the vertical axis shows velocity. It carries more information than a distance–time graph:

  • The gradient equals the acceleration.
  • A horizontal line means constant velocity (zero acceleration).
  • The area under the graph equals the distance travelled (the displacement).
Figure 2. A velocity–time graph. The acceleration in the first stage is 20 ÷ 5 = 4 m/s²; the shaded area is the total distance. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)
Worked example 3: reading the graph in Figure 2

Find the acceleration in the first stage and the total distance travelled.

Step 1. Acceleration = gradient = (20 − 0) ÷ (5 − 0) = 4 m/s².

Step 2. Area of the first triangle: ½ × 5 × 20 = 50 m.

Step 3. Area of the rectangle: 10 s × 20 m/s = 200 m.

Step 4. Area of the last triangle: ½ × 5 × 20 = 50 m.

Step 5. Total distance = 50 + 200 + 50 = 300 m.

7.The Equations of Motion

When an object moves in a straight line with constant acceleration, four equations connect the five quantities u (initial velocity), v (final velocity), a (acceleration), t (time) and s (displacement):

Equations (constant acceleration)

Want to see why these equations are true? Our companion article, Equations of Motion: Three Equations and Their Proofs, derives each one using algebra, velocity–time graphs, calculus and energy, with many more worked examples.

v = u + at

s = ut + ½at²

v² = u² + 2as

s = ½(u + v)t

Strategy: list what you know, identify what you want, and pick the equation that has exactly those quantities.

Worked example 4: starting from rest

A car starts from rest and accelerates at 3.0 m/s² for 8.0 s. Find its final velocity and the distance covered.

Known: u = 0, a = 3.0 m/s², t = 8.0 s.

v = u + at = 0 + 3.0 × 8.0 = 24 m/s (about 86 km/h).

s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 3.0 × 8.0² = ½ × 3.0 × 64 = 96 m.

Worked example 5: braking

A car moving at 20 m/s brakes with a constant deceleration of 5.0 m/s² until it stops. Find the braking distance and time.

Known: u = 20 m/s, v = 0, a = −5.0 m/s².

v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 400 + 2(−5.0)s → 10s = 400 → s = 40 m.

v = u + at → 0 = 20 − 5.0t → t = 4.0 s.

8.Free Fall and Terminal Velocity

An object falling with only gravity acting on it is in free fall. Near the Earth's surface, every freely falling object accelerates downward at the same rate, the acceleration due to gravity, g. The standard value is 9.80665 m/s², usually rounded to 9.8 m/s² (or 10 m/s² for quick estimates). Galileo is traditionally credited with arguing that, in the absence of air resistance, heavy and light objects fall together. In 1971 Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott demonstrated this on the Moon, where there is almost no air, by dropping a hammer and a feather; they landed at the same time.

Worked example 6: a falling stone

A stone is dropped from rest and falls for 3.0 s. Ignoring air resistance, find its speed and the distance fallen (g = 9.8 m/s²).

v = u + at = 0 + 9.8 × 3.0 = 29.4 m/s.

s = ½at² = ½ × 9.8 × 3.0² = 44.1 m.

On Earth, air resistance matters. As an object speeds up, the upward air resistance grows. Eventually it equals the object's weight. The forces are then balanced, so the acceleration is zero and the object falls at a constant speed called the terminal velocity. A skydiver in a spread position reaches roughly 55 m/s (about 200 km/h). Opening a parachute greatly increases air resistance, so the skydiver slows to a new, much lower terminal velocity that is safe for landing.

9.Stopping Distances and Road Safety

A driver cannot stop instantly. The stopping distance has two parts:

  • Thinking distance: how far the vehicle travels during the driver's reaction time (the delay before the brakes are applied). Thinking distance = speed × reaction time, so it is proportional to speed.
  • Braking distance: how far the vehicle travels once the brakes are on. It is proportional to the square of the speed, as v² = 2as shows.
Worked example 7: stopping distance

A driver with a reaction time of 0.70 s is travelling at 20 m/s (72 km/h). The brakes give a deceleration of 5.0 m/s². Find the stopping distance. Then repeat it for 40 m/s (144 km/h).

At 20 m/s: thinking distance = 20 × 0.70 = 14 m; braking distance = 20² ÷ (2 × 5.0) = 40 m. Stopping distance = 54 m.

At 40 m/s: thinking distance = 40 × 0.70 = 28 m; braking distance = 40² ÷ 10 = 160 m. Stopping distance = 188 m.

Figure 4. Stopping distance grows much faster than speed. Bars are drawn to scale. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)

Many factors make stopping distance longer: higher speed, a tired, distracted or impaired driver (longer reaction time), wet or icy roads, worn tyres and worn brakes, and a heavily loaded vehicle. The World Health Organization identifies speed as a key risk factor for road traffic injuries and recommends measures such as speed limits, seat belts and child restraints. Seat belts, airbags and crumple zones all work by increasing the time over which a person's velocity changes in a crash, which reduces the force.

10.Measuring Motion in the Lab

  • Stopwatch and metre rule: time a trolley over a measured distance; repeat and take the mean.
  • Light gates: a card of known length breaks a light beam; the computer divides the length by the time to find speed with great precision.
  • Ticker-timer: a vibrating marker makes dots on tape at equal time intervals (typically 50 per second). Evenly spaced dots mean constant velocity; increasingly spaced dots mean acceleration.
  • Video analysis: record motion next to a ruler and read positions frame by frame.
Worked example 8: measuring reaction time

A friend drops a ruler and you catch it after it has fallen 20 cm. Find your reaction time.

Step 1. From rest, s = ½gt², so t = √(2s ÷ g).

Step 2. s = 0.20 m, so t = √(2 × 0.20 ÷ 9.8) = √0.0408 ≈ 0.20 s.

A typical result is around 0.2 s for simple catching. Reaction times when driving are usually longer, because the driver must notice, decide and move a foot.

11.Common Mistakes to Avoid

Watch out
  • Mixing up speed and velocity. Velocity includes direction; use signs.
  • Forgetting to convert units. Convert km/h to m/s (÷ 3.6) and minutes to seconds before using the equations.
  • Reading a distance–time graph as a velocity–time graph. On a d–t graph the slope is speed; on a v–t graph the slope is acceleration and the area is distance.
  • Thinking a flat line on a v–t graph means "not moving". It means constant velocity. A stationary object has a flat line at zero.
  • Using the equations when acceleration is not constant. They only work for constant acceleration.
  • Thinking heavier objects fall faster. Without air resistance they fall at the same rate; a feather falls slowly on Earth because of air resistance.
  • Forgetting that a zero-acceleration skydiver can still be moving fast. At terminal velocity, acceleration is zero but speed is high.
  • Believing braking distance doubles when speed doubles. It quadruples.

12.Check Your Understanding

Try each question before opening the answer.

1. A runner completes one 400 m lap of a track in 50 s. State her distance, displacement and average speed.

Distance = 400 m. Displacement = 0 m (she is back at the start). Average speed = 400 ÷ 50 = 8 m/s.

2. Convert 90 km/h to m/s.

90 ÷ 3.6 = 25 m/s.

3. A train accelerates from 10 m/s to 30 m/s in 40 s. What is its acceleration?

a = (30 − 10) ÷ 40 = 0.50 m/s².

4. On a distance–time graph, what does a horizontal line mean?

The object is stationary (its distance is not changing).

5. A velocity–time graph shows a straight line from 0 to 12 m/s over 6 s. Find the distance travelled.

The area is a triangle: ½ × 6 × 12 = 36 m.

6. A ball is thrown straight up at 14.7 m/s. How long does it take to reach its highest point? (g = 9.8 m/s²)

At the top v = 0. 0 = 14.7 − 9.8t, so t = 14.7 ÷ 9.8 = 1.5 s.

7. Why is a skydiver's acceleration zero at terminal velocity?

Air resistance upward equals weight downward. The forces are balanced, so there is no resultant force and no acceleration.

8. Why does doubling a car's speed more than double its stopping distance?

Thinking distance doubles, but braking distance is proportional to the square of the speed, so it increases fourfold.

Summary

  • Distance and speed are scalars; displacement and velocity are vectors. Speed = distance ÷ time; 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h.
  • Acceleration a = (v − u) ÷ t, in m/s².
  • On a d–t graph, slope = speed. On a v–t graph, slope = acceleration and area = distance.
  • For constant acceleration: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as.
  • Free fall near Earth has g ≈ 9.8 m/s². At terminal velocity, weight equals air resistance.
  • Braking distance rises with the square of speed, so speed limits save lives.
For teachers and parents

Easy experiments: time students walking and running over a measured corridor and plot distance–time graphs; roll a trolley down a ramp and use a phone video to read its positions; use the ruler-drop test for reaction times and compare results with and without a distraction; drop a flat sheet of paper and the same sheet crumpled to discuss air resistance. Never test braking with real vehicles, and keep drop experiments on the floor level or low. Numerical values are rounded; please check that they match the data booklet used by your examination board.
