R18: Comparing and Contrasting Texts

A focused Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 unit on comparing ideas, perspectives, language choices, evidence and effects across texts, while maintaining a clear and balanced line of reasoning.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|Unit 26|English Language

Comparing texts is more than finding things that are the same or different. A strong comparison identifies a meaningful relationship between the texts, selects precise evidence from both, and explains how the writers present their ideas, experiences or viewpoints.

1. Unit Purpose

R18: Comparing and Contrasting Texts develops the ability to move between two texts without losing focus. You will learn how to compare what writers say and how they say it, using balanced evidence and clear analytical reasoning.

EDUSAMBAM principle

COMPARE THE IDEA → SELECT EVIDENCE FROM BOTH → ANALYSE THE DIFFERENCE OR SIMILARITY → EXPLAIN THE EFFECT

2. Learning Outcomes

  • identify the exact comparison focus in a question;
  • summarise similarities and differences accurately;
  • select relevant evidence from both texts;
  • compare ideas, experiences, attitudes and perspectives;
  • compare language and structural choices when required;
  • explain how similar methods can create different effects;
  • explain how different methods can communicate a similar idea;
  • maintain balance between Text A and Text B;
  • use comparative vocabulary precisely;
  • integrate evidence smoothly from both texts;
  • develop comparisons rather than listing observations;
  • reach a supported overall judgement where appropriate.

3. What Does “Compare” Really Mean?

To compare means to examine two things together and identify a meaningful relationship between them. That relationship may involve similarity, difference, development, degree or contrast.

Simple comparison

Text A: The journey is presented as exciting.

Text B: The journey is presented as frightening.

Comparison: Both writers focus on the uncertainty of the journey, but Text A presents uncertainty as exciting whereas Text B presents it as threatening.

4. Start With the Question

Underline the words that tell you what to compare. A question about attitudes is not the same as a question about language. A question about experiences is not automatically a question about writing techniques.

Question focus

Compare what the writers think about working alone.

Your main task is to compare the writers' views. Language analysis may support your comparison, but it should not replace the comparison of ideas.

5. Similarity Is Not Enough

“Both texts mention travel” is a weak comparison. A stronger answer explains what each writer says about travel and how their views relate.

Developing the point

Both writers describe travel as demanding, but Text A treats difficulty as a rewarding challenge, whereas Text B emphasises exhaustion and inconvenience.

6. Difference Is Not Enough

A list of differences does not automatically become analysis. Explain the significance of the difference.

Weak → strong

Weak: Text A is positive. Text B is negative.

Strong: Text A uses enthusiastic descriptions to make the experience seem worthwhile, while Text B focuses on discomfort and obstacles, creating a much more critical perspective.

7. Build a Comparison Grid

8. Compare Like With Like

Make the comparison on the same dimension. If Text A is being discussed for its attitude towards risk, Text B should be considered for its attitude towards risk—not for an unrelated feature.

Useful question

What does each writer think about the same issue?

9. Compare Ideas

Ideas may include opinions about education, travel, technology, work, nature, competition, responsibility, change or human behaviour.

Example

Text A presents technology as a tool that increases independence, whereas Text B argues that excessive dependence on technology can reduce practical skills.

10. Compare Experiences

Two writers may describe the same type of experience but respond to it differently.

Example

Both writers experience a difficult journey, but Text A emphasises discovery and achievement while Text B focuses on uncertainty and physical discomfort.

11. Compare Perspectives

A perspective is the way a person or writer views an issue. It is influenced by experience, purpose, attitude and position.

Example

A resident may describe a new development as disruptive, while a visitor may describe the same development as exciting and modern.

12. Compare Attitudes

Attitudes can be enthusiastic, doubtful, amused, critical, admiring, frustrated, sympathetic, cautious or mixed. Always support the label with evidence.

Evidence-led comparison

Text A's admiration is suggested by the praise “remarkably skilled”, while Text B's reservations are shown by the phrase “not without problems”.

13. Compare Tone

Tone is the manner or emotional quality of expression. Two texts may discuss the same subject in different tones.

Example

Both writers discuss school examinations, but Text A uses a humorous tone to reduce anxiety, whereas Text B uses a serious and cautionary tone to emphasise their consequences.

14. Compare Language Choices

When the task requires language comparison, identify the actual words or phrases used by both writers.

Example

Text A describes the sea as “glittering”, creating an attractive image, while Text B calls it “restless”, giving the same environment a more unsettled quality.

15. Similar Method, Different Effect

The same technique does not guarantee the same effect. Context matters.

Example

Both writers use personification. Text A's “the wind danced” makes nature seem lively and welcoming, whereas Text B's “the wind attacked” makes it seem aggressive and threatening.

16. Different Method, Similar Effect

Writers may use different techniques to create a similar response.

Example

Text A uses a vivid metaphor to make the city seem overwhelming; Text B uses a long list of sounds and movements to create the same impression of intense activity.

17. Compare Evidence, Not Just Topics

Both texts may mention the same topic, but the evidence may reveal very different meanings.

Remember

Same topic ≠ same viewpoint. Always ask what the writer says about the topic.

18. Select Evidence From Both Texts

A comparison must be supported by evidence from both texts. Select evidence that represents the point accurately and gives you something useful to explain.

Two-text evidence

Text A: “a chance to discover the unknown”

Text B: “a risk no sensible person would take”

The contrasting descriptions provide direct evidence of sharply different attitudes.

19. Integrate Two Quotations

Where appropriate, place evidence from both texts into the same analytical sentence.

Model

Text A presents the challenge as “a chance to discover”, suggesting enthusiasm, whereas Text B describes it as “a risk”, framing the same challenge as dangerous.

20. Use Comparative Connectives

bothunlikeon the other hand
in the same wayrather thanby comparison

Use connectives because they clarify the relationship between ideas—not simply to decorate the paragraph.

21. Balance Your Comparison

A common weakness is to analyse Text A in detail and then add a short sentence about Text B. Keep both texts visible throughout the response.

Balanced structure

Text A point + evidence + explanation → Text B point + evidence + explanation → relationship between them.

22. Avoid “Text A Paragraph, Text B Paragraph”

Separate paragraphs can work, but if they prevent direct comparison they can become descriptive. A comparative paragraph should repeatedly connect the two texts.

Better habit

Instead of writing everything about Text A first, compare one meaningful aspect at a time: attitude → evidence → contrast → explanation.

23. Compare Degree, Not Only Direction

Both writers may be positive, but one may be much more enthusiastic. Both may be concerned, but one may express stronger alarm.

Example

Both writers recognise the value of exercise, but Text A is strongly enthusiastic, describing it as “essential”, whereas Text B is more cautious, suggesting that benefits depend on moderation.

24. Compare Change Within a Text

A writer's position may shift. Compare the beginning and later sections before comparing the two texts with each other.

Example

Text A initially appears doubtful but becomes more positive after describing the results. Text B remains sceptical throughout. This makes their final positions noticeably different.

25. Compare Purpose and Audience When Relevant

Purpose and audience can help explain why two writers present a subject differently. Do not mention them mechanically; use them when the text supports the inference.

Example

A promotional text may emphasise benefits to encourage readers to participate, while a reflective account may focus more heavily on difficulties because its purpose is to recount personal experience.

26. A Strong Comparative Paragraph

Model paragraph

Both writers recognise that the journey is demanding, but they interpret that difficulty differently. Text A calls it “a challenge worth taking”, using the positive noun “challenge” to frame difficulty as an opportunity for achievement. In contrast, Text B describes the journey as “an exhausting ordeal”, whose noun “ordeal” presents the experience as something to endure rather than enjoy. Therefore, although both acknowledge hardship, Text A is more optimistic about its value while Text B remains strongly negative.

27. Comparison Ladder

Build upward

Level 1: Both mention the journey.

Level 2: Both say the journey is difficult.

Level 3: Both present difficulty, but with different attitudes.

Level 4: Their different word choices reveal contrasting attitudes and position the reader differently.

28. Common Weaknesses

  • writing about only one text;
  • summarising instead of comparing;
  • listing techniques without explaining their relevance;
  • using quotations without explanation;
  • making a similarity sound like a difference;
  • using “similarly” or “however” when the ideas are not actually related;
  • comparing unrelated details;
  • giving unequal attention to the two texts;
  • making claims that the evidence does not support.

29. Improving a Weak Comparison

Weak

Text A likes the city. Text B does not. Text A says “beautiful”. Text B says “dirty”.

Improved

Text A views the city positively, calling it “beautiful”, a word that highlights its attractiveness. In contrast, Text B describes the same environment as “dirty”, shifting attention towards neglect and unpleasantness. The opposing descriptions reveal sharply different attitudes towards the city.

30. Exam Strategy: C-L-E-A-R

C-L-E-A-R method

C — Compare: Identify the relationship between the texts.

L — Locate: Find precise evidence from both.

E — Explain: Analyse what each piece of evidence shows.

A — Assess: Consider degree, significance or effect where appropriate.

R — Return: Link the comparison back to the exact question.

31. Final Accuracy Check

  • Have I answered the exact comparison question?
  • Have I discussed both texts?
  • Does every comparison involve the same issue or dimension?
  • Have I selected precise evidence from both texts?
  • Have I explained rather than merely quoted?
  • Have I shown meaningful similarities and differences?
  • Have I used comparative language accurately?
  • Have I kept the two texts balanced?
  • Have I avoided unsupported assumptions?
  • Have I checked that each quotation is accurate?

32. Worked Cambridge-Style Example

Read and compare

Text A: “The mountain path was demanding, but every turn revealed another spectacular view. I felt tired, yet I wanted to continue.”

Text B: “The climb seemed endless. My legs ached, the wind cut through my coat, and I began to wonder why I had agreed to come.”

Question: Compare how the writers present their experiences of climbing the mountain.

Strong answer: Both writers acknowledge that climbing the mountain is physically difficult, but Text A treats the difficulty as worthwhile whereas Text B focuses on discomfort and regret. Text A admits that the path is “demanding”, yet the positive description “spectacular view” makes the effort seem rewarding; the statement that the writer “wanted to continue” reinforces this enthusiasm. By contrast, Text B describes the climb as “endless” and emphasises physical suffering through “my legs ached”, before questioning the decision to participate. Thus, both writers experience hardship, but Text A sees it as part of a rewarding adventure while Text B presents it as increasingly unpleasant.

33. Final R18 Checklist

  • I know exactly what the question asks me to compare.
  • I have identified a meaningful similarity or difference.
  • I have evidence from both texts.
  • I have integrated evidence rather than dumping quotations.
  • I have explained what each quotation shows.
  • I have compared attitudes, ideas, experiences or methods accurately.
  • I have considered degree where useful.
  • I have used comparative connectives naturally.
  • I have maintained balance between the texts.
  • I have avoided irrelevant technique-spotting.
  • I have supported my interpretation with evidence.
  • My final point answers the question directly.
Key takeaway

A real comparison connects two texts. Do not simply describe Text A and Text B separately. Put their ideas and evidence into conversation with each other and explain what the relationship means.
