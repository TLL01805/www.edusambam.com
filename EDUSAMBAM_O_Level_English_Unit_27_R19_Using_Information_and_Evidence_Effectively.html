R19: Using Information and Evidence Effectively

A focused Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 unit on selecting, organising, integrating and evaluating information and evidence so that every point is accurate, relevant and clearly supported.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|Unit 27|English Language

Strong answers do not simply contain information. They use the right information for the right purpose, select evidence carefully, explain its significance and keep every statement connected to the question.

1. Unit Purpose

R19: Using Information and Evidence Effectively develops the ability to turn reading material into accurate, purposeful answers. You will learn how to distinguish relevant information from detail that does not help answer the question.

EDUSAMBAM principle

SELECT → CHECK → ORGANISE → INTEGRATE → EXPLAIN

2. Learning Outcomes

  • identify information directly relevant to a question;
  • distinguish key information from supporting detail;
  • select accurate evidence;
  • avoid irrelevant information;
  • combine information from different parts of a text;
  • combine information from two texts when required;
  • paraphrase accurately;
  • use short quotations appropriately;
  • explain why evidence supports a point;
  • organise information logically;
  • avoid unsupported assumptions;
  • check accuracy before submitting an answer.

3. What Is Evidence?

Evidence is information from a text that supports a statement or interpretation. It may be a fact, detail, example, quotation, description, action or clearly expressed viewpoint.

Example

If you claim that a character is nervous, evidence might include the character's repeated hesitation, physical reactions or anxious words.

4. Information vs. Evidence

Information tells you something. Evidence is information selected because it supports a particular point.

Distinction

Information: The expedition lasted three weeks.

Evidence for determination: The writer continued despite being forced to change plans several times.

5. Read the Question First

Before collecting details, identify the exact task. Circle or underline the key words. Ask: What information do I actually need?

Example

If the question asks why an expedition was difficult, dates and names may be less important than weather, terrain, equipment problems and physical demands.

6. Relevance

Relevant information helps answer the question directly. Interesting information is not necessarily useful information.

Quick test

Ask: If I remove this detail, does my answer become less accurate or less complete? If not, the detail may be unnecessary.

7. Key Information

Key information is essential to the answer. Supporting details strengthen it, but not every supporting detail needs to be included.

Example

For a question about reasons for a decision, identify the major reasons first. Then select one or two precise details that demonstrate each reason.

8. Supporting Detail

Supporting detail can clarify, illustrate or strengthen a main point.

Example

Main point: The journey was physically demanding.
Support: The writer describes steep ground, exhaustion and limited opportunities to rest.

9. Evidence Must Be Accurate

Do not change the meaning of the source. If you paraphrase, preserve the original idea. If you quote, copy the relevant words accurately.

Accuracy warning

If a writer says an event was “unexpected”, do not paraphrase it as “impossible”. The second statement is stronger and may distort the source.

10. Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing expresses source information in your own words while keeping its meaning.

Example

Source idea: The workers had little time to prepare because the announcement came suddenly.

Paraphrase: The workers were poorly prepared because they received very little warning.

11. When to Quote

A short quotation is useful when the writer's exact wording is important. Select the smallest amount of text needed to support your point.

Better evidence

Instead of copying a whole sentence, use a precise phrase such as “little warning” and explain what it demonstrates.

12. Avoid Quotation Dumping

Several quotations without explanation do not demonstrate understanding. Evidence should be followed by a clear explanation of its relevance.

Evidence chain

Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link to question.

13. The Evidence Sandwich

Method

Before: Introduce the point.

Middle: Give the relevant evidence.

After: Explain what the evidence proves or suggests.

14. Explain the Evidence

Do not assume that the examiner will make the connection for you. State clearly why the evidence supports your point.

Model

The writer repeatedly mentions delays and failed arrangements. This suggests that the plan was difficult to control and depended on circumstances outside the writer's influence.

15. Combine Information

Some questions require information from different parts of the same text. Search beyond the first paragraph and bring related details together.

Example

A writer may introduce a problem at the beginning, explain its cause later and describe its consequence near the end. A complete answer connects all three.

16. Track the Question Across the Text

As you read, keep the question in mind. Mark only information connected to the task.

Reading habit

Use brief margin labels such as CAUSE, EFFECT, ATTITUDE, EXAMPLE or CONTRAST.

17. Separate Fact From Inference

A fact is directly stated or clearly established. An inference is a reasonable conclusion drawn from evidence. Do not present an unsupported inference as a fact.

Example

If the writer says she checked the equipment three times, you can infer that she was concerned about safety. You should not claim that she had previously experienced an accident unless the text supports it.

18. Avoid Adding Outside Knowledge

When a reading question is based on a supplied text, answer from the text unless the task specifically invites outside knowledge.

Rule

Text-based question → text-based evidence. Do not replace what the writer says with what you already know about the subject.

19. Organise Information by Idea

Do not simply reproduce the order in which every detail appears. Group related information when the question requires a synthesis.

Example

Information about cost, equipment and transport can be grouped under practical difficulties if that reflects the question.

20. Organise by Cause and Effect

Cause explains why something happened. Effect explains what happened as a result.

Model

Cause: Heavy rain made the track unstable. Effect: The group had to change its route and arrived later than planned.

21. Organise by Problem and Solution

Some texts naturally present a difficulty followed by a response. Recognising this structure can help you select information accurately.

Model

Problem: Supplies were running low. Response: The team reduced its daily consumption and changed its schedule.

22. Use the Source's Scope Carefully

Do not generalise beyond what the text supports. “The writer found this route difficult” does not necessarily mean “everyone would find the route difficult.”

Precision

Use words such as the writer, the group, the report, the experience described when the evidence applies specifically to the source.

23. Avoid Repetition

Repeating the same information in different words does not create a fuller answer. Select additional evidence only when it adds a distinct point.

Check

Before adding another sentence, ask: Does this introduce a new idea, a useful qualification or stronger evidence?

24. Use Precise Verbs

Precise reporting verbs make evidence handling clearer.

BasicMore precise
saysstates, explains, argues, suggests
showsdemonstrates, reveals, indicates
talks aboutdescribes, discusses, focuses on
thinksbelieves, questions, doubts, argues

25. Integrate Evidence Smoothly

Evidence should fit naturally into your sentence.

Model

The writer suggests that the situation is unpredictable by describing the weather as “constantly changing”.

26. Evaluate the Strength of Evidence

Some evidence directly supports a point; other evidence only partly supports it. Strong readers distinguish between them.

Example

The statement “I noticed the clouds” is weak evidence for fear. The later description “I began to worry that we would not return before dark” is much stronger evidence of anxiety.

27. Cross-Check Details

Before writing, check names, numbers, sequence, causes and consequences against the text. Small inaccuracies can change meaning.

Final check

Who? What? When? Where? Why? What happened as a result?

28. Common Weaknesses

  • including every detail instead of selecting relevant information;
  • copying large portions of the text;
  • paraphrasing inaccurately;
  • using evidence without explanation;
  • adding assumptions not supported by the source;
  • repeating the same point;
  • answering a different question from the one asked;
  • confusing cause with consequence;
  • using outside knowledge when it is not required.

29. Improve a Weak Answer

Weak

The journey was hard. There was rain. The road was bad. They were tired. They had problems.

Improved

The journey was difficult because heavy rain affected the route and made progress slower. The writer also describes increasing tiredness, showing that the physical demands became more serious as the journey continued. These details together explain both the practical and physical difficulties.

30. Exam Strategy: S-E-L-E-C-T

SELECT method

S — Study: Read the question carefully.

E — Extract: Find information that answers it.

L — Link: Connect related details.

E — Explain: Show how the evidence supports the point.

C — Check: Verify accuracy and relevance.

T — Tie back: Make sure the final answer addresses the question.

31. Final Accuracy Checklist

  • Have I answered the exact question?
  • Is every major detail relevant?
  • Have I selected rather than copied unnecessarily?
  • Is my paraphrasing faithful to the source?
  • Are my quotations accurate?
  • Have I explained important evidence?
  • Have I combined information where necessary?
  • Have I separated evidence from inference?
  • Have I avoided unsupported assumptions?
  • Have I checked names, facts, sequence and causes?

32. Worked Cambridge-Style Example

Read and select

Text: “When the community centre first opened, only a few residents attended. The organisers responded by changing the timetable and introducing evening sessions. Within two months, attendance had increased substantially, particularly among people who worked during the day.”

Question: Explain what the organisers did to increase attendance and why the changes were effective.

Strong answer: The organisers changed the timetable and introduced evening sessions after noticing that initial attendance was low. These changes were effective because they made the centre accessible to people who worked during the day. The later increase in attendance provides direct evidence that the new arrangements reached more residents.

33. Final R19 Checklist

  • I know exactly what information the question requires.
  • I have selected relevant evidence.
  • I have avoided unnecessary detail.
  • I have kept the source's meaning accurate.
  • I can distinguish information from inference.
  • I have combined related details when needed.
  • I have organised my answer logically.
  • I have explained why evidence matters.
  • I have checked every factual detail against the text.
  • My answer remains focused from beginning to end.
Key takeaway

Good evidence is not simply information copied from a text. It is carefully selected, accurately represented, logically organised and clearly connected to the purpose of the answer.
