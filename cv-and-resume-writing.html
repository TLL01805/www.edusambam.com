CV and Resume Writing: A Step-by-Step Guide

Employers may read dozens of applications for one job. Learn how to build a CV step by step, from collecting your evidence to checking the final file, so that the reader wants to meet you.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 16 min read | English

An employer may receive dozens, or even hundreds, of applications for a single job. Your CV is usually the first thing that person reads, and often the only thing, before deciding who to invite for an interview. A strong CV does not list everything you have ever done. It gives a busy reader clear evidence, in a few pages, that you can do this particular job. This guide shows you how to build that kind of CV step by step, from collecting your evidence to checking the final file.

Key Takeaways

A CV has one job: to win an interview. Collect your evidence first, choose a simple format, and begin each bullet point with an action verb that shows a result. Adjust the CV for every job advert, keep it to one or two pages, and check every line. Never include anything untrue.

1.CV, Resume, Cover Letter and Application Form: What Is the Difference?

These words are often mixed up, so it helps to define them. A CV (short for curriculum vitae, Latin for "course of life") is a short written summary of your education, work experience, skills and achievements. A resume is the same kind of document. In some countries "CV" is the normal word for any job application document, while in others "resume" is the normal word and "CV" means a longer document for academic or research jobs. The safest approach is to read the employer's instructions and follow their word and their length limits.

DocumentWhat it isWhen you use it
CV / resumeA structured summary of your background and achievementsAlmost every job application
Cover letterA short letter that explains why you want this job and why you suit itTogether with your CV, when the employer asks for one or allows one
Application formA form created by the employer, with fixed questionsWhen the employer wants every candidate to answer the same questions
Personal statementA longer piece of writing about your motivation and goalsUniversity or scholarship applications

The CV gives the facts. The cover letter explains the story behind them. Because the two work as a pair, they should agree with each other: the same dates, the same job titles and the same skills.

2.What Employers Look For

Employers read CVs to answer a few practical questions. Can this person do the job? Is there evidence for it? Will they be reliable? Is the document easy and quick to read? Many recruiters scan a CV rapidly before deciding whether to read further, so your most useful information should be easy to find near the top of the first page.

  • Relevance: does your experience match what the advert asks for?
  • Evidence: do you show results, not only duties?
  • Clarity: is the layout simple, and are the sentences short and direct?
  • Accuracy: are the spelling, dates and facts correct and consistent?
  • Honesty: can everything on the page be checked and proved?

3.Before You Write: Build Your Evidence Bank

Many people open a blank page and immediately get stuck. A better way is to collect your material first. Take a sheet of paper, or open a notes file, and write down everything under these headings: jobs, part-time work and internships; school, college or university projects; volunteering and community activities; clubs, sports and leadership roles; courses, certificates and training; languages and computer skills; and awards or praise you received.

Then turn each item into evidence using a simple three-part method. Write the challenge (what needed to be done), your action (what you personally did), and the result (what changed because of it). Some people call this the CAR method. A related method used in interviews is STAR: situation, task, action, result. Both lead you to the same place: a fact with a result attached to it.

Example

Challenge: the school library records were kept on paper and books were often lost. Action: I created a simple spreadsheet and trained three classmates to use it. Result: the library found 40 missing books in one term. Later, this becomes one strong CV line: "Created a spreadsheet record for the school library and trained three volunteers, helping to locate 40 missing books in one term."

Do not worry about length at this stage. Your evidence bank will be longer than your CV, and that is useful: it lets you choose the best evidence for each job, instead of using the same material every time.

4.Choosing a Format

There are three common ways to organise a CV. The right choice depends on your experience and the job.

FormatHow it is organisedBest for
Reverse-chronologicalMost recent job or study first, then going backwards in timeMost applicants, especially with a steady history
Skills-based (functional)Grouped by skills, with a short work history belowCareer changers, or people with gaps in their history
CombinedA skills summary first, then a reverse-chronological historyPeople with strong skills and clear experience

The reverse-chronological format is the most widely used because the reader can see your latest and most relevant experience at once. Some employers and software systems also find it easier to read. If you are a student or recent graduate, put your education first and add projects, volunteering and part-time work. When you have several years of experience, move your education below your work history.

5.The Sections of a CV, One by One

Contact details

Include your full name, a phone number, a professional email address and, if you wish, your town or city and a link to a professional profile. Use an email address based on your name, such as firstname.lastname, and avoid nicknames or jokes. Do not write your passwords, bank details or identity card number. A full street address is usually unnecessary and, for your own safety, many applicants give only the town or city.

Personal statement (profile)

This is a short summary of two to four lines at the top of the page. It should say who you are, what your strongest skills are, and what kind of job you want. It should also be written for the job, not copied from an online sample.

Weak profileStrong profile
Hard-working person looking for a good job in a good company where I can learn and grow.Organised business graduate with experience in customer service and record keeping. Skilled in spreadsheets and scheduling. Seeking an office assistant role where accurate work and clear communication are valued.

The weak profile could be written by anybody and applied to any job. The strong profile gives the reader specific facts and shows the exact job the candidate wants.

Work experience

For each job, give the job title, the employer, the place (town or city) and the dates (month and year). Underneath, write three to five bullet points, with the most important or most relevant point first. Older and less relevant jobs can have fewer points. If you have no formal jobs yet, use the heading "Experience" and include internships, volunteering and projects.

Education and training

List your qualification, the institution, and the dates or year of completion. Add relevant subjects, a final project, or an award if it supports your application. Include short courses and professional certificates in a separate "Training" or "Certificates" section, so they are easy to find.

Skills

Hard skills are specific abilities that can be taught and tested, such as typing speed, a computer program, a language or a technical method. Soft skills are personal qualities such as teamwork, communication and time management. Hard skills can be listed simply. For soft skills, do not just write the word "teamwork". Prove it through a bullet point elsewhere in the CV, such as leading a group project.

Languages and extra sections

Languages can be described honestly with simple levels, such as native, fluent, intermediate or basic. Add other sections only if they help the application, for example volunteering, publications, awards or professional memberships.

Referees

A referee is a person who can speak about your work or character, such as a former manager or a teacher. List two, with their name, role and contact details, or write "References available on request". Always ask for permission before you list anyone, and tell them about the job so they can prepare.

6.Writing Strong Bullet Points

The experience section is where most CVs succeed or fail. Weak CVs list duties ("responsible for..."). Strong CVs show achievements. Use this formula for each point:

Action verb + what you did + result or number

The three-part formula for a strong CV bullet point.

An action verb is a strong verb in the past tense that shows what you did. Choose the verb that best matches the skill you want to show.

Skill to showUseful action verbs
Leadershipled, managed, coordinated, supervised, organised
Communicationpresented, explained, negotiated, wrote, trained
Problem solvingresolved, improved, reduced, redesigned, solved
Technical skillbuilt, programmed, installed, tested, analysed
Organisationscheduled, recorded, planned, prepared, updated
WeakStrong
Responsible for customersServed up to 60 customers a day and resolved complaints politely and quickly
Worked on a school projectLed a team of four students to design a recycling project and presented it to 200 pupils
Good with peopleTrained 12 new volunteers in customer service
Did the accountsRecorded daily sales in a spreadsheet and corrected errors before the weekly report

Where you can, use numbers: how many people, how much money, how much time, how many items. Numbers make an achievement real. If you do not know an exact figure, use an honest estimate and the word "approximately". Never invent a number, because employers can ask about it at the interview or check it with a referee.

7.A Complete Worked Example

The following example is for an imaginary applicant, Jordan Lee, who is applying for an office assistant job. Notice the structure, the verbs and the numbers. This is a model of the shape of a good CV, not text to copy.

Sample CV (shortened)

JORDAN LEE
Phone: 000 000 0000 · Email: jordan.lee@example.com · Town or city only

Profile
Organised business graduate with experience in customer service and record keeping. Skilled in spreadsheets and scheduling. Seeking an office assistant role where accurate work and clear communication are valued.

Experience
Sales Assistant, City Stationery Shop (June 2024 to present)
• Served up to 60 customers a day and resolved complaints politely and quickly.
• Reduced waiting time at the counter from 10 to 6 minutes by reorganising stock and the till area.
• Recorded daily sales in a spreadsheet and checked totals for the weekly report.

Volunteer Administrator, Community Library (January 2023 to May 2024)
• Created a spreadsheet record of 1,200 books and trained three volunteers to use it.
• Helped to locate 40 missing books in one term.

Education
Diploma in Business Studies, City College (2021 to 2023)

Skills
Spreadsheets (formulas, charts) · Scheduling · Typing at 50 words per minute · English (fluent), Spanish (intermediate)

References
Available on request.

This sample is short enough to fit on one page. It starts with a targeted profile, gives results in the bullet points, and keeps the formatting simple and consistent.

8.Language, Tone and Tense

Write in a clear, formal and positive style. Use the past tense for jobs and tasks you have finished and the present tense for your current job. Leave out the word "I" at the start of each line. This keeps each statement short and focused on actions and results, and it is the normal convention in CVs. Prefer short sentences and common words over long or rare ones: the reader wants to understand you quickly.

If English is your second language, you can write an excellent CV without complicated English. Use simple, correct sentences, copy the vocabulary from the job advert where it is true for you, and avoid idioms or fashionable phrases you are not sure about. Choose one spelling style and use it from beginning to end. Then ask a teacher, friend or family member to read the CV, because spelling and grammar mistakes can make a good applicant look careless.

9.Layout, Length and File Type

FeatureRecommended
LengthOne to two pages for most students and early-career applicants
FontA plain, readable font at about 10 to 12 points
Margins and spacingEven margins, clear headings and space between sections
StyleThe same date style, the same tense and the same bullet style from top to bottom
Colours and graphicsFew or none; use bold for headings and job titles
Photograph and personal detailsCustoms differ by country and employer, so follow the advert and local practice
File typePDF unless the employer asks for another type
File nameA clear name such as Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf

A PDF keeps your layout the same on every phone and computer, while a word-processor file can look different when it is opened on another device. After you save the file, open it and read it again as the employer will see it.

10.Working with Software: Applicant Tracking Systems

Many employers use an applicant tracking system (ATS): software that receives applications, stores them and helps recruiters search by keyword. Not every system works in the same way, but a few habits help both the software and the human reader.

  • Use standard headings such as "Experience", "Education" and "Skills".
  • Keep the key information in normal text, not inside pictures, text boxes or complicated tables.
  • Use the same words as the job advert for skills you really have, for example "customer service" rather than a different phrase with the same meaning.
  • Follow the file type that the employer asks for.

Do not copy a long list of keywords that you cannot support. A CV filled with words that are not true for you may pass a search, but it will fail at the interview.

11.Tailoring Your CV to Each Job

Tailoring means adjusting your CV so that it fits one specific job. It takes ten to twenty minutes and is often the most valuable step in the whole process. Follow this method:

  1. Read the job advert carefully and underline the skills, duties and qualities it mentions.
  2. Choose the items in your evidence bank that best match them.
  3. Rewrite your profile so that its first line fits the job.
  4. Move the most relevant bullet points to the top of each job.
  5. Use the advert's own wording, but only for things that are true for you.
Real-World Example

Two people apply for the same office assistant job. One sends the same general CV to every employer. The other reads the advert, sees that it asks for "scheduling" and "using spreadsheets", and rewrites two bullet points to show exactly that experience. The second CV is far easier for the employer to match to the job, and it takes the candidate only a short time to adjust.

12.Special Situations

First job. Lead with education, then projects, volunteering, part-time work and skills. Employers know that a first-time applicant has little paid experience, so they look for responsibility, learning and reliability.

Changing career. Use a skills-based or combined format. Show how your existing skills, such as communication, planning and problem solving, apply to the new field, and mention any training you have done for it.

Gaps in your history. Be honest and brief. A short note such as "career break to study" or "caring for a family member" is better than hiding the dates. Then show any skills or courses you gained in that time.

Many short jobs. Group similar short roles under one heading, for example "Temporary and seasonal work, 2022 to 2024", and highlight the skills that they have in common.

Applying abroad. Customs for photographs, personal details, length and layout differ from one country and employer to another. Read the advert, look at official career advice for that country, and follow what the employer asks.

13.Honesty and Privacy

A CV must be true. Inventing a qualification, extending a date or claiming a skill you do not have can lead to losing an offer, or even a job you already hold, when it is discovered. You can present facts in the best honest light, but you cannot change them.

Protect your privacy too. Share only the contact details that an employer needs, never your passwords, bank information or identity numbers, and be careful about posting your full CV on public websites. Check that the public profiles you list, such as a professional networking page, look appropriate to an employer.

14.Ten Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

MistakeFix
Sending the same CV to every employerTailor the profile and top bullet points for each job
Listing duties instead of achievementsUse action verb + task + result
Spelling or grammar mistakesRead it slowly, use a spell-checker and ask someone else to check
An unprofessional email addressCreate one based on your name
Inconsistent dates, fonts or bulletsChoose one style and apply it everywhere
Too long or crowdedKeep to one or two pages and cut the least relevant items
Vague words such as "hard-working" with no proofShow the quality through a specific example
Listing referees without askingAsk permission and tell them about the job
False or exaggerated claimsInclude only facts that you can prove
A confusing file name or file typeUse a clear PDF name unless the employer asks for something else

15.Your Final Checklist

Before you send your CV, read it once more from the top, and tick off each of these:

  • Is it written for this job, with the most relevant information first?
  • Does every bullet point begin with an action verb and show a result where possible?
  • Are all dates, job titles and facts correct, and the same as in your cover letter?
  • Are spelling, grammar, tense and formatting consistent?
  • Is it one or two pages, in a plain readable font?
  • Are your contact details correct, with a professional email address?
  • Did you ask your referees for permission?
  • Is the file saved in the right type, with a clear file name?

A Closing Thought

A CV is not a list of everything you have done. It is an argument that you are the right person for one particular job, supported by evidence. Collect your evidence, choose a clear format, turn your experience into action-verb statements with results, tailor the document for each advert, and check every line before you send it. If you do this, your CV will do its one job: to earn you the interview. The next step is to prepare for that interview, which is the subject of another article in this series.
