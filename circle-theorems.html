Circle Theorems

Every circle theorem you need: the angle in a semicircle, centre and circumference angles, cyclic quadrilaterals, tangents, chords and the alternate segment theorem, with proofs, reasons to write, and tools to check your work.

A circle looks simple, but it hides a set of powerful angle rules. They let you find unknown angles without a protractor, and they let you prove that a result must be true. Once you recognise the shape a diagram is hinting at (a diameter, four points on a circle, a tangent touching a chord) the right theorem almost announces itself. This guide covers every circle theorem you need, shows why each one is true, gives the exact words to write as a reason, and shows how to combine them in multi-step problems.

1.Parts of a Circle

You must be sure of the vocabulary first. The centre is the fixed middle point and the circumference is the distance around (also the name for the curve itself). A radius joins the centre to any point on the circle. A chord is a straight line joining two points on the circle, and a diameter is a chord through the centre, equal to two radii. An arc is part of the circumference. A sector is the region between two radii and an arc. A segment is the region between a chord and an arc. A tangent is a straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point.

TermMeaning
RadiusCentre to circumference
ChordLine joining two points on the circle
DiameterChord through the centre (2 × radius)
ArcPart of the circumference
SectorRegion between two radii and an arc
SegmentRegion between a chord and an arc
TangentLine touching the circle at one point

A fact that powers many proofs: every radius of a circle has the same length. So any triangle made from two radii and a chord is isosceles, and its two base angles are equal. For example, if the angle at the centre between two radii OA and OB is 100°, then the other two angles of triangle OAB are (180° − 100°) ÷ 2 = 40° each. Look for these hidden isosceles triangles in every diagram.

The theorems below use the idea of an angle subtended by an arc or a chord: the angle formed by lines from a point to the two ends of the arc.

2.Theorem 1: The Angle in a Semicircle

If AB is a diameter and C is any other point on the circle, then angle ACB is exactly 90°. This is often called Thales’ theorem, after an ancient Greek mathematician. Wherever C sits on the circle, the angle does not change.

Figure 1. Three positions of C on the circle. In each case angle ACB is 90°.
Example 1 · Angle in a semicircle

AB is a diameter and C is on the circle. Angle BAC = 35°. Find angle ACB and angle ABC.

Angle ACB = 90° (angle in a semicircle). Angle ABC = 180° − 90° − 35° = 55° (angles in a triangle add up to 180°).

Why it is true. Join C to the centre O. Triangles OAC and OBC are isosceles (OA = OC = OB), so if angle OAC = a then angle OCA = a, and if angle OBC = b then angle OCB = b. The angles of triangle ABC are a, b and (a + b), and they add up to 180°. So 2(a + b) = 180°, so a + b = 90°, which is angle ACB.

“Angle ACB = 90° because it is on the circle”

The theorem needs a diameter.

The fix: the 90° only appears when the side AB passes through the centre. A triangle whose three corners are on a circle, but whose sides are ordinary chords, does not have a right angle. Before you use the theorem, check the diagram or the question for the words “diameter” or a line through the centre O.

3.Theorem 2: Angle at the Centre = 2 × Angle at the Circumference

For an arc AB, the angle subtended at the centre is twice the angle subtended at any point P on the remaining circumference (the same arc, both angles standing on AB).

Figure 2. The gold angle at the centre (70°) is twice the red angle at the circumference (35°). They pulse in turn.
Example 2 · Both directions

(a) An arc subtends 76° at the circumference. The angle at the centre is 2 × 76° = 152°.

(b) An arc subtends 130° at the centre. The angle at the circumference is 130° ÷ 2 = 65°.

(c) The angle at the centre can be reflex. If the angle at the circumference is 140°, the reflex angle at the centre is 280° (the other side of the chord).

Why it is true. Join P to O and extend the line to the other side of the circle. The triangle OAP is isosceles (OA = OP), so its base angles are equal. Call each a. The exterior angle of this triangle at O is the sum of the two opposite interior angles, so it equals a + a = 2a. In the same way the exterior angle in the triangle OBP is 2b, where b is the base angle there. Then angle AOB = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b) = 2 × angle APB.

Setting the angle at the centre to 180° (when AB is a diameter) gives an angle at the circumference of 90°: Theorem 1 is the special case of Theorem 2.

“The centre angle is half the circumference angle”

The relationship is the other way round.

The fix: the centre is the “big” angle. The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference. A memory trick: the point O at the centre is closer to the chord, so it needs a wider angle to see the same arc. If you know the circumference angle, double it; if you know the centre angle, halve it.

4.Theorem 3: Angles in the Same Segment

Angles at the circumference that stand on the same arc (or the same chord) and lie in the same segment are equal. Each is half of the same centre angle, so they must all be equal.

Figure 3. Points C, D and E are on the same arc and all see the chord AB at the same angle of 40°.
Example 3 · Same segment

C and D are on the same side of chord AB. Angle ACB = 48°. Then angle ADB = 48° (angles in the same segment). The angle at the centre, AOB, is 2 × 48° = 96°.

5.Theorem 4: Cyclic Quadrilaterals

A cyclic quadrilateral has all four corners on a circle. Its opposite angles add up to 180°. The second fact that comes with it: an exterior angle (found by extending one side) equals the interior opposite angle.

Figure 4. Opposite angles A and C (and then B and D) add up to 180°.

Why it is true. The two opposite angles at A and C each stand on one of the two arcs between B and D. The angles at the centre for those arcs are 2A and 2C, and they make a full turn: 2A + 2C = 360°. So A + C = 180°.

Example 4 · Cyclic quadrilateral

In cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, angle A = 105° and angle B = 80°. Find C and D.

Angle C = 180° − 105° = 75° (opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral). Angle D = 180° − 80° = 100°.

Example 5 · Exterior angle

ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral. The side AB is extended to E, and angle CBE = 85°. Then the interior opposite angle, angle ADC, is also 85°. Check: angle ABC = 180° − 85° = 95° (angles on a straight line), and 95° + 85° = 180° ✓ (opposite angles).

“Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral are equal”

This is true for a parallelogram, not for a cyclic quadrilateral.

The fix: in a cyclic quadrilateral, opposite angles are supplementary: they add up to 180°. They are equal only in the special case of a rectangle (or square), where each is 90°. In Figure 4 the angles are 75°, 95°, 105° and 85°, and no opposite pair is equal.

6.Theorem 5: Tangents

A tangent touches a circle at one point, the point of contact. Two facts matter:

  1. A tangent is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of contact.
  2. Two tangents drawn from the same external point are equal in length.
Figure 5. Both tangents from P are the same length, and each makes 90° with its radius. The dashes flow along the tangents.
Example 6 · Tangent and radius

A tangent from P touches a circle of centre O at T. OT = 5 cm and PT = 12 cm. Find OP.

Angle OTP = 90°, so triangle OTP is right-angled at T. OP² = 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169, so OP = 13 cm.

Example 7 · The kite

Tangents from P touch the circle at T and T′, and the angle TOT′ at the centre is 110°. Find angle TPT′.

The quadrilateral OTPT′ has two right angles (at T and T′), because each tangent is perpendicular to its radius. Its angles add up to 360°, so angle TPT′ = 360° − 90° − 90° − 110° = 70°. This quadrilateral is a kite, because OT = OT′ and PT = PT′.

7.Theorem 6: The Alternate Segment Theorem

The angle between a tangent and a chord drawn from the point of contact equals the angle in the alternate segment. The alternate segment is the segment on the opposite side of the chord from that angle.

Figure 6. The gold angle between the tangent and chord TA equals the red angle at C in the alternate segment.
Example 8 · Alternate segment

A tangent at T makes an angle of 58° with the chord TA. C is on the circle in the alternate segment. Then angle TCA = 58°.

If CT = CA, triangle TCA is isosceles. The angles at T and A are equal, and each is (180° − 58°) ÷ 2 = 61°.

“The alternate segment angle is on the same side as the tangent-chord angle”

“Alternate” means the other side.

The fix: the chord splits the circle into two segments. The tangent-chord angle opens towards one of them. The equal angle is in the other segment, the “alternate” one, and its corner must be on the circumference there. A good way to check: the two equal angles must be on opposite sides of the chord.

8.Chord Properties

  • The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord (cuts it into two equal halves). The converse is true too: a line from the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to it.
  • Equal chords are the same distance from the centre.

These facts create right-angled triangles (radius = hypotenuse, half the chord, distance from the centre), so Pythagoras finds chord lengths.

Example 9 · Length of a chord

A circle has radius 13 cm. A chord is 5 cm from the centre. Find its length.

Half the chord = √(13² − 5²) = √(169 − 25) = √144 = 12 cm. The chord is 2 × 12 = 24 cm.

Example 10 · Distance from the centre

A circle has radius 10 cm and a chord of length 16 cm. How far is the chord from the centre?

Half the chord is 8 cm. The distance is √(10² − 8²) = √(100 − 64) = √36 = 6 cm.

9.Combining Theorems: A Method

Real problems mix several theorems. This approach works every time:

  1. Scan the diagram for clues: a diameter (90°), a tangent (90° with a radius, alternate segment), four points on a circle (cyclic quadrilateral), two radii (isosceles triangle), an angle at the centre (double or half).
  2. Mark everything you can on the diagram, one angle at a time.
  3. Use ordinary angle facts too: angles in a triangle add up to 180°, angles on a straight line to 180°, angles at a point to 360°.
  4. Write each step with a reason. A reason is the name of the theorem in words, as in the table below.
FactReason to write
Angle subtended by a diameter is 90°Angle in a semicircle
Centre angle = 2 × circumference angleAngle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference
Equal angles on the same arcAngles in the same segment are equal
Opposite angles sum to 180°Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral
Tangent meets radius at 90°Tangent is perpendicular to the radius
Equal tangent lengthsTangents from an external point are equal
Tangent-chord angle equals angle at CAlternate segment theorem
Base angles of a triangle with two radiiTriangle OAB is isosceles (OA = OB, radii)
Perpendicular from centre cuts the chord in twoPerpendicular from the centre bisects a chord
Example 11 · Combining theorems

AB is a diameter of a circle. C and D are on the circle so that ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral. Angle BAC = 25° and angle ADC = 115°. Find (a) angle ACB, (b) angle ABC, (c) angle ADB, (d) angle BDC.

(a) Angle ACB = 90° (angle in a semicircle, AB is a diameter).

(b) Angle ABC = 180° − 90° − 25° = 65° (angles in a triangle).

(c) Angle ADB = 90° (angle in a semicircle).

(d) Angle BDC = angle ADC − angle ADB = 115° − 90° = 25°. This equals angle BAC, as it should: both stand on chord BC in the same segment ✓.

Check: opposite angles ABC and ADC should add to 180°: 65° + 115° = 180° ✓.

Example 12 · Centre, isosceles and cyclic

O is the centre. Angle ACB = 54° at the circumference. Find angle AOB and angle OAB.

Angle AOB = 2 × 54° = 108° (angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference). Triangle OAB is isosceles (OA = OB, radii), so angle OAB = (180° − 108°) ÷ 2 = 36°.

10.Circle Theorems in Real Life

  • Shooting angle in football. Every point on one arc through the two goalposts sees the goal at the same angle (angles in the same segment). Moving along the arc does not change how wide the goal looks.
  • Making right angles. A carpenter or surveyor can mark a right angle with a rope and pegs, because the angle in a semicircle is 90°: two pegs at the ends of a diameter and any third point on the circle form a right angle.
  • Wheels and gears. A tangent line shows the direction an object leaves a circular path, such as a stone released from a sling. Tangent lengths and radii are used in belt and pulley design.
  • Architecture. Arched windows and circular ceilings use circle geometry for strength and a pleasing shape.

11.Common Mistakes

  • Using the semicircle theorem when AB is not a diameter, or using it without checking for a line through the centre.
  • Halving when you should double (and the other way): the centre angle is the larger one.
  • Treating opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral as equal instead of supplementary (180°).
  • Missing the hidden isosceles triangle made by two radii.
  • Using the alternate segment theorem away from the point of contact, or choosing the angle on the wrong side of the chord.
  • Not writing a reason. “Angle ACB = 90°” earns less than “angle ACB = 90° (angle in a semicircle)”.
  • Saying a quadrilateral is cyclic without checking. If opposite angles do not sum to 180°, it is not cyclic: for example, angles of 74° and 96° at opposite corners add up to 170°.

12.Try It Yourself

  1. An arc subtends 100° at the centre. What angle does it subtend at the circumference?
  2. In a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, angle A = 95°. Find angle C.
  3. A tangent from P touches a circle at T. The radius is 9 cm and PT = 12 cm. Find OP.
Show answers

1. 100° ÷ 2 = 50° (angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference).

2. 180° − 95° = 85° (opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral).

3. OP² = 9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225, so OP = 15 cm (the tangent is perpendicular to the radius).

13.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] AB is a diameter of a circle and C is on the circle. Angle CAB = 28°. Find angle ABC, giving a reason.
  2. [3 marks] O is the centre of a circle. A, B and C are on the circle. Angle ACB = 54°. Find (a) angle AOB and (b) angle OAB, giving reasons.
  3. [3 marks] PQRS is a cyclic quadrilateral. Angle PQR = 88° and angle QRS = 102°. Find angle QPS and angle PSR.
  4. [4 marks] A tangent from P touches a circle with centre O at T. OT = 8 cm and PT = 15 cm. Find (a) OP and (b) angle TOP to 1 d.p.
  5. [3 marks] A tangent at T makes an angle of 72° with the chord TA. C is a point on the circle in the alternate segment. (a) Write down angle TCA with a reason. (b) CT = CA. Find angle CTA.
  6. [4 marks] A chord of length 24 cm is 5 cm from the centre of a circle. Find the radius.
  7. [5 marks] AB is a diameter. C and D are on the circle with ABCD a cyclic quadrilateral. Angle BAC = 25° and angle ADC = 115°. Find (a) angle ACB, (b) angle ABC, (c) angle BDC, giving reasons.
Show answers

1. Angle ACB = 90° (angle in a semicircle). Angle ABC = 180° − 90° − 28° = 62° (angles in a triangle).

2. (a) Angle AOB = 2 × 54° = 108° (angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference). (b) OA = OB (radii), so triangle OAB is isosceles: angle OAB = (180° − 108°) ÷ 2 = 36°.

3. Angle QPS is opposite angle QRS: 180° − 102° = 78°. Angle PSR is opposite angle PQR: 180° − 88° = 92° (opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral).

4. (a) Angle OTP = 90°, so OP² = 8² + 15² = 289 and OP = 17 cm. (b) cos(TOP) = 8 ÷ 17 = 0.4706, so angle TOP = 61.9°.

5. (a) Angle TCA = 72° (alternate segment theorem). (b) Triangle TCA is isosceles with CT = CA, so angle CTA = (180° − 72°) ÷ 2 = 54°.

6. The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, so half the chord is 12 cm. Radius = √(12² + 5²) = √169 = 13 cm.

7. (a) Angle ACB = 90° (angle in a semicircle). (b) Angle ABC = 180° − 90° − 25° = 65° (angles in a triangle). (c) Angle ADB = 90° (angle in a semicircle), so angle BDC = 115° − 90° = 25°. (Check: this equals angle BAC, angles in the same segment.)

14.Key Points

  • Angle in a semicircle = 90° (the side must be a diameter).
  • Angle at the centre = 2 × angle at the circumference on the same arc.
  • Angles in the same segment are equal.
  • Cyclic quadrilateral: opposite angles add to 180°, and an exterior angle equals the interior opposite angle.
  • Tangent ⟂ radius; two tangents from a point are equal in length; the figure OTPT′ is a kite.
  • Alternate segment theorem: tangent-chord angle = angle in the alternate segment.
  • The perpendicular from the centre bisects a chord. Two radii make an isosceles triangle.
  • Always write the reason for each step.

15.For Teachers and Parents

Start with measuring. Give learners a circle with a chord and three points on the major arc, and ask them to measure the angles with a protractor. They find the same angle each time, and the central angle is twice as large. The Circle Theorem Explorer on this page does the same thing with a screen: it computes the angles from the coordinates rather than from the theorem. After measuring, teach one proof (the centre angle or the semicircle) so that learners see the rules are consequences, not magic. Insist on written reasons from day one and let learners build a “theorem toolkit” card with a small diagram for each fact. For multi-step problems, ask learners to colour each angle once it has been found, so that they can see the diagram fill in.
