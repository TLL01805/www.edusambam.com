Narrative Writing: Storytelling, Plot and Character

A comprehensive EDUSAMBAM unit on constructing purposeful narratives through setting, characterisation, conflict, viewpoint, sequence, tension, dialogue, pacing, climax, resolution and precise language.

Unit purpose: Narrative writing is not simply a list of events. It is the deliberate shaping of connected events, characters, setting, viewpoint, language and structure so that a reader experiences a meaningful story. Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 Paper 2 Section B asks candidates to write one descriptive or narrative composition of about 350–450 words. This unit develops the narrative skills needed to make a story coherent, credible, engaging and controlled.

1. What Narrative Writing Really Means

A narrative tells a story containing a sequence of connected events that may be real or imaginary. The events matter, but effective narrative writing also depends on why those events happen, how characters respond, how tension develops and how the writer controls the reader's experience.

EDUSAMBAM principle

CHARACTER + DESIRE + PROBLEM + CHOICES + CONSEQUENCES = NARRATIVE MOVEMENT

2. Narrative Is More Than “Then This Happened”

A weak story often becomes a timetable: first this happened, then this happened, then something else happened. A stronger narrative makes events causally connected. Each important event changes the situation, reveals something about a character or creates a new problem.

Ask

What changes because this event happened?

3. Start With the Task

Before inventing a plot, read the narrative prompt carefully. Identify the required idea, phrase or situation and decide what role it will play. A title such as “The Unexpected Visitor” could become a comedy, mystery, family story or realistic encounter. The best choice is one that can be developed convincingly within the available word count.

Planning sequence

TASK → CENTRAL IDEA → CHARACTER → PROBLEM → ESCALATION → CLIMAX → ENDING

4. Choose a Manageable Story

In a 350–450 word composition, a small situation can be more effective than an enormous adventure. One journey, one decision, one misunderstanding, one difficult conversation or one unexpected discovery can provide enough material. Avoid plots that require ten characters, several locations and years of history.

Exam wisdom

Depth usually beats breadth. Develop one meaningful situation rather than mentioning many events superficially.

5. Build a Character Who Wants Something

Characters become easier to write when they have a clear desire. The desire may be simple: reach home, tell the truth, win trust, protect someone, apologise, find an object or prove something. Once the reader understands what matters to the character, obstacles have meaning.

Character engine

WANT → OBSTACLE → CHOICE → CONSEQUENCE

6. Give the Character a Reason to Act

A believable character does not move randomly through the plot. Their decisions should arise from personality, circumstances, relationships or information. If a normally cautious student suddenly enters a dangerous building, the story should give the reader a reason that makes the decision credible.

Ask: What does the character know? What do they fear? What do they misunderstand? What do they need?

7. Characterisation Through Action

One of the strongest ways to reveal character is through behaviour. Instead of writing “Aisha was brave”, show her stepping forward when others hesitate, speaking calmly under pressure or returning for someone she could have left behind.

Show, then interpret

TRAIT → BEHAVIOUR → READER INFERENCE

8. Characterisation Through Speech

Dialogue can reveal confidence, fear, humour, hesitation, relationships and conflict. It should have a purpose. A conversation in which characters merely exchange information can slow a narrative. Strong dialogue changes something: a secret is revealed, a decision is made, tension rises or a relationship shifts.

Dialogue test

If the conversation were removed, would the story lose information, tension or characterisation? If not, shorten it.

9. Use Setting as Part of the Story

Setting is not merely background. A narrow stairwell can create confinement; an empty sports ground can create isolation; a crowded station can make a character feel lost. Choose setting details that interact with the character's situation.

EDUSAMBAM principle

SETTING SHOULD AFFECT MOOD, ACTION OR MEANING

10. Establish the Scenario Early

The opening should orient the reader without wasting the first paragraph on unnecessary history. Establish enough information about who, where, when and what matters for the narrative to begin moving.

Compare a long explanation of a character's childhood with an opening that places the character immediately outside a locked room. The second creates an active question that the story can develop.

11. Create a Central Problem

Most effective short narratives have a problem or uncertainty that gives the events direction. The problem does not need to be life-threatening. It may be a misunderstanding, a difficult choice, a lost object, a broken promise, a deadline or a conflict between two people.

Simple plot architecture

STABLE SITUATION → DISRUPTION → RESPONSE → ESCALATION → CLIMAX → CONSEQUENCE

12. Cause and Consequence

Connect events through cause and consequence. If a character lies, that lie should create a later complication. If a character takes a risk, the risk should have a consequence. Causal links make the narrative feel designed rather than assembled.

Test every major event

Because this happened, what becomes possible or impossible next?

13. Raise the Stakes

Tension grows when the consequences of failure become clearer or the available choices become harder. The stakes can be emotional, social, practical or physical. A character who might disappoint a friend may experience intense tension even when nobody is in physical danger.

Make the stakes specific: What exactly could be lost?

14. Build Tension Gradually

Do not make every sentence equally dramatic. Tension works through variation. A calm detail can make a later interruption more powerful. Silence, delay, uncertainty, incomplete information and small warning signs can all prepare the reader for a turning point.

Tension pattern

CALM → SIGN → QUESTION → DELAY → ESCALATION → CLIMAX

15. Foreshadowing

Foreshadowing places a small clue before an important event. The clue should feel natural when first encountered and meaningful when the reader later understands it. A damaged lock, an unanswered message or a character's unusual warning can prepare the reader for what follows.

Avoid making the clue so obvious that the outcome becomes predictable.

16. Viewpoint and Narrative Voice

Choose who tells the story and what that narrator can know. A first-person narrator can reveal personal thoughts directly but cannot automatically know another person's private thoughts. A third-person narrator can offer wider access, but the writer should still control viewpoint rather than jumping between minds without purpose.

Viewpoint check

WHO IS EXPERIENCING THIS MOMENT? WHAT CAN THIS NARRATOR ACTUALLY KNOW?

17. First Person: Strengths and Risks

First person can create immediacy because the reader experiences the event through “I”. It can also make emotion convincing. However, first-person narration can become repetitive if every sentence begins with “I”, and the narrator should not know facts they could not reasonably discover.

18. Third Person: Distance and Flexibility

Third person can provide a wider view of the setting and characters. It can also remain close to one character's experience. The important principle is consistency. Do not move suddenly from one character's private thoughts to another's without a deliberate structural reason.

19. Description Inside Narrative

Description and narrative are not enemies. A story needs enough setting and sensory detail to make the events believable. The key is proportion: description should support the story rather than stopping it for an unrelated catalogue of scenery.

Narrative balance

EVENT → DETAIL → CHARACTER RESPONSE → EVENT

20. Pacing: Slow Down Important Moments

Not every minute of the story deserves the same amount of space. Summarise routine travel or repetitive action. Slow down at the moment of discovery, decision, confrontation or danger. This gives the climax enough weight.

Pacing principle

COMPRESS THE ORDINARY; EXPAND THE SIGNIFICANT.

21. Sentence Rhythm in Narrative

Sentence length can influence pace. Longer sentences may carry continuous movement or reflection. Shorter sentences can create shock, urgency or finality. Variety is more effective than using short sentences everywhere to imitate excitement.

Use deliberately

LONGER FLOW → SHORTER EMPHASIS → CONTROLLED PAUSE

22. Dialogue: Keep It Natural and Useful

Real conversation contains repetitions and irrelevant comments; examination dialogue usually needs tighter selection. Give each speaker a distinct purpose. Use speech tags and actions where necessary, but do not overload every line with “he said” and “she said”.

Dialogue can also reveal what characters avoid saying. Hesitation and interruption can be meaningful.

23. Showing Emotion Without Over-Explaining

Instead of repeatedly naming emotions—“he was very frightened”—use physical response, thought, action and speech. A character may check the door twice, struggle to steady a hand or suddenly become unable to speak. These details allow the reader to infer the emotion.

Emotion method

BODY + THOUGHT + ACTION + SPEECH → READER FEELS THE EMOTION

24. Conflict Between Characters

Conflict becomes interesting when both sides have understandable reasons. A disagreement does not need shouting. Two friends may want different things; a parent may fear a risk that a child sees as an opportunity; one character may know something another does not.

Better conflict

Give characters competing goals, not merely competing volume.

25. Flashback: Useful but Dangerous

A flashback can reveal an earlier event that changes the meaning of the present. Cambridge examiner feedback notes that flashback was sometimes used effectively in successful narratives. But a flashback should have a clear structural purpose and should not confuse the timeline.

Signal the transition through tense, time markers or a clear shift in viewpoint.

26. The Climax

The climax is the point of greatest narrative pressure—the moment when a major question is confronted or a decisive choice is made. It should grow from what came before. A sudden solution that appears from nowhere usually feels unearned.

Climax test

Has the story earned this moment through earlier choices, clues and consequences?

27. Resolution: Finish the Story

A strong ending gives the reader a sense of consequence. It does not need to explain every detail, but it should show what has changed. A character may understand something, repair a relationship, accept a consequence or return to an ordinary world with a new perspective.

Avoid endings that simply say “and then I woke up” unless the dream device has been carefully prepared.

28. Avoid the Forced Twist

Twists can be effective when they grow naturally from the story. A twist introduced only in the final sentence often feels like a trick. If the reader can recognise earlier clues after the reveal, the ending feels more satisfying.

Twist principle

SURPRISE + PREPARATION = CREDIBLE REVELATION

29. Avoid Formulaic “Horror” Narratives

Cambridge's June 2024 examiner report noted that some narratives relied on familiar haunted houses, monsters and violent themes, often becoming formulaic. The report also noted that simpler, realistic narratives could work very well when they conveyed convincing experiences and feelings. Originality does not require extreme danger; it requires purposeful choices.

Choose wisely

Credibility, character and emotional consequence often create stronger stories than exaggerated shock.

30. A Worked Mini-Plot

Prompt: “The message I should not have ignored”

Opening: A student receives a short message from an elderly neighbour asking for help.

Disruption: The student delays replying because of an important school commitment.

Escalation: A second message arrives, but the student assumes it can wait.

Climax: Returning home, the student discovers why the first message mattered.

Resolution: The immediate problem is addressed, but the student recognises the cost of assuming there would always be another chance.

The plot is small, but it contains desire, delay, consequence, emotional change and a clear ending.

31. Improving a Weak Narrative

Weak version

We went to the old house. It was scary. We went inside. We saw many things. Then we heard a noise. We ran outside. It was a cat.

What needs improvement?

The plot has events but little character motivation, limited development, predictable tension and a weak ending. Improve it by giving the protagonist a reason to enter, a meaningful uncertainty, a specific sensory detail, a decision and a consequence.

32. Paragraphs Should Control the Story

Paragraphs should mark meaningful shifts: a new stage of action, a change of focus, a new piece of information, a significant reaction or a movement towards the climax. Random paragraph breaks make a narrative harder to follow.

Paragraph question

What is this paragraph's job in the story?

33. The 350–450 Word Composition Boundary

For the current Cambridge 1123 Paper 2 Section B, candidates write about 350–450 words. Treat this as a planning constraint. A manageable plot with selective detail is safer than an over-complicated story that cannot be finished or edited.

StageApproximate purpose
OpeningEstablish character, setting and narrative question
DevelopmentBuild problem, choices and tension
ClimaxGive the decisive moment enough space
EndingShow consequence and close the narrative

34. A Fast Examination Plan

Use a brief planning grid before writing:

  1. Prompt: What must the story contain?
  2. Character: Who wants what?
  3. Problem: What interrupts the situation?
  4. Escalation: Why does it become harder?
  5. Climax: What decisive moment occurs?
  6. Ending: What changes?

Then choose one viewpoint, one main setting and two or three details that you can develop well.

35. Editing for W1–W5

Cambridge assesses the composition through W1, W2, W3 and W5: articulating experience and imagination; organising and structuring ideas for deliberate effect; using a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context; and making accurate use of spelling, punctuation and grammar. Use the final minutes to check each area.

ObjectiveFinal check
W1Is the story developed and purposeful?
W2Do events connect and build towards an ending?
W3Are vocabulary and sentence choices varied and appropriate?
W5Have I checked tense, spelling, punctuation and grammar?

36. Final Narrative Mastery Checklist

  • I can interpret the narrative task before inventing a plot.
  • I can create a manageable central situation.
  • I can give my main character a clear desire or problem.
  • I can reveal character through action, speech and response.
  • I can connect events through cause and consequence.
  • I can build tension rather than rushing to the climax.
  • I can control viewpoint and tense.
  • I can use description where it strengthens the story.
  • I can use dialogue purposefully.
  • I can give the story a convincing ending.
  • I can edit for W1, W2, W3 and W5.

37. Narrative Language: Precision Without Decoration

Strong narrative language is not the same as constant figurative language. Choose verbs that reveal action, nouns that identify important details and comparisons that genuinely clarify an image. Cambridge examiner feedback noted that figurative language could be effective but was sometimes used to excess. Precision should come before ornament.

Language test

Does this word make the action, image, character or emotion clearer? If not, replace or remove it.

38. Practice-to-Transfer Task

Write

Write a 350–450 word narrative beginning with the sentence:

“I knew immediately that something had changed.”

Plan the character's situation, build a believable problem, develop tension through at least two stages, create a decisive moment and finish with a meaningful consequence. After writing, underline one sentence that establishes setting, one that reveals character, one that builds tension and one that creates the ending.

The purpose of the task is transfer: use the principles of this unit in a new situation rather than copying the worked example.

39. Source Basis and Authenticity

This unit is based primarily on the Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 syllabus for examination in 2027 and 2028, especially Paper 2 Section B Composition. Cambridge defines narrative writing as a story containing a sequence of connected events which may be real or imaginary, and specifies approximately 350–450 words. The unit also draws on Cambridge's published June 2024 Principal Examiner Report for observations about successful narrative structure, characterisation, tension, flashback, dialogue, endings, paragraphing, tense control and the risks of formulaic plots.
