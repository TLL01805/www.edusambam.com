Parts of Speech: The Building Blocks of English

Every sentence you have ever spoken or written is built from just a handful of word types. Learn to recognise all eight, and grammar stops being a mystery.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 10 min read | Grammar

Before a sentence can carry meaning, its words must each play a role — naming, doing, describing, linking. That role is called a part of speech.

English has eight parts of speech. Every single word you use, no matter how long or short, belongs to one of these eight categories depending on the job it is doing in a sentence. Some words can even change category depending on how they are used — which is exactly why understanding the categories, rather than memorising individual words, is what actually makes grammar make sense.

This guide walks through each part of speech in turn, with clear definitions and examples, before testing your understanding with ten practice questions at the end.

1.Nouns

A noun names a person, place, thing, or idea. Nouns are the labels we attach to everything that exists — physical objects, living beings, locations, and even abstract concepts that cannot be touched.

Examples

Person: teacher, Sarah, doctor

Place: school, London, kitchen

Thing: book, laptop, mountain

Idea: honesty, freedom, happiness

Nouns are further divided into common nouns (general: city, dog) and proper nouns (specific, always capitalised: Paris, Rex). There are also countable nouns (books, chairs) and uncountable nouns (water, information) — a distinction that trips up even confident writers.

2.Pronouns

A pronoun replaces a noun so that language does not become repetitive. Without pronouns, we would have to repeat the same name or object again and again in every sentence.

Example

"Maria picked up Maria's bag and Maria left" becomes "Maria picked up her bag and she left."

Common pronouns include: he, she, it, they, we, this, those, who, myself, and someone. Pronouns must agree in number and gender with the noun they replace.

3.Verbs

A verb expresses an action, occurrence, or state of being. Every complete sentence needs at least one verb — it is the engine that makes the sentence move.

Examples

Action: run, write, calculate

Occurrence: become, happen, occur

State of being: is, seem, appear

Verbs change form to show tense (walk, walked, will walk), and can be regular (following a predictable pattern) or irregular (go, went, gone).

4.Adjectives

An adjective describes or modifies a noun or pronoun, adding detail about size, colour, quantity, quality, or other characteristics.

Example

The tall, ancient, weathered castle stood on the hill. (Each bolded word modifies "castle.")

Adjectives typically appear directly before the noun they describe, or after a linking verb such as "is" or "seems" (e.g., "The castle is ancient.").

5.Adverbs

An adverb modifies a verb, an adjective, or even another adverb — usually describing how, when, where, or to what extent something happens. Many (though not all) end in -ly.

Example

She spoke quietly (modifies the verb "spoke"). The exam was extremely difficult (modifies the adjective "difficult").

6.Prepositions

A preposition shows the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and another word in the sentence — usually relating to place, time, or direction.

Examples

The keys are on the table. We will meet after lunch. She walked through the door.

7.Conjunctions

A conjunction joins words, phrases, or clauses together. Without conjunctions, every sentence would have to stand entirely alone.

Examples

Coordinating: and, but, or, so — join equal ideas.

Subordinating: because, although, if — join a main idea to a dependent one.

8.Interjections

An interjection expresses sudden emotion — surprise, joy, pain, or hesitation — and usually stands apart from the rest of the sentence, often followed by an exclamation mark.

Examples

Wow! That's incredible. Ouch! That hurt. Well, I suppose that's true.

Real-World Example

Read this text message: "Wow, that new phone is incredibly expensive, but Sarah still bought it for herself because she loves gadgets." In just one sentence: Wow is an interjection, that and Sarah relate to nouns, is/bought/loves are verbs, new/expensive/incredible are adjectives and adverbs, herself/she are pronouns, and but/because are conjunctions. Every single word, in every sentence you will ever write, falls into one of these eight categories.

Why This Matters

Understanding parts of speech is not an academic exercise — it is the foundation for everything else in grammar. You cannot fix a sentence structure problem, correct a punctuation error, or improve your writing style without first being able to identify what job each word is doing. This is exactly why Parts of Speech is topic one in our Grammar Basics series: everything that follows builds on it.
