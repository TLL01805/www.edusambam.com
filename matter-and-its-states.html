Matter and Its States: Solid, Liquid, and Gas

Why ice is hard, water pours and steam spreads: the particle model, density, latent heat and the gas laws, explained step by step with diagrams and worked examples.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 16 min read | Science | Last updated: October 2026

A block of ice, a glass of water and a cloud of steam look nothing alike, yet all three are made of exactly the same kind of molecule, H₂O. The difference is not what they are made of but how their particles are arranged and how much energy those particles have. This simple idea, called the particle model of matter, explains why ice is hard, water pours, steam fills a room, and why a kettle needs so much energy just to turn boiling water into steam.

1.What Matter Is

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space (has volume). Your desk, the water you drink and the air you breathe are all matter. Light and sound are not matter, because they are forms of energy carried by waves. Matter is made of tiny particles, which may be atoms, molecules (groups of atoms joined together) or ions (charged atoms). These particles are far too small to see with an ordinary microscope, but their behaviour explains most of what we observe.

The three common states of matter are solid, liquid and gas. Which one a substance is in depends on its temperature and the pressure around it.

2.The Particle Model

The particle model (also called the kinetic theory) rests on a few ideas:

  • All matter is made of particles that are constantly moving. "Kinetic" means to do with movement.
  • There are attractive forces between particles. They are strong when particles are close and weak when they are far apart.
  • The higher the temperature, the faster the particles move on average. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles.

In a solid, the attractive forces are strong enough to hold the particles in fixed positions, and they can only vibrate. In a liquid, the particles are still touching, but they have enough energy to slide past each other. In a gas, the particles have so much energy that the forces between them hardly matter; they fly about independently and are very far apart compared with their size.

3.Solids, Liquids and Gases Compared

PropertySolidLiquidGas
ShapeFixedTakes the shape of its containerTakes the shape of its container
VolumeFixedFixedFills the whole container

Particle movementVibrate in placeSlide past each otherMove quickly and freely

Typical densityHighHigh (a little lower than the solid)Very low

Some solids, such as salt and metals, have particles arranged in a regular repeating pattern called a crystal. Others, such as glass, have particles that are fixed but not in a regular pattern; these are called amorphous solids. Glass is not a very slow liquid, as is sometimes claimed; it is an amorphous solid.

A fluid is any substance that can flow, so liquids and gases are both fluids. How easily a liquid flows is called its viscosity: honey has a high viscosity and water a low one.

4.Density

Density is the mass of a substance in each unit of volume. It tells you how tightly the matter is packed.

Formula

ρ = m ÷ V   (density = mass ÷ volume)

In SI units, mass is in kilograms (kg), volume in cubic metres (m³), and density in kg/m³. In the laboratory, g/cm³ is also common: 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.

MaterialApproximate density (kg/m³)
Air (about 20 °C, sea level)1.2
Ice920
Water1000
Aluminium2700
Iron7900
Copper8900–9000

Values are rounded and vary a little with temperature and purity. The very low density of gases is a direct result of their particles being so far apart.

Worked example 1: finding density

A metal block has a mass of 540 g and a volume of 200 cm³. Find its density and identify the likely metal.

Step 1. ρ = m ÷ V = 540 g ÷ 200 cm³.

Step 2. Calculate: ρ = 2.7 g/cm³, which is 2700 kg/m³.

Step 3. Compare with the table: this matches aluminium.

Worked example 2: finding mass from density

A tank holds 0.50 m³ of water. What is the mass of the water? (ρ = 1000 kg/m³)

m = ρ × V = 1000 kg/m³ × 0.50 m³ = 500 kg.

To find the density of an irregular solid in the lab, you measure its mass with a balance and find its volume by displacement: lower it into a measuring cylinder of water and note how much the water level rises.

5.Changes of State

A change of state is a physical change: the substance stays the same, but its particles rearrange. No new substance is formed, and the change can be reversed.

  • Melting: solid → liquid. Energy is absorbed. The temperature at which a pure solid melts is its melting point.
  • Freezing: liquid → solid. Energy is released. For a pure substance the freezing point equals the melting point.
  • Boiling: liquid → gas throughout the liquid, at the boiling point.
  • Evaporation: liquid → gas at the surface, at temperatures below boiling.
  • Condensation: gas → liquid. Energy is released.
  • Sublimation: solid → gas directly, without becoming a liquid. Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) does this at normal air pressure, at about −78.5 °C; it must never be handled with bare hands because it can cause cold burns.
  • Deposition: gas → solid directly. Frost forming on a cold window is water vapour turning straight into ice.
Figure 2. The six changes of state. Moving towards the gas state needs energy to be absorbed; moving towards the solid state releases energy. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)

6.The Heating Curve and Latent Heat

If you heat ice steadily and record the temperature, the graph is a heating curve. It has sloping parts and two flat parts.

Figure 3. Heating curve of water (not to scale). During the flat parts, energy is used to change the state, not to raise the temperature. In reality the boiling section is much longer than the melting section. (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)

During the flat sections, heat energy is still being supplied, but the temperature does not change. The energy is being used to pull the particles apart against the attractive forces between them (when melting and boiling) rather than to make them move faster. This hidden energy is called latent heat ("latent" means hidden). It is released again when the substance freezes or condenses; this is why steam can cause a worse burn than boiling water at the same temperature, because the steam also releases its latent heat as it condenses on skin.

The energy needed to change the state of a mass m is:

Formulae

Q = m × L   (during a change of state; L is the specific latent heat in J/kg)

Q = m × c × ΔT   (while the temperature changes; c is the specific heat capacity in J/kg°C)

Approximate values for water: specific latent heat of fusion (ice) L ≈ 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg; specific latent heat of vaporisation L ≈ 2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg; specific heat capacity c ≈ 4200 J/kg°C.

Worked example 3: melting ice

How much energy melts 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C?

Q = mL = 0.50 kg × 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg = 1.67 × 10⁵ J = 167 kJ.

Worked example 4: melt, then warm

How much energy changes 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C into water at 20 °C?

Step 1. Melt the ice: Q₁ = mL = 0.50 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = 1.67 × 10⁵ J.

Step 2. Warm the water: Q₂ = mcΔT = 0.50 kg × 4200 J/kg°C × 20 °C = 4.2 × 10⁴ J.

Step 3. Total: Q = 1.67 × 10⁵ + 0.42 × 10⁵ = 2.09 × 10⁵ J (about 209 kJ).

Worked example 5: boiling

How much energy turns 0.20 kg of boiling water at 100 °C into steam at 100 °C?

Q = mL = 0.20 kg × 2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg = 4.52 × 10⁵ J = 452 kJ.

Notice how much more energy is needed to boil a kilogram of water than to melt a kilogram of ice. Pulling particles completely apart takes far more energy than letting them slide.

7.Evaporation and Boiling

Both processes turn a liquid into a gas, but they are not the same:

EvaporationBoiling
WhereOnly at the surfaceThroughout the liquid (bubbles form)
TemperatureAt any temperature above freezingOnly at the boiling point
SpeedSlowFast
EnergyTaken from the liquid, which coolsSupplied continuously from the heat source

In any liquid the particles move at different speeds. The fastest particles near the surface can escape into the air. Because the faster particles are the ones leaving, the average energy of the remaining liquid falls, so evaporation causes cooling. This is how sweating cools your body and why wet clothes feel cold. Evaporation is faster if the liquid is warmer, has a larger surface area, there is a draught or wind, and the surrounding air is dry (low humidity).

The boiling point depends on the pressure above the liquid. Water boils at 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure (about 101 kPa). At the top of a very high mountain the air pressure is much lower, so water boils at a lower temperature (roughly 70 °C near the top of Mount Everest), which is why food takes longer to cook there. A pressure cooker does the opposite: it raises the pressure so the water boils above 100 °C and food cooks faster.

8.Gas Pressure and the Gas Laws

Gas pressure is caused by particles hitting the walls of their container. Each tiny collision gives the wall a small push, and billions of them every second add up to a steady force on each unit of area. Pressure is measured in pascals (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m².

Boyle's law. For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. Squeezing the gas into a smaller volume makes the particles hit the walls more often, so the pressure rises.

Figure 4. The same number of gas particles in a smaller volume hit the walls more often, so the pressure is higher (Boyle's law, constant temperature). (Original EDUSAMBAM diagram.)
Worked example 6: Boyle's law

A gas occupies 600 cm³ at a pressure of 100 kPa. It is compressed to 200 cm³ at constant temperature. Find the new pressure.

Step 1. p₁V₁ = p₂V₂, so p₂ = p₁V₁ ÷ V₂.

Step 2. p₂ = (100 kPa × 600 cm³) ÷ 200 cm³ = 300 kPa.

Charles's law. For a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure, volume is directly proportional to the absolute (kelvin) temperature: V₁ ÷ T₁ = V₂ ÷ T₂. The kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, the lowest possible temperature, which is −273.15 °C. To convert, T (K) = θ (°C) + 273 (273.15 for more precision). You must always use kelvin in gas-law calculations.

Worked example 7: Charles's law

A balloon contains 2.0 litres of air at 27 °C. It is warmed to 127 °C at constant pressure. Find its new volume.

Step 1. Convert to kelvin: T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K; T₂ = 127 + 273 = 400 K.

Step 2. V₂ = V₁ × T₂ ÷ T₁ = 2.0 L × 400 ÷ 300.

Step 3. V₂ ≈ 2.7 L.

A third relation says that at constant volume, the pressure of a gas rises in proportion to its kelvin temperature. This is why a sealed can can burst if it is heated, and why a car tyre's pressure rises after a long drive.

9.Brownian Motion and Diffusion

In 1827 the Scottish botanist Robert Brown looked through a microscope at tiny pollen grains in water and saw them jiggling in an irregular way. In 1905 Albert Einstein showed mathematically that this Brownian motion is caused by countless collisions with fast-moving water molecules, which strike the grain unevenly from different sides. Later experiments, notably by Jean Perrin, supported this explanation, and it became strong evidence that particles exist. You can see a similar effect with smoke particles in air under a microscope.

Diffusion is the spreading of particles from a region of high concentration to a region of lower concentration, caused by their random motion. A smell of cooking spreads across a house this way. Diffusion is fastest in gases, slower in liquids and extremely slow in solids. At the same temperature, lighter gas molecules move faster on average and diffuse faster than heavier ones. Higher temperature also speeds diffusion.

10.Why Ice Floats

For most substances the solid is denser than the liquid. Water is unusual. As water cools it contracts until about 4 °C, where it is densest. Below that it expands slightly as it approaches freezing, and when it becomes ice the molecules lock into an open, regular arrangement that holds them slightly further apart than in liquid water. Ice is therefore about 8–9% less dense than liquid water, so it floats. This matters in nature: in cold weather a lake freezes from the top, and the ice layer insulates the water beneath, which often stays liquid so that fish and other life can survive. It also explains why a bottle of water left in a freezer can crack: the freezing water expands.

11.Beyond the Three States

  • Plasma: when a gas is heated to very high temperature or exposed to strong electric fields, atoms lose electrons and form a mixture of ions and free electrons. Plasma conducts electricity and responds to magnetic fields. Stars, lightning and neon signs involve plasma. Much of the visible matter in the universe is plasma.
  • Bose–Einstein condensate: a state formed by some atoms cooled to extremely close to absolute zero. It was first made in laboratories in 1995 and is studied in advanced research.

These states are not usually needed at O-Level, but they show that the three everyday states are only part of the picture.

12.Common Mistakes to Avoid

Watch out
  • "The particles themselves melt, expand or get hot." Particles do not change size; their spacing and speed change.
  • "Temperature rises during melting." It stays constant while a pure substance changes state, even though energy is supplied.
  • Using °C in gas-law problems. Convert to kelvin first.
  • Mixing up evaporation and boiling. Evaporation happens only at the surface and at any temperature; boiling happens throughout at the boiling point.
  • Thinking steam is visible. Pure steam (water vapour) is invisible; the white cloud above a kettle is tiny liquid droplets formed as the vapour condenses.
  • Forgetting unit conversions in density. 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³ and 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³.
  • Believing gas particles are "light and empty". Gas particles have mass; the gas is mostly empty space between particles.

13.Check Your Understanding

Try each question before opening the answer.

1. Why can a gas be compressed but a solid cannot?

Gas particles are very far apart with mostly empty space between them, so they can be pushed closer. Solid particles are already touching, so there is almost no room to squeeze them.

2. A 250 g block has a volume of 100 cm³. What is its density in kg/m³?

ρ = 250 g ÷ 100 cm³ = 2.5 g/cm³ = 2500 kg/m³.

3. Why does the temperature stay at 100 °C while water boils?

The energy supplied is used to separate the particles (latent heat), not to increase their average kinetic energy, so the temperature does not rise.

4. Explain why sweating cools you down.

The fastest-moving water molecules escape from the skin surface as the sweat evaporates. This removes energy, so the remaining liquid and the skin become cooler.

5. A gas at 200 kPa and volume 3.0 L is expanded to 6.0 L at constant temperature. What is the new pressure?

p₂ = 200 × 3.0 ÷ 6.0 = 100 kPa.

6. Convert 25 °C to kelvin.

T = 25 + 273 = 298 K (298.15 K using 273.15).

7. How much energy is needed to melt 2.0 kg of ice at 0 °C? (L = 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg)

Q = mL = 2.0 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = 6.68 × 10⁵ J.

8. Why does ice float on water?

Ice is less dense than liquid water because its molecules are held in an open, regular arrangement that takes up more space.

Summary

  • All matter is made of moving particles. Solids vibrate in fixed positions, liquids slide, gases rush about freely.
  • Density ρ = m ÷ V. Gases have the lowest density because their particles are far apart.
  • Changes of state are physical changes that absorb or release latent heat; temperature stays constant during them.
  • Gas pressure comes from collisions with the walls. Boyle: pV is constant. Charles: V ÷ T is constant (kelvin).
  • Evaporation cools; boiling depends on pressure; ice floats because water expands on freezing.
For teachers and parents

Useful safe activities: use a syringe with the nozzle sealed to feel how a gas compresses but a liquid does not; heat ice with a thermometer and record temperature every minute to draw a real heating curve (the plateau is a striking result); measure density of regular blocks by weighing and measuring, and of stones by displacement; compare evaporation of the same amount of water on a plate in a warm place, a cool place and in a breeze. Hot water, steam and dry ice need adult supervision. All numerical values are rounded, so please check that they match the data booklet used by your examination board.
