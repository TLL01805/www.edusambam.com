Multiply & Divide Fractions: Cross, Flip, and Fly

Multiplying fractions is easier than adding them, because no common denominator is needed. Dividing takes one clever flip. Learn why both work, with diagrams and real problems.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|12 min read|Last updated: October 2026|Mathematics

Good news first: multiplying fractions is more straightforward than adding them. There is no common denominator and no matching pieces: you simply multiply straight across. Dividing takes one extra step, and this guide shows exactly why that step works, not just what it is. By the end you will be able to multiply and divide fractions, whole numbers and mixed numbers, and use them to solve real problems with cloth, land, money and time.

A quick note before starting: unlike addition, multiplying and dividing fractions never requires a common denominator. That single fact trips up many students who apply the rules from Fraction Fix-It out of habit, so the first job is to unlearn that reflex.

Did You Know?

Multiplying two fractions smaller than 1 always makes the answer smaller, not bigger. This is the opposite of what multiplication usually does with whole numbers. 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, which is smaller than either fraction you started with.

1.Multiplying Fractions: Straight Across

To multiply two fractions, multiply the numerators together, then multiply the denominators together. That is the entire rule, and no common denominator is required.

Example 1

2/3 × 3/4 = (2 × 3) / (3 × 4) = 6/12, which simplifies to 1/2.

2/3 × 3/4: “first I need a common denominator…”

This is the most common instinct, carried over from adding fractions. It is unnecessary here and only adds extra work.

The fix: multiplication never needs matching denominators. Just multiply straight across: numerator × numerator, denominator × denominator. 2/3 × 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2, done in one step.

2.What Multiplying Fractions Actually Means

“2/3 × 3/4” means “two-thirds of three-quarters”. Multiplying by a fraction smaller than 1 finds a part of a part, which is why the answer ends up smaller than either fraction you began with. The word “of” in a fraction problem almost always means multiply.

The overlap, where the two fractions meet, is the answer. It pulses gently. The area model shows why you multiply numerators and denominators.
Example 2: A Garden Bed

A garden bed is 3/4 planted with flowers. Of that planted section, 2/3 are roses. The roses cover 2/3 × 3/4 = 1/2 of the whole garden bed.

Example 3: Fraction of an Amount

(a) 3/4 of 20 = 3/4 × 20/1 = 60/4 = 15.

(b) 2/5 of Rs 3,500 = 2/5 × 3,500 = 7,000/5 = Rs 1,400.

A quick method: divide by the denominator first, then multiply by the numerator. 3,500 ÷ 5 = 700, and 700 × 2 = 1,400. ✓

3.Cross-Cancelling: Simplify Before You Multiply

Before multiplying, check whether any numerator and any denominator share a common factor. Cancelling them first keeps the numbers small and often skips the simplifying step at the end.

4/9 × 3/8 = 12/72, then simplify the hard way

The fix: before multiplying, notice 4 and 8 share a factor of 4 (4 ÷ 4 = 1, 8 ÷ 4 = 2), and 3 and 9 share a factor of 3 (3 ÷ 3 = 1, 9 ÷ 3 = 3). Cancel first: 1/3 × 1/2 = 1/6. This is the same answer as 12/72 simplified, reached with much smaller numbers.

Example 4: Cancelling With Bigger Numbers

Work out 14/15 × 25/21.

Step 1: 14 (top) and 21 (bottom) share a factor of 7: 14 ÷ 7 = 2 and 21 ÷ 7 = 3.

Step 2: 25 (top) and 15 (bottom) share a factor of 5: 25 ÷ 5 = 5 and 15 ÷ 5 = 3.

Step 3: Multiply what is left: (2 × 5) / (3 × 3) = 10/9 = 1 1/9.

Answer: 10/9 or 1 1/9. Check without cancelling: 14 × 25 = 350 and 15 × 21 = 315, so 350/315. Divide both by 35: 10/9. ✓

Remember: you can only cancel a top number against a bottom number. You cannot cancel two top numbers against each other.

4.Multiplying Whole Numbers and Mixed Numbers

A whole number can always be written as a fraction over 1. Once it is in fraction form, the same “multiply straight across” rule applies. For mixed numbers, first change them to improper fractions.

Example 5

(a) 3/5 × 4 = 3/5 × 4/1 = 12/5, which as a mixed number is 2 2/5.

(b) 2 1/2 × 1 1/3. Change to improper fractions: 2 1/2 = 5/2 and 1 1/3 = 4/3. Then 5/2 × 4/3 = 20/6. Simplify: 20/6 = 10/3 = 3 1/3.

Cancel first to make it easier: 5/2 × 4/3 → the 2 and the 4 share a factor of 2, giving 5/1 × 2/3 = 10/3. ✓

2 1/2 × 1 1/3 = (2 × 1) and (1/2 × 1/3) = 2 1/6

Multiplying the whole parts and the fraction parts separately is tempting, but it misses the cross-terms.

The fix: always convert mixed numbers to improper fractions before multiplying. 2 1/2 × 1 1/3 = 5/2 × 4/3 = 20/6 = 3 1/3. The wrong method gives 2 1/6, which is much too small. A quick estimate shows why: 2.5 × 1.3 is about 3.3, not 2.2.

5.Dividing Fractions: Keep, Change, Flip

Dividing by a fraction uses three steps: keep the first fraction the same, change the division sign to multiplication, and flip the second fraction upside down (its reciprocal). Then multiply as usual.

1/2 ÷ 1/4 = 1/8: dividing straight across

Dividing numerator by numerator and denominator by denominator is not how fraction division works.

The fix: keep 1/2, change ÷ to ×, flip 1/4 to 4/1. Now multiply: 1/2 × 4/1 = 4/2 = 2. Check that it makes sense: how many quarters fit into a half? Two, so the answer 2 is correct.

The reciprocal of a fraction is that fraction turned upside down. The reciprocal of 2/3 is 3/2, and the reciprocal of 5 (which is 5/1) is 1/5. A number multiplied by its reciprocal always gives 1: 2/3 × 3/2 = 1. The number 0 has no reciprocal, because you cannot divide by zero.

6.Why Flipping Actually Works

Dividing by a fraction answers the question “how many of these fit inside that?” Dividing 1/2 by 1/4 asks how many quarters fit inside a half. The answer is a whole number (2), not a smaller fraction, because you are counting pieces, not shrinking an amount.

The quarter pieces light up one at a time as they are counted. Flipping 1/4 to 4 is just counting how many pieces fit in each whole.
Example 6: Dividing a Whole by a Fraction

6 ÷ 1/2 asks “how many halves fit into 6 wholes?” Since each whole holds 2 halves, 6 wholes hold 12 halves: 6 ÷ 1/2 = 6 × 2/1 = 12.

Did You Know?

Dividing by a fraction smaller than 1 always makes the answer bigger, the reverse of what happens when multiplying. That is because you are asking how many small pieces fit into a bigger amount, and small pieces fit in many times over.

7.Dividing in Every Combination

The same keep-change-flip method covers every case. Just remember to write whole numbers as fractions over 1 first, and to change mixed numbers to improper fractions.

Example 7: Whole ÷ Fraction

4 ÷ 2/3 = 4/1 ÷ 2/3 = 4/1 × 3/2 = 12/2 = 6.

Example 8: Fraction ÷ Whole

3/4 ÷ 3 = 3/4 ÷ 3/1 = 3/4 × 1/3 = 3/12 = 1/4. This makes sense: sharing three-quarters equally among 3 people gives each person one quarter.

And 2/5 ÷ 4 = 2/5 × 1/4 = 2/20 = 1/10.

Example 9: Mixed Number ÷ Mixed Number

2 1/4 ÷ 1 1/2. Change to improper fractions: 9/4 ÷ 3/2. Keep, change, flip: 9/4 × 2/3. Cancel 9 and 3 (both divide by 3): 3/4 × 2/1. Cancel 4 and 2 (both divide by 2): 3/2 × 1/1 = 3/2 = 1 1/2.

Check: 1 1/2 × 1 1/2 = 3/2 × 3/2 = 9/4 = 2 1/4. ✓ (Multiplying the answer by the divisor must give back the starting number.)

8.Mixing Operations: Multiply Before You Add

When a question has several operations, the usual order applies: multiplication and division come before addition and subtraction (see Brackets, Fractions & Decimals in BODMAS).

Example 10

Work out 1/2 + 1/3 × 3/4.

Step 1 (multiply first): 1/3 × 3/4 = 3/12 = 1/4.

Step 2 (then add): 1/2 + 1/4 = 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4.

10.Multiplying and Dividing Fractions in Real Life

Real-World Example

A recipe uses 2/3 cup of sugar per batch. Making half a batch needs 2/3 × 1/2 = 1/3 cup. Later, a baker has 5 cups of flour and wants to know how many 3/4-cup batches that makes: 5 ÷ 3/4 = 5 × 4/3 = 20/3, which is 6 2/3 batches, enough for 6 full batches with a little flour left over.

Example 11: A Tailor’s Cloth

A tailor uses 3/4 m of cloth for each shirt. How many shirts can be made from 12 m of cloth?

12 ÷ 3/4 = 12 × 4/3 = 48/3 = 16 shirts. Check: 16 × 3/4 = 12 m. ✓

Example 12: Land for Wheat

A farmer owns 3/4 of a hectare and plants wheat on 2/5 of it. How much land is that in square metres? (1 hectare = 10,000 m².)

2/5 × 3/4 = 6/20 = 3/10 of a hectare. 3/10 × 10,000 = 3,000 m².

Check in square metres first: 3/4 of a hectare = 7,500 m², and 2/5 of 7,500 = 3,000. ✓

Example 13: A Sale Price and a Time

(a) A shirt marked Rs 2,800 has 1/4 off. You pay 3/4 of the price: 3/4 × 2,800 = Rs 2,100.

(b) 5/6 of an hour is 5/6 × 60 = 50 minutes.

11.Common Mistakes

  • Finding a common denominator to multiply. It is not needed and it makes the work longer.
  • Adding when you should multiply. 1/2 × 1/3 is 1/6, not 2/5. Never add the tops or bottoms.
  • Flipping the wrong fraction. In keep-change-flip, only the second fraction (the divisor) is flipped.
  • Cancelling two tops or two bottoms. Cancel only a top against a bottom. In 2/3 ÷ 4/5 you must flip first before you can cancel anything.
  • Multiplying mixed numbers part by part. Convert to improper fractions first.
  • Believing multiplication always makes things bigger. Multiplying by a fraction smaller than 1 makes the answer smaller. Dividing by one makes it bigger.
  • Dividing straight across. 1/2 ÷ 1/4 is 2, not 1/8.

13.Exam-Style Questions

  1. [2 marks] Work out 3/8 × 4/9. Give your answer in its simplest form.
  2. [3 marks] Work out 5/6 ÷ 10/9.
  3. [3 marks] Work out 2 1/4 × 2/3. Give your answer as a mixed number.
  4. [4 marks] A tailor uses 3/4 m of cloth for each shirt. How many complete shirts can be made from 10 m of cloth, and how much cloth is left over?
Show answers

1. 3/8 × 4/9 = 12/72 = 1/6. (Cancelling first: 3 and 9 share 3, and 4 and 8 share 4, giving 1/2 × 1/3 = 1/6.)

2. 5/6 × 9/10 = 45/60 = 3/4.

3. 9/4 × 2/3 = 18/12 = 3/2 = 1 1/2.

4. 10 ÷ 3/4 = 10 × 4/3 = 40/3 = 13 1/3, so 13 complete shirts. Cloth used: 13 × 3/4 = 39/4 = 9 3/4 m. Cloth left: 10 − 9 3/4 = 1/4 m.

14.Key Points

  • To multiply fractions, multiply across: top × top and bottom × bottom. No common denominator is needed.
  • “Of” means multiply. Multiplying by a proper fraction gives a smaller answer.
  • Cancel a numerator against a denominator before multiplying to keep numbers small.
  • To divide, keep the first fraction, change to multiplication, and flip the second.
  • Dividing by a proper fraction gives a bigger answer, because you are counting how many pieces fit.
  • Change mixed numbers to improper fractions, and write whole numbers over 1.

15.For Teachers and Parents

Many students memorise “keep, change, flip” without understanding it. Use drawings: an area model for multiplication, and strips cut into pieces for division, as in the diagrams above. Ask “should the answer be bigger or smaller?” before every calculation, which catches most errors. Real tasks such as dividing cloth, scaling a recipe or finding part of a field give meaning to the rules. Allow time for cross-cancelling practice, because it makes harder questions manageable and builds number sense.
