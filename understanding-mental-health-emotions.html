Understanding Mental Health and Emotions

Just like the body, the mind needs care and attention. Learning to understand your emotions is one of the most valuable skills you can develop.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 14 min read | Last updated: October 2026 | Health

Everyone experiences a wide range of emotions every day: excitement, frustration, sadness, joy and worry. These feelings are a completely normal part of being human. Just like the body, the mind needs care and attention. This guide explains what mental health is, what emotions are for, how they affect the body, healthy ways to manage them, and how to find help when things feel too heavy to carry alone.

1.What Is Mental Health?

Mental health refers to a person's emotional, psychological and social wellbeing. It affects how we think, feel, handle stress, relate to others and make decisions. Everyone has mental health, just as everyone has physical health, and it changes over time. Good mental health does not mean feeling happy all the time. It means being able to cope with normal stresses, learn, build relationships and enjoy life, even when you have hard days.

The mental health spectrum
A simple idea: where you are can change, and you can move in both directions.
Doing well
Coping
Struggling
Needs urgent help

Moving towards "struggling" does not mean something is wrong with you. It happens to many people at some time, and support helps you move back.

Mental health problems are common. The World Health Organization estimates that about 1 in 7 young people aged 10 to 19 experience a mental health condition, such as anxiety or depression. Many do not get help, often because of silence or stigma, which is why talking openly matters.

2.Understanding Emotions

Emotions are the brain and body's natural response to events, thoughts or experiences. They are not "good" or "bad" in themselves. Even difficult emotions like fear or anger serve important purposes. What matters is how we understand them and what we do next.

EmotionCommon purpose
FearAlerts you to potential danger
AngerSignals that a boundary or value has been challenged
SadnessHelps process loss and encourages support from others
JoyReinforces positive experiences and connections
AnxietyPrepares the body to respond to perceived threats

An emotion is a short, strong reaction, such as a sudden fright. A mood lasts longer and is less tied to one event, such as feeling low for an afternoon. Both are normal. Feeling a lot of different emotions in one day, including mixed ones, is also normal, especially in the teenage years, when the brain and body are changing quickly.

3.The Mind-Body Connection

Emotions are not just "in your head". They cause real physical changes in the body. This is why stress can cause a stomach ache and excitement can cause a racing heart. When the brain senses a threat or a big challenge, the body releases stress hormones such as adrenaline. The heart beats faster, breathing quickens and muscles tighten, ready to act. This is often called the fight-or-flight response.

This reaction is helpful in real danger, but it can feel uncomfortable when it happens before an exam or a presentation. Knowing that these feelings are a normal body response, and not a sign that something is wrong with you, often makes them easier to handle.

4.Stress and Anxiety

Stress is the body's response to a demand or pressure, such as homework deadlines, exams or arguments. A little stress can help you focus. Anxiety is a feeling of worry or fear, often about something that might happen. Short periods of both are normal and usually pass.

It becomes a concern when the worry is very strong or constant, lasts for weeks, and gets in the way of school, sleep, friendships or enjoying things. Then it is important to seek support, because anxiety and low mood are treatable.

5.Healthy Ways to Manage Emotions

  • Name the feeling. Simply identifying "I feel anxious" or "I feel frustrated" can reduce its intensity, because naming it helps the brain process it.
  • Talk to someone you trust. Sharing feelings with a friend, family member or teacher gives support and a new point of view.
  • Practise slow breathing. Slow, deep breaths calm the body's stress response.
  • Get physical activity. Exercise helps release built-up tension. See our guide to physical fitness.
  • Write it down. Journaling can help organise confusing or overwhelming thoughts.
  • Protect your sleep. Tiredness makes emotions harder to manage. See sleep and rest.
  • Do something creative or calming, such as drawing, music, prayer or quiet time outdoors, if these help you.
Example

Before a big presentation, a student notices their heart racing and hands shaking. Instead of ignoring it, they recognise "I'm feeling nervous", take a few slow breaths, and remind themselves they have prepared well. This turns an overwhelming feeling into a manageable one.

Worked example: a one-minute calming breath

1Sit comfortably and relax your shoulders.
2Breathe in slowly through your nose for a count of 4.
3Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of 6. A longer breath out helps the body relax.
4Repeat 5 times. Each round takes 4 + 6 = 10 seconds, so 5 rounds take 5 × 10 = 50 seconds.

The animated circle below grows as you breathe in and shrinks as you breathe out. Try to follow it.

If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe normally.

6.Changing Unhelpful Thought Patterns

How we think about a situation affects how we feel. Sometimes our first thought is harsher than the facts. A useful skill is to notice the thought, ask whether it is completely true, and look for a more balanced one.

SituationFirst thoughtBalanced thought
A friend does not reply to a message"They are angry with me.""They may be busy. I can ask them later."
A low mark on a test"I am hopeless at this.""I did not do well this time. I can find out what to improve."
Nervous before speaking"Everyone will laugh.""I have prepared. Most people are focused on their own work."

This is a general skill used in many wellbeing programmes. It does not mean pretending everything is fine. It means checking whether the story you tell yourself matches the facts.

7.Looking After Your Mind Every Day

Mental wellbeing is built through small, regular habits, not only through dealing with problems when they appear. Keep a steady sleep routine, eat regular meals, move your body, and spend time with people you care about. Make time for something you enjoy, such as a hobby, sport, reading or helping others. Take breaks from screens and from constant news, and try to spend some time outdoors in daylight. These habits do not remove all stress, but they give you more energy and resilience to handle it. You can read more about the building blocks of health in What Is Nutrition? and Food Groups and Healthy Eating.

8.Coping With Big Changes and Loss

Life brings changes that can be hard, such as changing school, moving home, family problems, exam pressure or losing someone you love. It is normal to feel sad, angry, confused or numb at such times, and there is no single right way to feel. Grief takes time, and it often comes in waves. Be patient with yourself, keep some routine in your day, lean on family and friends, and ask for help if the feelings stay very heavy. Cultural and religious traditions, such as prayer, remembering the person and spending time with family, can also bring comfort for many people.

9.Friends, Family and Social Media

Good relationships protect mental health. Spending time with people who care about you, being kind, and being able to say how you feel all help. Online life can be positive, but it can also bring pressure to compare yourself with others, and cyberbullying. Remember that people usually post only the best moments. If someone is bullying you or others, online or offline, do not suffer in silence: save the evidence if you can, and tell a trusted adult.

10.How to Support a Friend

  • Choose a quiet time and ask kindly how they are.
  • Listen without judging or rushing to fix things.
  • Tell them their feelings matter and that they are not alone.
  • Encourage them to talk to a trusted adult, counsellor or doctor, and offer to go with them.
  • If a friend talks about hurting themselves or not wanting to live, take it seriously, and tell a trusted adult straight away, even if they asked you to keep it a secret. Their safety is more important than a secret.

11.When to Seek Extra Support

It is normal to feel low or anxious sometimes. But if difficult feelings last for weeks, feel overwhelming, or start affecting daily life, such as school, friendships, sleep or appetite, it is important to talk to a trusted adult, a school counsellor or a doctor. Asking for help is a sign of strength, not weakness.

If you are in danger, or thinking about harming yourself: tell a trusted adult now, call your local emergency number, or contact a crisis helpline in your country. You do not have to deal with this alone, and help works. The website Befrienders Worldwide lists helplines in many countries.

Professionals such as counsellors, psychologists and doctors are trained to listen and to help. Talking therapies and other treatments are effective for many young people, and most people who get support improve.

Real-World Example

Many schools now run wellbeing programmes that teach students to recognise early signs of stress and practise coping strategies before problems build up. This reflects a wider shift in how mental health is treated, moving from silence and stigma towards open conversation and early support.

12.Common Myths About Mental Health

MythMental health problems are a sign of weakness.
FactThey are health conditions that can affect anyone, and they are not a character flaw.
MythYoung people do not get serious mental health problems.
FactThey do. Many start in the teenage years, which is why early support matters.
MythTalking about feelings makes things worse.
FactTalking to someone supportive usually helps, and it can make feelings easier to handle.
MythIf someone looks fine, they are fine.
FactMany people hide their struggles. Checking in kindly can make a real difference.
Key points to remember
  • Mental health means emotional, psychological and social wellbeing, and everyone has it.
  • Emotions are not good or bad. They are useful signals.
  • Emotions cause real physical changes through the mind-body connection.
  • Naming feelings, slow breathing, activity, sleep and talking to someone help.
  • If feelings last for weeks or overwhelm you, tell a trusted adult or a professional.
For teachers and parents

Activity: use a "feelings check-in" at the start of a lesson, and practise the one-minute breathing exercise together. Show students which adults and services at school they can go to.

Please note: this article is general education and not medical advice or therapy. If a young person seems distressed, or talks about harming themselves, seek help from a qualified professional or a crisis service straight away.
