Writing with Accuracy: Grammar, Spelling and Punctuation

A comprehensive Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 unit on controlling grammar, spelling and punctuation so that ideas are communicated clearly, accurately and effectively.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | Unit 37 | English Language

Strong writing is not only about having good ideas. A reader must be able to understand those ideas without being distracted by avoidable grammatical, spelling or punctuation errors. Accuracy means controlling the language so that meaning remains clear, precise and appropriate. This unit develops accuracy as an active writing skill: not simply finding mistakes, but making deliberate choices that help communication.

1. Unit Purpose

This unit develops the ability to produce writing that is grammatically controlled, correctly spelt and appropriately punctuated. It also develops the ability to proofread, diagnose and repair errors without damaging the original meaning, voice or purpose of a piece of writing.

EDUSAMBAM principle

MEANING → CONTROL → CHECK → CORRECT → IMPROVE

2. What Does Accuracy Mean?

Accuracy is the degree to which a writer uses language conventions correctly and consistently. It includes grammar, spelling, punctuation and appropriate word forms. Accuracy is not the same as using complicated language. A simple sentence that is correct and clear is more effective than an impressive-looking sentence that contains errors.

Compare

Less controlled: “The students was tired because they has been studying all night.”

Accurate: “The students were tired because they had been studying all night.”

3. Accuracy Supports Meaning

Grammar and punctuation are not merely decoration. They can change the meaning of a sentence. Compare “Let’s eat, Grandma” with “Let’s eat Grandma.” The comma separates the person being addressed from the action. Similarly, changing a verb tense can alter whether an event is completed, continuing or expected.

4. Accuracy Does Not Mean Perfectly Complicated English

O Level writers sometimes try to sound advanced by producing very long sentences. This can create more opportunities for errors. Accuracy is better understood as reliable control. Choose structures that you can use correctly, then extend your range gradually.

5. The Four Accuracy Areas

AreaWhat to controlTypical question
GrammarSentence structure, agreement, tense, pronouns and word formsDoes the sentence work grammatically?

PunctuationBoundaries, pauses, relationships and emphasisDoes the punctuation make the meaning clear?

6. Sentence Completeness

A complete sentence normally contains a subject and a finite verb and expresses a complete thought. A sentence fragment may look like a sentence because it begins with a capital letter, but it does not communicate a complete grammatical unit.

Repair

Fragment: “Because the road was flooded.”

Complete: “The bus arrived late because the road was flooded.”

7. Subject–Verb Agreement

The subject and verb must agree in number. Singular subjects normally take singular verb forms in the present tense, while plural subjects take plural forms.

Common error

“The list of instructions are on the desk.”

The subject is list, not instructions: “The list of instructions is on the desk.”

8. Agreement with Compound Subjects

When two subjects are joined by and, they usually form a plural subject: “The teacher and the students were ready.” Be careful when the subjects are joined by expressions such as or or nor; agreement depends on the construction and intended subject.

9. Verb Tense and Time

Tense helps the reader understand when an action happens. Writers should choose a tense that matches the meaning and maintain it unless there is a reason to change.

Inconsistent

“Yesterday we visit the museum and learned about ancient tools.”

Controlled: “Yesterday we visited the museum and learned about ancient tools.”

10. Choosing the Right Tense

Past tense is common for completed events, present tense for current or general statements, and future forms for predictions, plans and possibilities. More advanced writing may deliberately shift tense, but the shift should be meaningful rather than accidental.

11. Maintaining Tense Consistency

When describing a sequence of past events, do not jump randomly between past and present forms. Read each paragraph and ask: What time frame am I in? If the time frame changes, make the change clear.

12. Perfect and Continuous Forms

Perfect forms can show relationships between events, while continuous forms can emphasise an action in progress. They should be used because they communicate a useful time relationship, not merely because they sound advanced.

Meaning difference

“She studied for three hours” presents a completed period of study. “She had been studying for three hours when the lights went out” establishes an ongoing activity before another past event.

13. Pronoun Reference

Pronouns such as he, she, it, they, this and those should clearly refer to something identifiable. An unclear pronoun forces the reader to guess.

Unclear reference

“Ali told Hamza that he had forgotten the tickets.”

Who forgot them? If the meaning matters, name the person: “Ali told Hamza that Hamza had forgotten the tickets.”

14. Pronoun Agreement

Pronouns should agree appropriately with their antecedents in number and person. Avoid switching unnecessarily between I, you, we and they in the same general statement.

15. Articles: a, an and the

Articles help readers understand whether a noun is being introduced generally or identified specifically. Errors with a, an and the can make otherwise clear writing sound uncontrolled.

Think about meaning

“I saw a dog outside” introduces one dog. “The dog was wearing a red collar” refers to the dog already identified.

16. Countable and Uncountable Nouns

Some nouns can be counted directly, while others normally behave as uncountable nouns. This affects determiners and verb forms. Learn common patterns rather than translating expressions word for word from another language.

Common distinction

“Some information” is standard; “informations” is not normally used in this meaning. “Two pieces of information” can express a number.

17. Prepositions

Prepositions express relationships of place, time, direction and association. Many combinations are conventional: interested in, responsible for, depend on and similar to. Accuracy requires learning these combinations through meaningful examples.

18. Adjective and Adverb Forms

Adjectives normally describe nouns, while adverbs often modify verbs, adjectives or other adverbs. Choosing the wrong form can make a sentence grammatically incorrect or change its meaning.

Compare

“She gave a careful answer.” / “She answered carefully.”

19. Modifiers Must Be Clear

A modifier should be placed close enough to the word it describes that the reader cannot misunderstand the relationship.

Awkward

“Walking through the market, the smell of bread attracted me.”

The sentence makes it sound as though the smell was walking. Better: “Walking through the market, I was attracted by the smell of fresh bread.”

20. Word Forms

English words can change form depending on their grammatical role. For example: decide, decision, decisive, decisively. Check which form the sentence requires before inserting a word.

21. Subject, Object and Complement

Understanding basic sentence roles helps writers diagnose errors. In “The committee approved the proposal,” committee is the subject and proposal is the object. In “The proposal was successful,” successful functions as a complement describing the subject.

22. Sentence Boundaries

One of the most important accuracy checks is deciding where one sentence ends and another begins. A full stop, question mark or exclamation mark can separate independent sentences. A comma alone normally cannot join two complete independent clauses.

23. Sentence Fragments: A Common Problem

Fragments often begin with words such as because, although, when, while or which and are left without a main clause.

Repair options

Fragment: “Although the weather was improving.”

Repair 1: “Although the weather was improving, the match remained cancelled.”

Repair 2: “The weather was improving.”

24. Run-on Sentences

A run-on sentence joins ideas without the grammatical punctuation or conjunction needed to separate them. Do not repair every run-on by adding random commas. Decide whether to use a full stop, semicolon, conjunction or restructuring.

25. Comma Splices

A comma splice occurs when two independent clauses are joined only by a comma. It can often be repaired by using a full stop, a semicolon, or a suitable coordinating conjunction.

Repair

Problem: “The road was flooded, we took another route.”

Better: “The road was flooded, so we took another route.”

Or: “The road was flooded. We took another route.”

26. Full Stops

A full stop marks the end of a complete statement. Strong writing uses full stops to control clarity and prevent overlong sentences. Short sentences are not automatically weak; they become effective when used deliberately.

27. Question Marks

Use a question mark after a direct question. Do not automatically use one after an indirect question.

Compare

“Where is the library?”

“She asked where the library was.”

28. Exclamation Marks

Exclamation marks indicate strong feeling or emphasis. In formal O Level writing, they should be used selectively. Excessive exclamation marks can make a serious argument look uncontrolled.

29. Commas in Lists

Commas can separate items in a list: “The project needs time, money, equipment and trained staff.” The writer should make the structure of the list easy to follow.

30. Commas After Introductory Elements

A comma can help separate an introductory phrase or clause from the main clause, especially when the opening is long or when clarity benefits.

Example

“After several hours of discussion, the committee reached a decision.”

31. Commas Around Non-Essential Information

Information that interrupts the main sentence and is not essential to identifying the noun can be enclosed by commas.

Example

“The head teacher, who had joined the school that year, welcomed the visitors.”

32. Semicolons

A semicolon can connect closely related independent clauses when the relationship is strong enough to keep them together but distinct enough to deserve separate clauses.

Example

“The first plan was inexpensive; it was also impractical.”

33. Colons

A colon can introduce an explanation, example, list or elaboration after a complete clause.

Example

“The team faced one major problem: there was not enough time.”

34. Apostrophes

Apostrophes commonly show possession or contractions. Writers must distinguish its from it's: its is normally possessive, while it's means it is or it has.

35. Possessive Apostrophes

Possession requires attention to whether the noun is singular or plural. Compare “the student's book” with “the students' books.” The apostrophe helps show the relationship.

36. Quotation Marks and Direct Speech

When presenting direct speech, punctuation and quotation marks should clearly distinguish the spoken words from the surrounding reporting clause. Keep the chosen convention consistent throughout the piece.

37. Capital Letters

Capital letters are required at the beginning of sentences and for proper nouns and other conventions. Random capitalisation can be as distracting as missing capitals.

38. Hyphens and Compound Words

Some compound expressions require or commonly use hyphens depending on their grammatical function and convention. If a compound adjective appears before a noun, a hyphen may help prevent ambiguity: “a well-designed programme.”

39. Spelling Is Part of Communication

Spelling errors can obscure meaning, reduce precision and distract the reader. The solution is not merely to memorise enormous word lists; it is to identify your personal high-frequency errors and build reliable checking habits.

40. Commonly Confused Words

Some errors occur because words look or sound similar. Examples include their/there/they're, your/you're, affect/effect, advice/advise and practice/practise in varieties of English where the distinction applies.

A useful check

Replace you're with “you are.” If the sentence still works, you're is possible; otherwise, your may be required.

41. Homophones

Homophones sound alike or nearly alike but have different meanings and spellings. Because a spellchecker may not detect a correctly spelled but wrongly chosen homophone, meaning must be checked as well as spelling.

42. British English Consistency

For Cambridge O Level English, follow the conventions expected by the course and remain consistent. Forms such as colour, centre and organise are standard British English forms. The most important principle is controlled, consistent usage.

43. Spelling Patterns

Learn families of words rather than isolated spellings: happy → happiness, decide → decision, possible → possibility. However, do not assume every word follows a single pattern. English contains many exceptions.

44. Proofreading Is Different from Reading

When reading for meaning, the brain often automatically supplies what it expects. During proofreading, deliberately slow down and inspect one feature at a time. A useful sequence is sentence boundaries → verbs → agreement → spelling → punctuation → consistency.

45. The Three-Pass Accuracy Check

EDUSAMBAM method

Pass 1 — Meaning: Is every sentence saying what I intend?

Pass 2 — Grammar: Are verbs, agreement, pronouns and structures controlled?

Pass 3 — Surface accuracy: Are spelling, punctuation and capitalisation correct?

46. Read Aloud to Detect Problems

Reading aloud can reveal missing words, repeated words, awkward sentence boundaries and places where punctuation does not match the intended rhythm. It should support—not replace—visual proofreading.

47. Check One Sentence at a Time

For difficult passages, isolate each sentence. Identify its subject and main verb. Then check whether every clause is properly connected. Finally check punctuation. This makes large pieces of writing manageable.

48. Check One Paragraph at a Time

After sentence-level correction, examine each paragraph as a whole. Ask whether tense, person, spelling conventions and punctuation patterns remain consistent. Look especially for one sentence that suddenly changes style or grammatical control.

49. Do Not Edit Only the Last Paragraph

Writers often run out of time and proofread only the ending. This can leave earlier errors untouched. If time is limited, scan the entire response systematically rather than polishing one section excessively.

50. Accuracy in Formal Writing

Reports, formal letters, formal emails and serious articles require particularly careful grammar, punctuation and spelling because the reader expects controlled communication. Informality is not an excuse for careless errors.

51. Accuracy in Speeches

A speech can sound natural and engaging while remaining grammatically controlled. Spoken-style features may be appropriate, but deliberate fragments, repetition and rhetorical questions should be distinguishable from accidental errors.

52. Accuracy in Narrative Writing

Narrative writing may contain dialogue, short sentences and stylistic fragments in appropriate places. The writer should know the difference between a deliberate stylistic choice and an error caused by weak control.

53. Accuracy in Descriptive Writing

Descriptive writing often uses varied sentence lengths and rich modifiers. Accuracy remains essential: adjectives must modify the intended nouns, verb forms must remain controlled, and punctuation must help the reader follow complex descriptions.

54. Accuracy in Directed Writing

In directed writing, accuracy must work together with the task's purpose, audience and form. A formally correct response can still be weak if it does not fulfil the task. Accuracy is therefore one part of effective communication, not a substitute for relevant content.

55. Before-and-After Editing Example

Original

“The school have introduced a new rule, it will effect many students. Their going to have less time and some students doesn't understand why.”

Edited

“The school has introduced a new rule. It will affect many students. They're going to have less time, and some students don't understand why.”

The correction addresses agreement, sentence boundaries, word choice, apostrophe use and verb agreement.

56. Editing Without Destroying Voice

Accuracy editing should not turn every sentence into the same pattern. Preserve effective personal choices in rhythm, vocabulary and structure when they are grammatically purposeful. The goal is controlled expression, not mechanical uniformity.

57. Common Accuracy Mistakes to Watch For

  • subject–verb disagreement;
  • unnecessary tense changes;
  • unclear pronoun references;
  • missing articles or incorrect articles;
  • incorrect prepositions;
  • sentence fragments;
  • run-on sentences;
  • comma splices;
  • missing apostrophes;
  • confused homophones;
  • inconsistent capitalisation;
  • misspelled high-frequency words;
  • punctuation that changes or obscures meaning.

58. Build a Personal Error Log

One of the most effective ways to improve accuracy is to record recurring mistakes. Create four columns: my error → correction → rule/pattern → new example. Review the log before the next writing task.

Example error log

Error: “He go to school every day.”
Correction: “He goes to school every day.”
Pattern: third-person singular present tense.
New example: “She studies every evening.”

59. The Accuracy Ladder

LevelWriter behaviour
1. NoticeCan identify that something looks wrong.
2. DiagnoseCan explain what the problem is.
3. CorrectCan repair the sentence.
4. ExplainCan state the relevant rule or pattern.
5. TransferCan avoid the same error in new writing.

60. A Worked Proofreading Routine

  1. Read the whole response once for meaning.
  2. Circle or note uncertain sentences.
  3. Check every finite verb against its subject.
  4. Check tense consistency.
  5. Check pronoun references.
  6. Check articles and prepositions where needed.
  7. Scan spelling of commonly confused words.
  8. Check sentence boundaries.
  9. Check commas, apostrophes and other punctuation.
  10. Read the final version once more for overall clarity.

61. A Five-Minute Examination Proofread

When only a few minutes remain, do not attempt a complete rewrite. Use a targeted scan: first sentence boundaries, then verbs and agreement, then spelling, then punctuation. Check names, numbers and key factual details last.

Five-minute sequence

1 minute: sentence boundaries • 1 minute: verbs/agreement • 1 minute: spelling • 1 minute: punctuation • 1 minute: final meaning check.

62. Accuracy and the Writer's Confidence

Accuracy should make a writer more confident, not more afraid of writing. Draft freely enough to develop ideas, then edit systematically. Separating drafting from proofreading helps prevent constant interruption of the creative process.

63. Accuracy Is Part of Style

Style is not only vocabulary and sentence variety. Reliable control of grammar and punctuation allows deliberate stylistic choices to be understood. When the basic structure is unstable, the reader may mistake an error for an intended effect.

64. The EDUSAMBAM ACCURACY Framework

Remember ACCURACY

A — Assess the meaning
Is the intended message clear?

C — Check clauses and sentences
Are sentence boundaries correct?

C — Check core grammar
Agreement, tense, pronouns and word forms.

U — Use punctuation purposefully
Does punctuation show the intended relationships?

R — Review spelling
Especially high-frequency and commonly confused words.

A — Assess consistency
Person, tense, spelling conventions and style.

C — Correct recurring errors
Use your personal error log.

Y — Yield a final clean copy
Read once more for meaning and presentation.

65. Accuracy Does Not Replace Effective Writing

A response can be grammatically accurate yet fail to answer the question. Always remember the hierarchy of good writing: fulfil the task → develop relevant ideas → organise them clearly → choose appropriate language → maintain accuracy. Accuracy strengthens communication; it does not replace it.

66. Cambridge O Level Connection: W5

Accuracy is closely connected with the writing objective that expects candidates to use correct spelling, punctuation and grammar. In examination writing, accuracy works alongside organisation, vocabulary and sentence structures, register, audience, purpose and form.

67. Unit Mastery Checklist

  • I can write complete grammatical sentences.
  • I can maintain subject–verb agreement.
  • I can control tense consistently.
  • I can use pronouns clearly.
  • I can use articles and prepositions appropriately.
  • I can recognise fragments and run-ons.
  • I can repair comma splices.
  • I can use commas, apostrophes, colons and semicolons purposefully.
  • I can spell commonly confused words correctly.
  • I can proofread systematically rather than randomly.
  • I can distinguish deliberate style from accidental error.
  • I can maintain accuracy under examination time pressure.
  • I can record and target my recurring errors.
  • I can produce a final version that is clear, controlled and accurate.

68. What Comes Next?

Once a writer can plan, develop ideas, organise them coherently, select effective vocabulary and control accuracy, the next stage is to apply these skills to specific Cambridge writing forms and purposes. The following units can therefore move increasingly into directed writing, composition and examination-style application.

69. Final Thought

Good writers do not try to avoid every difficult sentence. They learn to control difficult sentences. They do not fear punctuation; they use it to guide the reader. They do not simply memorise spelling lists; they notice patterns and correct recurring errors. Above all, they understand that accuracy is a tool for making meaning dependable.

EDUSAMBAM takeaway

Write your ideas boldly. Develop them carefully. Then edit them accurately.
