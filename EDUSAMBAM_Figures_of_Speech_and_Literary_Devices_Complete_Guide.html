Figures of Speech and Literary Devices: A Complete Guide

Learn how writers make language vivid, memorable and powerful through figures of speech and literary devices—with clear definitions, examples, effects and practical analysis.

Article purpose: Figures of speech and literary devices are techniques writers use to communicate ideas, create images, emphasise meaning, shape tone, influence readers and make language memorable. A strong reader does not merely identify a device; they explain what it suggests and how it contributes to the writer's purpose and effect.

1. What Are Figures of Speech and Literary Devices?

A figure of speech is an expression in which words are used in a striking, imaginative or non-literal way. A literary device is a deliberate technique used by a writer to create meaning, structure, emphasis or effect.

The terms overlap. Some techniques are primarily forms of figurative language, while others concern sound, repetition, contrast, structure, characterisation or narrative technique.

Simple comparison

Literal: The wind was very strong.

Figurative: The wind roared through the valley.

The second version gives the wind a more powerful and dramatic quality.

2. Why Do Writers Use These Devices?

  • To create vivid mental pictures.
  • To make an idea easier to remember.
  • To emphasise an important point.
  • To communicate emotion.
  • To establish mood and atmosphere.
  • To reveal a character's feelings or personality.
  • To make descriptions more precise or imaginative.
  • To create rhythm, musicality or emphasis.
  • To contrast people, ideas or situations.
  • To influence the reader's response.

3. Simile

A simile compares two different things explicitly, commonly using like or as.

Examples

Her smile was like sunshine.

The water was as cold as ice.

He moved like a shadow.

Effect: A simile helps the reader connect an unfamiliar or abstract quality with something more familiar and visual.

Analysis: Do not stop at “this is a simile”. Explain what the comparison suggests. For example, “like a shadow” may suggest quietness, darkness, secrecy or constant movement, depending on the context.

4. Metaphor

A metaphor describes one thing as if it were another, creating an implied comparison without using like or as.

Examples

The classroom was a zoo.

Her voice was music to him.

Fear is a prison.

Effect: Metaphors can compress a complex idea into a vivid image and can communicate attitude or emotion very efficiently.

5. Extended Metaphor

An extended metaphor develops a metaphor across several words, sentences or even a longer passage.

Example

The journey through school was a mountain climb. Each difficult lesson was another steep slope, every examination another rocky ledge, and each success a view from higher ground.

The writer keeps the same mountain image and develops it to represent educational progress.

6. Personification

Personification gives human qualities, actions or feelings to something non-human.

Examples

The wind whispered through the trees.

The old house watched over the village.

The angry sea attacked the shore.

Effect: Personification can make nature or objects seem alive. The particular human action also matters: a sea that “attacks” seems threatening, while a breeze that “whispers” seems quiet and secretive.

7. Hyperbole

Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration used for emphasis, humour, emotional force or dramatic effect. It is not normally intended to be taken literally.

Examples

I have told you a million times.

The bag weighed a ton.

She waited forever.

The key is the purpose of the exaggeration: it may show frustration, excitement, amusement or intensity.

8. Understatement

Understatement deliberately presents something as less important, serious or impressive than it really is.

Example

After walking through a severe storm for three hours, he said, “It was a little wet.”

Effect: Understatement can create humour, irony, calmness or controlled emotional expression.

9. Irony

Irony occurs when there is a meaningful contrast between what is said, what is expected and what is actually meant or happens.

Example

A fire station catches fire, and someone remarks, “Well, this is convenient.”

Irony depends heavily on context. A reader should not label any unexpected event as irony without explaining the contrast.

10. Oxymoron

An oxymoron places apparently contradictory words together to create a striking expression.

Examples

deafening silence

bittersweet memory

living dead

Effect: The contradiction can express complexity, tension or a mixture of emotions.

11. Paradox

A paradox is a statement or idea that appears contradictory but may reveal a deeper truth.

Example

“The more we learn, the more we realise how much we do not know.”

A paradox encourages the reader to think beyond the surface meaning.

12. Alliteration

Alliteration is the repetition of the same or similar initial consonant sound in nearby words.

Examples

Wild winds whipped the trees.

Silent shadows slipped across the street.

Effect: Alliteration can create rhythm, emphasis and memorable sound. The repeated sound may also contribute to the mood, but its effect must be judged from context.

13. Assonance

Assonance is repetition of vowel sounds in nearby words.

Example

The moon glowed over the road.

Assonance can create musicality, rhythm or subtle sound patterns.

14. Consonance

Consonance is repetition of consonant sounds, especially within or at the ends of nearby words.

Example

The black rock lay beside the lake.

Like all sound devices, its effect should be explained rather than automatically described as “beautiful” or “musical”.

15. Onomatopoeia

Onomatopoeia uses a word whose sound suggests or imitates the sound associated with what it describes.

Examples

buzz, hiss, bang, crash, whisper, tick-tock

Effect: It can make a scene more immediate and sensory by helping readers imagine the sound.

16. Repetition

Repetition deliberately repeats a word, phrase, sound or idea.

Example

Never give up. Never surrender. Never lose hope.

Effect: Repetition can reinforce a central idea, build emotional intensity, create rhythm or make a statement memorable.

17. Anaphora

Anaphora is repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses or sentences.

Example

We will work. We will learn. We will improve.

This creates rhythm and gives the repeated opening strong emphasis.

18. Rhetorical Question

A rhetorical question is asked to make a point, provoke thought or create emphasis rather than to obtain an ordinary answer.

Examples

Who would want to live in a world without kindness?

How can we ignore the problem?

Effect: Rhetorical questions can involve the reader and encourage them to consider the writer's viewpoint.

19. Direct Address

Direct address speaks directly to the reader or another person using words such as you or a person's name.

Example

You can make a difference if you act today.

Effect: It can create a personal relationship with the reader and make an argument feel immediate.

20. Juxtaposition

Juxtaposition places contrasting people, ideas, images or situations close together so that their differences become noticeable.

Example

The writer describes a luxurious mansion beside a row of damaged homes.

Effect: The contrast may highlight inequality, conflict, difference or change.

21. Antithesis

Antithesis presents contrasting ideas in a balanced or parallel structure.

Example

We must learn to live together, not learn to live apart.

The balanced contrast gives the opposing ideas emphasis and makes the statement memorable.

22. Symbolism

Symbolism occurs when an object, colour, place, action or image represents a larger idea.

Examples

A broken chain may symbolise freedom.

A darkened room may symbolise fear or uncertainty.

A rising sun may suggest hope or a new beginning.

Symbols are context-dependent. A dark room does not automatically mean sadness; the surrounding text determines its meaning.

23. Imagery

Imagery is language that creates sensory or mental pictures.

Example

The hot pavement shimmered beneath the harsh afternoon sun, while the smell of dust filled the dry air.

This creates visual, tactile and olfactory impressions.

24. Sensory Imagery

SenseExample
SightThe silver moon hung above the dark hills.
SoundThe distant thunder rolled across the valley.
SmellThe sharp scent of rain filled the air.
TasteThe lemon was intensely sour.
TouchThe rough stone scraped his hand.

25. Pathetic Fallacy

Pathetic fallacy is a form of personification in which human emotions or qualities are attributed especially to nature or the environment.

Examples

The angry storm raged around the house.

The cheerful morning welcomed the travellers.

It can make the setting reflect or reinforce the emotional atmosphere of a scene.

26. Euphemism

A euphemism uses a milder expression instead of one that may sound harsh, unpleasant or direct.

Examples

passed away instead of died

let go instead of dismissed from employment

The choice can make language sound more polite, indirect or sensitive.

27. Idiom

An idiom is a fixed expression whose overall meaning cannot always be understood simply by interpreting each word literally.

Examples

break the ice → begin a more relaxed interaction.

hit the nail on the head → describe something exactly or identify the key point.

once in a blue moon → very rarely.

Idioms are strongly connected to usage and context.

28. Foreshadowing

Foreshadowing gives an earlier hint of something that may happen later in a narrative.

Example

Before the character enters the abandoned house, the narrator mentions that nobody who enters after dark has ever returned.

Effect: Foreshadowing can create suspense, anticipation and a sense that events are connected.

29. Flashback

A flashback interrupts the chronological sequence to present an earlier event.

Purpose: It can reveal background information, explain a character's behaviour or deepen the reader's understanding of the present situation.

30. Contrast and Foil

A foil is a character whose qualities contrast with another character, helping the reader notice particular traits.

Example

A careful, patient character may be placed beside an impulsive, impatient character. The contrast makes both personalities clearer.

31. Rule of Three

The rule of three presents three related words, phrases or ideas, often creating rhythm and a sense of completeness.

Examples

She was brave, determined and patient.

Read, think, respond.

Three-part structures can be especially effective in speeches, arguments and persuasive writing.

32. Parallelism

Parallelism uses similar grammatical structures for related ideas.

Example

She wanted to learn, to practise and to improve.

Parallel structures make relationships clearer and can create rhythm and emphasis.

33. Choosing the Correct Device

Some devices are easy to confuse. Use the context and the precise definition.

Do not confuseKey distinction
Simile vs metaphorSimile makes an explicit comparison; metaphor presents one thing as another.
Personification vs pathetic fallacyPersonification gives human qualities to non-human things; pathetic fallacy specifically connects nature/environment with human emotion.
Repetition vs anaphoraAnaphora is a particular kind of repetition at the beginning of successive units.
Oxymoron vs paradoxOxymoron is usually a compact contradictory expression; paradox is a broader apparently contradictory idea or statement.
Hyperbole vs literal statementHyperbole intentionally exaggerates for effect.

34. The Most Important Skill: Explain the Effect

In an English examination, identifying a device is only the first step. Strong analysis explains what the language suggests and why the writer may have chosen it.

Weak analysis

“The writer uses a metaphor. It makes the writing interesting.”

Stronger analysis

“The writer describes fear as a ‘prison’, suggesting that the character feels trapped and unable to escape. The metaphor makes the emotional pressure seem restrictive and powerful.”

35. A Four-Step Method for Literary Language Analysis

  1. Identify: What word, phrase or device has the writer used?
  2. Quote: Select the smallest relevant quotation.
  3. Interpret: What does it suggest or imply?
  4. Effect: How does it influence the reader, mood, characterisation, atmosphere or argument?
Model

Device: personification.

Evidence: “the angry sea”.

Meaning: The sea is presented as hostile and threatening.

Effect: This creates a dangerous atmosphere and makes the natural environment seem like an active opponent.

36. Avoiding Formulaic “Effects”

Do not automatically write that a device “makes the reader interested” or “makes the writing beautiful”. Those statements are too general.

Instead ask:

  • What exactly is the reader encouraged to picture?
  • What emotion is created?
  • What attitude does the writer communicate?
  • Does the device make something seem larger, smaller, faster, slower, more threatening or more attractive?
  • Does it reveal a character's feelings?
  • Does it strengthen an argument?
  • Does it change the tone?

37. Worked Mini-Analysis

Passage

The village slept beneath a blanket of darkness. In the distance, the wind whispered through the empty streets while the old houses stared silently into the night.

“blanket of darkness” → metaphor. Darkness is compared to a blanket, suggesting that the village is completely covered and enclosed.

“wind whispered” → personification. The human action “whispered” creates a quiet, secretive atmosphere.

“houses stared” → personification. The houses seem watchful, making the setting feel eerie and almost alive.

38. How Devices Work Together

Writers often combine several techniques rather than using one device in isolation.

Example

The furious sea roared against the cliffs, while the black clouds gathered overhead.

Here, personification, strong imagery and carefully chosen descriptive vocabulary work together to create a threatening atmosphere.

39. Figures of Speech in Non-Fiction

These techniques are not limited to poetry and stories. Newspapers, speeches, advertisements, articles and opinion writing may use metaphor, repetition, rhetorical questions, contrast and other devices.

For example, an article might describe a difficult problem as “a ticking time bomb”. The metaphor suggests urgency and possible danger.

40. Final Master Checklist

When you meet figurative or literary language

1. What is the exact expression?

2. Which device is being used?

3. What is its literal meaning, if useful?

4. What image or comparison does it create?

5. What does it suggest?

6. What feeling or attitude does it communicate?

7. What effect does it have in this particular context?

8. Why might the writer have chosen this expression rather than a simpler one?
