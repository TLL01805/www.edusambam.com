Currencies of the World: Money, Codes and Exchange Rates

What money is, how it developed from shells to phones, how exchange rates, inflation and central banks work, and the story of the rupee.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|18 min read|General Knowledge |Last updated: October 2026

A banknote is just a piece of printed paper, and a number on a banking app is just a record. Yet people will work a whole month for them and hand over goods worth thousands. The reason is shared trust: everyone agrees that this paper or number can be exchanged for other things. This article explains what money is, how it developed from shells and coins to cards and phones, how exchange rates and inflation work, what central banks do, and how the rupee fits into the story. A table of capitals and currencies for 135 countries is in Countries, Capitals & Currencies; here the focus is on the money itself.

In this article

What money is · a timeline of money · currency codes and symbols · exchange rates · inflation and purchasing power · central banks · the rupee · shared currencies · digital money · four worked examples · myths corrected · key terms · practice questions and a quiz.

1.What Is Money?

Money is anything that people in a society generally accept as payment for goods, services and debts. Economists describe three jobs it does.

Figure 1. The three main functions of money.
  • Medium of exchange: you can swap it for things you want, without needing to find someone who wants exactly what you have to offer. Without money, trade needs barter, which requires a "double coincidence of wants".
  • Unit of account: prices are quoted in it, so goods can be compared. A pen costing Rs 50 and a notebook costing Rs 150 tells you the notebook is worth three pens.
  • Store of value: you can save it and use it later, although inflation can reduce what it buys.

Money is also legal tender when a government says it must be accepted for the payment of debts within the country. A currency is a country's (or group of countries') official form of money.

2.A Short History of Money

Figure 2. Milestones in the history of money. The marker moves from the oldest to the newest.
  • Barter and commodity money. Early societies swapped goods directly, and later used items such as cowrie shells, salt and metals as money. Cowrie shells were used across parts of Asia and Africa for many centuries.
  • Coins. The oldest known coins come from Lydia, in what is now western Turkey, around 600 BC. Stamped metal solved a problem: a coin's weight and purity were guaranteed by the ruler's mark.
  • Paper money. Paper notes first appeared in China, with the Song dynasty issuing paper money in the 11th century. Europe's first banknotes were issued by a Swedish bank, Stockholms Banco, in 1661.
  • The gold standard. In the 19th century many countries fixed their currencies to gold and promised to exchange notes for a set amount of it.
  • Bretton Woods (1944). At a conference in the United States, Allied countries agreed on a system of fixed exchange rates tied to the US dollar, which was itself tied to gold, and created the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank.
  • 1971. The United States ended the dollar's convertibility into gold. Since then, the major currencies have been fiat money: valuable because governments declare them legal tender and people trust them, not because they are backed by a precious metal.
  • Cards and phones. The first general-purpose charge card, Diners Club, appeared in 1950. Mobile money took off in 2007, when the M-Pesa service started in Kenya, letting people send money by text message.

3.Codes, Symbols and Sub-units

Every currency has a name, a three-letter international code (ISO 4217), often a symbol and a smaller unit. In most codes, the first two letters are the country's code and the third is the first letter of the currency name: PK + R = PKR (Pakistani rupee), US + D = USD (US dollar). The euro, EUR, is shared by several countries and is an exception.

4.Exchange Rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. It tells you, for example, how many rupees buy one US dollar. There are two broad systems:

  • Floating: the rate moves with supply and demand in the market, though a central bank may sometimes step in.
  • Fixed (pegged): a government keeps its currency at a set rate against another currency, such as the dollar or the euro. Pegs can be costly to defend.

Rates move because of differences in interest rates and inflation between countries, a country's trade (selling more abroad than it buys raises demand for its currency), investors' confidence and expectations, and political events.

Banks and money changers quote two prices: a buying price and a slightly higher selling price. The gap is the spread, and it is one way they earn money.

Figure 3. Share of currency trades involving each currency, April 2025 (Bank for International Settlements survey). Shares add up to 200% because every trade involves two currencies.

The foreign exchange market is the largest financial market in the world, with about $9.6 trillion traded per day in April 2025. The US dollar is on one side of about 89% of trades, followed by the euro, the Japanese yen and the pound sterling.

Worked example 1: The spread (sample rates)

Question: A money changer buys euros at 300 rupees and sells them at 306 rupees (sample rates, not real ones). A traveller buys 200 euros and, unused, sells them straight back. What does the traveller lose?

Step 1. Cost to buy: 200 × 306 = 61,200 rupees.

Step 2. Return from selling: 200 × 300 = 60,000 rupees.

Step 3. Loss: 61,200 − 60,000 = 1,200 rupees, which is about 2% of the amount paid.

Answer: 1,200 rupees. This is the spread, and it is why changing money back and forth is expensive.

5.Inflation and Purchasing Power

Inflation is a general rise in prices over time. It means each unit of money buys less. Statisticians measure it by following the cost of a typical "basket" of goods and services and reporting the change as a percentage, usually per year. This is the consumer price index (CPI).

Worked example 2: Measuring inflation

Question: A basket of goods cost 250 rupees last year and 280 rupees this year. What is the inflation rate?

Step 1. Find the increase: 280 − 250 = 30.

Step 2. Divide by the old price: 30 ÷ 250 = 0.12.

Step 3. Convert to a percentage: 12%.

Answer: 12% inflation.

Figure 4. What 100 rupees can buy if prices rise 8% every year.
Worked example 3: Purchasing power over time

Question: If prices rise by 8% each year, what is the purchasing power of 100 rupees after 10 years?

Step 1. Prices become 1.08 times as high each year, so after 10 years they are 1.0810 ≈ 2.16 times as high.

Step 2. Purchasing power = 100 ÷ 2.16 ≈ 46.

Answer: The same 100 rupees buys only about 46 rupees' worth of goods. That is why savings can lose value in times of high inflation.

Pakistan has experienced periods of high inflation. In May 2023, the year-on-year rate reached about 38%, one of the highest rates on record for the country, before easing. Very high inflation hurts people on fixed incomes the most.

6.Central Banks and Interest Rates

A central bank manages a country's currency and banking system. Its main jobs are to issue banknotes, to keep prices stable, to act as banker to the government and the banks, and to oversee the financial system. Examples include the State Bank of Pakistan, the Reserve Bank of India, the US Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank. The State Bank of Pakistan began work on 1 July 1948.

Figure 5. How raising the policy interest rate can bring inflation down.

The central bank's main tool is its policy interest rate, the rate that influences what banks charge each other and, in turn, what they charge their customers. Raising it makes borrowing more expensive, which tends to slow spending and cool inflation. Lowering it does the opposite. The effects take time, and sometimes policies must balance inflation against jobs and growth.

7.The Rupee: A South Asian Story

  • Origins. The word "rupee" comes from the Sanskrit rupya, meaning silver. The ruler Sher Shah Suri introduced a standard silver coin called the rupiya in the 1540s, and later Mughal and British rulers continued the system.
  • 1947 and after. At independence, Pakistan first used British Indian notes overprinted for Pakistan, and in 1948 the State Bank of Pakistan began operations and issued Pakistani currency.
  • Decimalisation. Until 1961, one Pakistani rupee equalled 16 annas. On 1 January 1961 Pakistan switched to a decimal system: 1 rupee = 100 paisa.
  • Exchange rate policy. Pakistan's rupee was linked to the British pound at first, then to the US dollar, and since the early 1980s the rate has been managed or market-driven.
  • Other rupees. The rupee is the currency name in India, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Mauritius and the Seychelles. The Maldives uses the rufiyaa. India introduced a special rupee sign (₹) in 2010.
  • Digital payments in Pakistan. Mobile wallets such as Easypaisa and JazzCash let people send and receive money by phone, and the State Bank's Raast instant payment system launched in 2021.

8.Shared Currencies

  • The euro is used by 21 of the 27 European Union countries as of 2026, after Bulgaria joined on 1 January 2026. Euro notes and coins first entered circulation on 1 January 2002.
  • Fourteen African countries use one of two CFA francs, one in West Africa and one in Central Africa, both pegged to the euro.
  • Eight Caribbean territories share the East Caribbean dollar.
  • Some countries, such as Ecuador and El Salvador, use the US dollar as their official currency.

9.Digital Money

Most money today exists as records in bank computers, not as paper. Debit and credit cards, online banking and mobile wallets move these records. Mobile money services in countries such as Kenya and Pakistan let people without bank accounts send and store money using a phone.

Cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, created in 2009 by an unknown person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto, are not issued by any government. Bitcoin's supply is capped at 21 million coins and its price has been very volatile. Rules about cryptocurrencies differ widely between countries and change quickly, so always check current official guidance, and treat them as high risk. Many central banks are also studying central bank digital currencies (CBDCs), which would be digital versions of national money.

Real banknotes include security features to resist forgery, such as watermarks, security threads, colour-changing ink, microprinting and raised printing.

Worked example 4: Simple and compound interest

Question: If you save 10,000 rupees at 10% a year for 3 years, how much do you have with simple interest, and how much with compound interest?

Step 1. Simple interest: 10% of 10,000 = 1,000 a year, so 3 × 1,000 = 3,000, giving 13,000.

Step 2. Compound interest: 10,000 × 1.10 × 1.10 × 1.10 = 10,000 × 1.331 = 13,310.

Answer: 13,000 with simple interest and 13,310 with compound interest, the difference being interest earned on interest. (This is a classroom example, not financial advice.)

10.Myths Corrected

"The currency with the highest value per unit belongs to the strongest economy"

The Kuwaiti dinar has one of the highest values per unit of any currency, so many people assume this must be true.

The fix: The value of one unit of a currency depends partly on how the currency is divided, not on the size of the economy. Kuwait's economy is much smaller than the United States' or China's. A country can also redenominate (drop zeros from) its currency, which changes the numbers on the notes, but not the economy.

"A government can solve its problems by simply printing more money"

It sounds easy, which is why it is so tempting.

The fix: Printing more money without producing more goods and services mostly raises prices, which is inflation. History has many examples of very high inflation caused by excessive money creation. Real wealth comes from goods, services and productivity, not from the quantity of paper.

"The US dollar is backed by gold"

This was true once.

The fix: Until 1971 the US dollar could be converted into gold at a fixed price for foreign governments. That link ended in 1971, and today's major currencies are fiat money, valued because of government backing, the law and public trust.

Real-World Example

When Pakistan's rupee falls in value against the dollar, the price of imported goods such as fuel and machinery, which are bought with dollars, rises, which can add to inflation. That connects the three ideas in this article: exchange rates, inflation and the central bank's response through interest rates.

Did You Know?

Because every currency trade involves two currencies, the currency shares in Figure 3 add up to 200%, not 100%. That is why the US dollar can be on one side of 89% of trades without being involved in everything.

12.Common Mistakes

  • Thinking money and wealth are the same thing. Money is a tool for exchange; wealth is the goods, land, skills and assets people have.
  • Believing that a currency with big numbers on its notes is "strong".
  • Forgetting that currencies with the same name (rupee, dollar) have different values.
  • Mixing up inflation (prices rising) with a falling exchange rate, although the two can be linked.
  • Using an old exchange rate. Rates change every day, so always check a current source.

13.Summary

  • Money works as a medium of exchange, a unit of account and a store of value.
  • It developed from barter and shells to coins (about 600 BC), paper notes, cards and mobile money.
  • Since 1971 the major currencies have been fiat money, valued by trust and law.
  • Exchange rates move with supply and demand; the US dollar is on one side of about 89% of currency trades.
  • Inflation reduces purchasing power, and central banks use interest rates to try to control it.
  • The rupee has a long history, from Sher Shah Suri's silver coin to Pakistan's decimal rupee of 1961 and today's digital payments.

For Teachers and Parents

Suggested activities: run a classroom "barter day" and then introduce a classroom currency to show why money helps; ask students to track the price of three items over a few months and calculate inflation; compare notes and coins from different countries for their security features. Discussion prompts: Why do we trust money? What happens if people stop trusting a currency? Exchange rates and economic figures change constantly, so the numbers in this article are examples or dated snapshots and not live data. Nothing here is financial advice.
