Job Interview English: Questions, Answers and Phrases

Interview questions are more predictable than most people think. Learn how to prepare your stories, answer in a clear structure and speak with confidence, from the greeting to the thank-you email.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 18 min read | English

A job interview is a short, structured conversation in which an employer decides whether you can do the job and whether you will work well with the team. It can feel frightening, especially in a second language, but it is also a skill that anyone can practise. The questions are more predictable than most people think, and good answers follow a few clear patterns. This guide takes you from preparation, through the interview itself, to the follow-up message afterwards, with the exact English you need at each step.

Key Takeaways

Prepare your evidence and your stories before the day. Answer in a clear structure: present, past, future for "Tell me about yourself", and situation, task, action, result for experience questions. Speak slowly, give examples, and ask the employer two or three good questions. Send a short thank-you message afterwards.

1.What an Interview Is Really Testing

Employers use interviews to check three things. First, can you do the job? They want evidence of your skills and experience. Second, will you fit? They want to know whether you communicate well, take responsibility and work with others. Third, do you want this job? They want to see real interest, not a general wish for any job. Every question, however it is worded, is a way of asking one of these three things. When you are unsure how to answer, ask yourself which of the three the interviewer is testing.

Remember also that the interview works in both directions. You are deciding whether the job, the team and the organisation are right for you, and your questions show that you take this decision seriously.

2.Types of Interview

TypeWhat it isUseful tip
One-to-oneYou and one interviewerBuild a calm, friendly conversation
PanelTwo or more interviewers ask questions in turnLook at the person who asks, then include the others as you answer
PhoneA conversation without videoYour voice does all the work: smile, speak clearly and keep notes in front of you
VideoAn interview on a screenTest the camera, sound and internet beforehand
GroupSeveral candidates are observed togetherContribute, listen to others and do not interrupt
Competency-basedQuestions ask for examples of what you did in the pastPrepare short stories with the STAR method

Many interviews combine these types. A competency-based question usually begins with "Tell me about a time when..." and tests a particular skill, such as teamwork or problem solving, through a real example from your life.

3.Preparation: What to Do Before the Day

Good preparation is the biggest single source of confidence. Work through these steps in the days before your interview.

  1. Study the job advert. Underline the skills and duties it mentions. These are the topics you will be asked about.
  2. Research the organisation. Read its website. Learn what it does, who its customers are and what recent news it has. Prepare one fact you can mention naturally.
  3. Prepare your evidence. Use the evidence bank from your CV (see our guide to CV and Resume Writing) and choose five or six stories that show different skills.
  4. Practise aloud. Say your answers out loud, not only in your head. Record yourself on a phone, listen once, and improve one thing at a time.
  5. Plan the practical details. Decide your route and arrive early, or prepare your video setup. Choose neat, comfortable clothes, appropriate for the workplace.
  6. Prepare your questions. Write two or three questions to ask the interviewer at the end.

Bring a copy of your CV, a notebook and a pen. If the interview is on the phone or by video, you may keep short notes in front of you, but do not read your answers aloud word for word.

4.The Shape of an Interview

Most interviews follow a similar order, so you can predict what comes next and feel less surprised.

StageWhat happensWhat you do
1. GreetingIntroductions and small talkSmile, greet politely, speak clearly
2. Opening questionUsually "Tell me about yourself"Give a short, structured summary
3. Main questionsSkills, experience, motivationAnswer with examples and results
4. Your questions"Do you have any questions for us?"Ask your prepared questions
5. ClosingNext steps and thanksThank them and confirm what happens next

5.Greetings, Small Talk and First Impressions

The first minute matters because people form an impression quickly. Stand when the interviewer arrives, give a firm but comfortable greeting, and use the interviewer's name if you know it. Customs about handshakes and eye contact differ from one place to another, so follow the interviewer's lead.

SituationUseful English
Greeting"Good morning. It is a pleasure to meet you."
Thanks for the invitation"Thank you for inviting me to interview today."
Small talk about the journey"Yes, it was easy to find. I allowed plenty of time."
Accepting a drink"Yes, water would be lovely, thank you."

6.The Most Important Question: "Tell Me About Yourself"

This question usually opens the interview, and a good answer sets the tone for everything else. Do not tell your life story. Use the present, past, future pattern and keep your answer to about one minute.

  • Present: who you are now and what you do.
  • Past: one or two relevant experiences or achievements that brought you here.
  • Future: why you are interested in this job and what you want to contribute.
Model answer

"I am a business graduate, and I currently work as a sales assistant in a stationery shop, where I serve customers and keep the daily sales records. Before that, I volunteered as a library administrator, where I created a spreadsheet record of 1,200 books and trained three volunteers to use it. I enjoy organised, accurate work, and I am excited about this office assistant role because it would let me use my record-keeping and communication skills in a larger team."

Notice the structure: it begins with the present, moves to a relevant past achievement with a number, and ends with a clear reason for wanting this job.

7.Answering Experience Questions with the STAR Method

When an interviewer says "Tell me about a time when...", the STAR method gives you a clear structure for your answer.

LetterMeaningWhat to say
SSituationWhere and when this happened (one or two sentences)
TTaskWhat you needed to achieve
AActionWhat you did, step by step (the longest part)
RResultWhat happened, with a number if possible, and what you learned
Example

Question: "Tell me about a time when you solved a problem."

Answer: "(Situation) In my job at the shop, customers were waiting a long time at the counter, and some were leaving without buying anything. (Task) My manager asked me to find a way to make the service faster. (Action) I watched where the delays happened, and I saw that the most popular items were stored at the back. I moved those items near the counter and prepared change in advance. (Result) Waiting time fell from about ten minutes to six, and my manager asked me to train the new staff in the same method."

Use "I", not "we", for the action part. Interviewers want to know what you personally did, even when the work was done in a team. You can say "my team and I", and then explain your own part clearly.

8.Common Questions and How to Answer Them

QuestionWhat the employer wants to knowHow to answer
Why do you want this job?Real interest and motivationLink two points from the advert to your own skills and goals
What are your strengths?Skills that fit the roleChoose two relevant strengths and prove each with a short example
What is your weakness?Honesty and self-awarenessName a real, small weakness and explain what you are doing to improve it
Why are you leaving your job?Attitude and reliabilityBe positive: talk about what you want next, not what was wrong before
Where do you see yourself in five years?Ambition and commitmentDescribe learning and growing in a way that fits this organisation
How do you work under pressure?Calmness and organisationGive a real example with a method you used, such as a to-do list
Tell me about a conflict at work.Teamwork and maturityShow how you listened, stayed respectful and reached a solution
What salary do you expect?Fit with the budgetResearch typical pay for the role and give a range, or say you are open to discussion
Example: the weakness question

"In the past, I sometimes spent too long on small details. I now set a time limit for each task and check the most important points first. This has helped me to finish my work on time while keeping it accurate."

This answer names a real but manageable weakness, shows action, and ends with a positive result. Avoid false weaknesses such as "I work too hard", because interviewers hear them often and do not believe them.

9.Language Tools: Useful Phrases

You do not need difficult English to interview well. You need a small set of reliable phrases that you have practised.

PurposePhrases to use
Starting an answer"That is a good question." / "I would like to answer that with an example."
Buying thinking time"Let me think about that for a moment." / "May I take a moment to organise my thoughts?"
Asking for repetition"Could you please repeat the question?" / "I am sorry, I did not quite catch that."
Checking you understood"Do you mean how I handle a busy day?" / "Just to check, are you asking about teamwork?"
Showing results"As a result, ..." / "This led to ..." / "The outcome was ..."
Showing teamwork"I worked with my colleagues to ..." / "My role in the team was to ..."
Disagreeing politely"I see your point, although my experience has been slightly different."
Closing"Thank you for your time. I look forward to hearing from you."

10.Grammar That Helps in Interviews

Past simple is the tense for finished actions with a clear time: "I organised a school event last year, and 80 people attended." Present perfect is useful for experience that continues up to now: "I have worked with customers for two years." Present simple describes your current role and general facts: "I manage the daily sales records."

For politeness, use modal verbs such as would and could: "I would like to learn more about the team" sounds more polite and natural than "I want to know about the team". When you describe goals, "I would like to..." and "I am keen to..." are both clear and professional.

Less effectiveMore effective
I am working here since 2022.I have worked here since 2022.
I have organised the event last month.I organised the event last month.
I want to know my salary.Could you tell me about the salary range for this role?
My old boss was bad.I am now looking for more responsibility and a chance to develop new skills.

11.Speaking Clearly: Pace, Stress and Intonation

Interviewers can accept an accent, but they need to understand you. Nervousness makes most people speak too quickly, so aim for a pace that is a little slower than normal conversation, with short pauses between ideas. A pause feels much longer to you than it does to the listener.

  • Word stress: say the important syllable more strongly, for example ex-PE-ri-ence and de-VEL-op-ment. Check the stress of key job words in a good dictionary or an audio dictionary.
  • Sentence stress: emphasise the key words in your sentence, such as the numbers and the results: "I reduced waiting time from ten to six minutes."
  • Intonation: let your voice go slightly down at the end of a statement, and use a friendly, varied tone. A flat voice sounds uninterested.
  • Clear endings: pronounce the final sounds of words, especially -ed endings in past tense verbs such as managed and organised.

Practise with the recording method: record a one-minute answer, listen once for clarity and pace, and repeat. Three short practice sessions are more effective than one long one.

12.Body Language and Video Interviews

Your body speaks before you do. Sit upright, keep your hands relaxed, and look at the interviewer with natural, friendly eye contact. Nod to show you are listening, and smile when it is natural. Avoid crossing your arms, tapping your fingers or looking at your phone.

For a video interview, prepare the setting as carefully as you prepare your answers:

  • Test your camera, microphone and internet connection at least a day before, and again a few minutes before the interview.
  • Sit in a quiet place with light in front of you, not behind you.
  • Place the camera at eye level and look at the camera lens when you speak, not only at the faces on the screen.
  • Close other programs and silence your phone and notifications.
  • Keep a glass of water and a notebook nearby, and have the link and the interviewer's phone number ready in case of technical problems.

13.Handling Difficult Moments

You do not understand a question. It is completely acceptable to ask politely: "Could you please rephrase that?" This shows that you want to answer correctly, which is better than giving an answer to a different question.

Your mind goes blank. Take a slow breath and use a thinking phrase such as "Let me think about that for a moment". A few seconds of silence is normal. If you still cannot find an example, say honestly what you would do: "I have not faced that exact situation, but this is how I would approach it."

You make a mistake. Correct it calmly and continue. Interviewers notice how you recover more than they notice the mistake.

You are very nervous. Nervousness is normal and often looks smaller to others than it feels to you. Breathe slowly, relax your shoulders and remember that the interviewer wants to find a good candidate. Good preparation is the best way to reduce anxiety.

14.Questions to Ask the Employer

At the end you will usually hear "Do you have any questions for us?" Answering "No" can look like a lack of interest. Choose two or three questions from your preparation.

  • "What would a typical day in this role look like?"
  • "How will success in this job be measured in the first six months?"
  • "What training or development opportunities are available?"
  • "Can you tell me about the team I would be working with?"
  • "What are the next steps in the process?"

Avoid asking about holidays or salary in the first interview unless the employer raises the subject. Focus on the work and the team.

15.After the Interview: The Thank-You Message

Within a day, send a short, polite email to thank the interviewer. It reminds them of you, shows good manners and gives you one more chance to confirm your interest.

Sample thank-you email

Subject: Thank you for the interview
Dear Ms Taylor,
Thank you for taking the time to interview me for the office assistant position yesterday. I enjoyed learning more about your team, and I am very interested in the role. Our conversation about improving the filing system made me even more motivated to contribute. Please let me know if I can provide any further information.
Kind regards,
Jordan Lee

If you were told when to expect a decision, wait until that date has passed before you write again. If you are not selected, you may politely ask for feedback. Every interview, successful or not, teaches you something for the next one.

16.Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Arriving late or without having studied the organisation.
  • Giving very short answers, such as "yes" or "no", without examples.
  • Speaking negatively about a previous employer, teacher or colleague.
  • Memorising long answers word for word, so that you sound like a recording.
  • Saying "we" for everything, so the interviewer cannot see your own contribution.
  • Exaggerating or inventing experience, which can be discovered later.
  • Using the phone, or looking away from the interviewer, during the conversation.
  • Having no questions at the end.

17.Your Interview Checklist

  • I know the job advert and I can link three of my skills to it.
  • I researched the organisation and prepared one fact to mention.
  • I prepared five or six STAR stories and practised them aloud.
  • I have a one-minute answer for "Tell me about yourself".
  • I prepared two or three questions to ask the employer.
  • I know my route or I have tested my video setup, and I will arrive early.
  • I have a copy of my CV, a notebook and a pen.
  • I will send a thank-you email within a day.

Key Terms

TermMeaning
Competency-based questionA question asking for a real example of how you used a skill
Panel interviewAn interview with two or more interviewers
STAR methodSituation, task, action, result: a structure for experience answers
IntonationThe rise and fall of your voice when you speak
Word stressThe syllable in a word that is said most strongly
Modal verbA helping verb such as would or could, often used to be polite
Follow-upA message you send after the interview to thank the employer

A Closing Thought

An interview is not a test in which you must be perfect. It is a conversation in which you show, calmly and honestly, how your skills match the needs of the job. Prepare your stories, use clear structures such as present-past-future and STAR, speak slowly and listen carefully, and finish with good questions and a polite message. With practice, the same phrases and patterns will become natural, and your English will feel stronger with each interview.
