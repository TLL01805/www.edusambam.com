R5: Selecting and Using Information for Specific Purposes

A focused Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 unit on selecting relevant information, shaping it to a stated purpose, and communicating it accurately and efficiently.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team|Unit 13|English Language

Strong readers do not simply collect everything a text says. They select the information that is relevant to a particular purpose, decide how it should be organised, and communicate it accurately. R5 develops this practical reading skill: knowing what to keep, what to leave out, and how to use selected information effectively.

1. Unit Purpose

R5: Selecting and using information for specific purposes is the skill of reading with a clear task in mind. The reader must identify the purpose of the question or activity, locate information that serves that purpose, and then use only the information that is relevant.

EDUSAMBAM principle

Read the purpose → locate the information → select what matters → organise it → communicate it accurately.

The key word is specific. A good response is not a summary of everything the writer says. It is a purposeful selection of the information needed for the task.

2. Learning Outcomes

  • identify the exact purpose of an information-selection task;
  • recognise the difference between relevant and irrelevant information;
  • locate information efficiently in a passage;
  • select points that directly answer the stated purpose;
  • distinguish essential information from interesting but unnecessary detail;
  • group related information logically;
  • combine information from different parts of a text;
  • select information from more than one source when required;
  • paraphrase selected information accurately;
  • avoid copying large amounts of unnecessary wording;
  • check that every selected point serves the task;
  • produce concise, purposeful responses.

3. What Does “Specific Purpose” Mean?

A purpose tells you why you are selecting information. The same passage may contain information about causes, effects, advantages, disadvantages, problems, solutions, experiences and opinions. A question may ask for only one of these.

Task purposeWhat you selectWhat you leave out
Explain the causesInformation showing why something happenedUnrelated effects and background details
Identify the benefitsPositive effects or advantagesProblems and disadvantages
Describe the problemsDifficulties, risks or negative consequencesUnrelated successes
Explain how to improve somethingSuggested actions and solutionsDetails that do not help explain improvement

The purpose acts like a filter. Before selecting a point, ask: “Does this help me answer the task?”

4. Relevant Information and Irrelevant Information

Relevant information contributes directly to the purpose of the task. Irrelevant information may be true or interesting, but it does not help answer the question.

Mini example

A passage says that a community garden provides fresh vegetables, gives residents a place to exercise, teaches children about plants, opened in 2019 and is beside the town library.

Question: What are the benefits of the community garden?

Relevant: fresh vegetables, exercise, learning about plants.

Not needed: the opening year and its location, unless the question specifically asks for them.

Notice that irrelevant information is not necessarily false. It is simply not useful for the particular purpose.

5. Read the Question Before You Search the Text

Efficient selection begins with the task, not with random reading. Identify the important words in the question.

Question wordingWhat it tells you
Give reasons for...Look for causes, explanations or justifications.
Identify the advantages...Select positive features or benefits.
Explain the problems...Select difficulties, risks or negative effects.
Describe the changes...Select information showing what became different.
Suggest ways to...Select proposed actions, methods or solutions.
Exam habit

Underline or mentally isolate the purpose word before searching the passage. Your purpose should control your selection.

6. Finding Information Efficiently

You do not always need to read every sentence with equal attention. First identify likely keywords and concepts from the task. Then scan for related words, phrases and ideas.

  • Use the wording of the question as a starting point.
  • Look for synonyms, not just exact repeated words.
  • Check the surrounding sentence before selecting a point.
  • Read across the passage because relevant information may appear in different places.
  • Do not stop after finding the first useful point if the task requires several.
Search example

If the task asks for ways to reduce food waste, the text might use phrases such as “plan meals carefully”, “store food correctly”, “use leftovers” or “buy only what is needed”. The exact words in the question may never appear.

7. Select Points, Not Whole Paragraphs

A common mistake is to copy a complete paragraph because it contains one useful sentence. Selection means extracting the specific point that serves the task.

Better selection

Text: “The centre opened in May. It offers free evening classes, provides computers for job searches and has a quiet room for study. Its blue entrance has become familiar to local residents.”

Task: Select the services provided by the centre.

Useful points: free evening classes; computers for job searches; a quiet room for study.

The opening month and the appearance of the entrance do not answer this particular task.

8. Combine Information from Different Parts of a Text

Relevant information may not appear together. One point may be near the beginning of the passage while another appears several paragraphs later. R5 therefore requires the reader to connect information across the text.

Worked method

Task: Select the reasons why the expedition was difficult.

Paragraph 2: The team faced unusually heavy rain.

Paragraph 5: A damaged bridge forced the team to take a longer route.

Selected response: The expedition was difficult because of unusually heavy rain and the need to take a longer route after a bridge was damaged.

The two points are separated in the source, but they belong together because they serve the same purpose.

9. Using More Than One Source

Some tasks require information from two texts. In such cases, do not treat the sources as two separate summaries. Read the task first and select information from each source that contributes to the same purpose.

Source ASource BCombined purpose
Public transport reduces the number of private cars.Cycling can reduce short car journeys.Ways of reducing car use.
Libraries provide access to books.Community centres provide shared study spaces.Places that support learning.

When combining sources, preserve the meaning of each point. Do not accidentally attribute Source A's information to Source B or blend two different claims into one unsupported statement.

10. Paraphrasing Selected Information

Paraphrasing means expressing a source idea in your own wording while keeping its meaning accurate. It is useful when a task requires a concise response or when several source points need to be combined smoothly.

Paraphrase carefully

Source idea: “The scheme enables residents to share unused equipment rather than buying separate items.”

Accurate paraphrase: Residents can share equipment they need instead of each purchasing their own.

Important: Do not change “can share” into “must share”, or “unused equipment” into “all equipment”. A paraphrase must preserve the strength and limits of the original meaning.

11. Organising the Selected Information

Good selection is not only about finding points. The points should be presented in a form that makes the response clear and useful.

  • Group points that belong to the same idea.
  • Use a logical order where the task suggests one.
  • Avoid repeating the same point in different words.
  • Combine closely related information when doing so keeps the meaning clear.
  • Use concise wording rather than unnecessary background explanation.
Organisation model

Task: Select the main reasons for choosing the new timetable.

Selected points: shorter waiting times; more convenient connections; better access for evening workers.

Organised response: The new timetable was chosen because it reduces waiting times, provides more convenient connections and improves access for people working in the evening.

12. The Selection Test

Before including a point, apply three quick tests:

TestQuestion
PurposeDoes this point directly serve the task?
AccuracyHave I kept the source meaning correct?
UsefulnessDoes including it improve the answer rather than merely make it longer?

If a point fails the first test, leave it out. If it fails the second, revise it. If it fails the third, consider whether it is necessary.

13. Worked Cambridge-Style Example

Source text

“The town introduced a repair programme for household appliances. Residents can bring broken items to monthly workshops where volunteers help them diagnose faults and make simple repairs. The programme has also encouraged some residents to learn basic maintenance skills. The first workshop attracted more visitors than organisers expected, although some participants initially found the booking system confusing. The programme is now considering additional sessions during school holidays.”

Task: Select information from the passage that explains how the repair programme benefits residents.

Step 1 — Identify the purpose: We need benefits to residents.

Step 2 — Locate possible points: help with diagnosing faults and repairs; learning maintenance skills.

Step 3 — Reject distractions: the first workshop's attendance and the confusing booking system do not explain benefits to residents.

Step 4 — Form the response: Residents receive help to diagnose and repair broken appliances, and the programme helps them develop basic maintenance skills.

14. Common R5 Mistakes

  • Copying everything: more words do not automatically make a better selection.
  • Ignoring the purpose: a true point can still be irrelevant to the task.
  • Using only the first part of the text: important points may appear later.
  • Repeating information: two sentences may express the same underlying point.
  • Changing the meaning: an inaccurate paraphrase is not a successful selection.
  • Adding personal opinions: unless the task asks for your judgement, stay with the information required.
  • Mixing sources carelessly: keep track of where each selected point comes from.
  • Writing a general summary: R5 requires purposeful selection, not a summary of the whole passage.

15. Exam Strategy: PURPOSE → SEARCH → SELECT → CHECK

Four-step R5 method

1. PURPOSE: What exactly does the task want?

2. SEARCH: Where in the text are ideas connected to that purpose?

3. SELECT: Which points directly answer it?

4. CHECK: Is every point relevant, accurate and necessary?

Use this sequence before writing. It prevents a common problem: producing a generally accurate answer that contains information the question did not ask for.

16. Practice Task

Read and select

“A school introduced a lunchtime reading club. Students can choose books from a small library, discuss what they have read and recommend titles to classmates. Teachers noticed that some students who rarely borrowed books began reading more often. The club meets on Tuesdays, and its first meeting was held in the science block while the usual room was being prepared.”

Task: Select the information that shows how the reading club supports students' reading.

Suggested selection: Students can choose books, discuss what they read and recommend books to classmates; some students who previously rarely borrowed books began reading more often.

Why? These points directly explain the reading support or its observed effect. The meeting day and temporary location are not needed for this purpose.

17. Final R5 Checklist

  • Have I identified the exact purpose?
  • Have I selected information rather than copied a whole passage?
  • Does every point directly answer the task?
  • Have I searched the whole relevant part of the text?
  • Have I combined related points clearly?
  • Have I kept the original meaning accurate?
  • Have I avoided repetition and irrelevant detail?
  • If there are two sources, have I used them accurately?
  • Is my response concise and purposeful?
Key takeaway

R5 is purposeful reading. The strongest response is not the one that contains the most information. It is the one that selects the right information and uses it accurately for the task.
