Main Idea & Supporting Details: The Complete Guide

Every paragraph is built around one central point, backed up by evidence. Learn to find both instantly — and how to turn that skill into a strong title.

Every well-written paragraph is doing one job: making a single point, and then proving it. Once you can reliably find that point, comprehension questions stop feeling like guesswork.

This guide opens our Reading Comprehension series by covering the single most tested skill in reading exams worldwide: identifying the main idea and the details that support it. It also covers a closely related, frequently tested skill — extracting or creating an accurate title for a passage — along with the traps that catch even strong readers on multiple-choice tests.

1.What Is Reading Comprehension?

Reading comprehension is the ability to process text and understand its meaning — not just decoding individual words, but grasping what a passage is actually saying, why it matters, and how its parts connect. Main idea identification is the foundation every other comprehension skill builds on.

2.What Is a Main Idea?

The main idea is the central point a paragraph or passage is making — the one thing the writer most wants the reader to take away. Every other sentence in a well-constructed paragraph exists to explain, prove, or illustrate it.

Example

"Regular exercise offers benefits far beyond physical fitness. It improves mood by releasing endorphins, sharpens focus and memory, and has been shown to reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression." The main idea: exercise benefits mental as well as physical health. Everything else is supporting detail.

3.Topic vs. Main Idea vs. Thesis vs. Title

These four terms are constantly confused with each other, yet each does a distinct job — and telling them apart is essential for both comprehension and the title-extraction skill covered later in this guide.

TermWhat It IsLength
TopicThe general subject — what the text is aboutA word or short phrase
Main ideaThe specific point made about the topicA full sentence
ThesisThe main idea of an entire essay (not just one paragraph)One or two sentences
TitleA short label capturing the topic and angleA few words
Same Passage, Four Labels

Topic: exercise. Main idea: exercise benefits mental as well as physical health. Thesis (if this were one paragraph of a longer essay): regular physical activity is one of the most effective, low-cost tools available for improving overall wellbeing. Title: "More Than Muscle: The Mental Benefits of Exercise."

4.Explicit vs. Implicit Main Ideas

A main idea can be stated directly, or it can be left for the reader to work out.

Explicit (Directly Stated)

"Reading fiction improves empathy by exposing readers to perspectives different from their own." The main idea is spelled out in a single sentence.

Implicit (Implied)

"She checked her phone for the fifth time in ten minutes. The flight was already forty minutes delayed, and the gate agent had stopped making eye contact with the waiting passengers." No sentence states it directly, but the implied main idea is clear: the passenger is growing anxious about the delay.

5.Topic Sentences and Where They Hide

When a main idea is explicit, it usually appears in a single sentence called the topic sentence — and it can appear in different places depending on the writer's style.

  • Beginning: the most common placement — the point is stated first, then explained. (Deductive structure.)
  • End: details build up to a concluding statement of the main idea. (Inductive structure.)
  • Middle: less common — the paragraph opens with a hook or example, states the main idea, then continues with further support.
  • Implied (nowhere): no single sentence states it; the reader must infer it from the details as a whole, as shown above.

6.Supporting Details: Major and Minor

Supporting details are the facts, examples, statistics, and explanations that develop and prove the main idea. Not all supporting details carry equal weight.

Major Supporting Details

Directly explain or prove the main idea. In the exercise example: "it improves mood," "it sharpens focus and memory," "it reduces anxiety and depression" — each is a major detail, a distinct reason supporting the main claim.

Minor Supporting Details

Add further explanation to a major detail, but don't stand on their own as a main reason. E.g., "endorphins are chemicals released during physical activity" — this explains how exercise improves mood, but it supports a major detail rather than the main idea directly.

7.How to Identify the Main Idea: A Step-by-Step Strategy

  1. Identify the topic first. Ask: "What is this paragraph generally about?" in one or two words.
  2. Ask what the writer is saying about that topic. Not just "exercise," but specifically what point is being made about exercise.
  3. Check the first and last sentences first. This is where topic sentences most often live.
  4. Test your answer against every other sentence. A correct main idea should be broad enough that every supporting detail relates back to it — and narrow enough that it isn't just the general topic restated.
  5. If nothing is stated directly, summarise the pattern across all the details to infer the implicit main idea.

8.How to Extract a Title From a Paragraph

Creating an accurate title is a distinct but closely related skill, frequently tested directly in comprehension exercises and exams. A good title is essentially a highly compressed version of the main idea — a signpost, not a summary.

The Title-Extraction Process

Step 1 — Find the topic. What is this text generally about, in a word or two?
Step 2 — Find the main idea. What specific point does the text make about that topic?
Step 3 — Identify the angle or tone. Is the passage informative, persuasive, cautionary, celebratory?
Step 4 — Compress it. Reduce the main idea to its essential few words — a title is not a full sentence, and does not need a verb.
Step 5 — Check it fits every paragraph, not just the opening line, if the passage has more than one paragraph.

Worked Example

Passage: "Bees are responsible for pollinating roughly a third of the food crops humans eat. In recent decades, bee populations have declined sharply due to pesticide use, habitat loss, and disease. Scientists warn that continued decline could seriously disrupt global food supplies." Topic: bees. Main idea: declining bee populations threaten global food supply. Possible titles: "The Hidden Cost of Disappearing Bees," "Why Bee Decline Threatens Our Food Supply," or simply "Bees in Crisis."

Common Title Mistakes

Too broad: "Bees" — this is just the topic, not a title reflecting the specific angle. Too narrow: "Pesticides Harm Bee Populations" — this captures only one supporting detail, not the full main idea about food supply. Just right: "Why Bee Decline Threatens Our Food Supply" — specific, accurate, and reflects the whole passage.

9.Common Mistakes on Multiple-Choice Comprehension Questions

  • Choosing a supporting detail instead of the main idea: the most common trap. A detail is often true and directly from the text, which makes it tempting — but it only covers part of the passage, not the whole point.
  • Choosing an answer that is too broad: technically related to the topic, but not specific to what the passage actually argues.
  • Choosing an answer that is too narrow: accurately describes one paragraph or detail, but not the passage as a whole.
  • Choosing an answer that is factually wrong but "sounds right": test writers often include a plausible-sounding option that contradicts or distorts the actual text.
Real-World Example

Consider this paragraph: "Remote work has changed how companies think about office space. Many businesses have downsized their headquarters, some have eliminated physical offices entirely, and others have redesigned them as collaboration hubs rather than daily workstations. What used to be a fixed cost is increasingly treated as a flexible, optional expense." The topic is remote work and office space. The main idea (implicit, since no single sentence states it directly): remote work has fundamentally changed how companies view and use office space. A weak answer choice might say "some companies eliminated offices" — true, but only a supporting detail. A strong answer covers the full shift described across all three examples. A fitting title might be: "The Office, Reimagined" — short, specific, and true to the whole passage, not just one sentence of it.

Why This Matters

Main idea identification is the single most tested reading skill on school exams, standardised tests, and everyday reading alike — from a news article to a workplace report. It is also the direct foundation for two other core skills: summarising a text accurately, and creating a title that genuinely represents what a passage says.
