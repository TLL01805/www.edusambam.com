Main Idea & Supporting Details: The Complete Guide

Every paragraph is built around one central point, backed up by evidence. Learn to find both instantly — and how to turn that skill into a strong title.

EDUSAMBAM Editorial Team | 20 min read | Last updated: November 2026 | Reading Comprehension

Every well-written paragraph does one job: it makes a single point and then proves it. Once you can find that point quickly, comprehension questions stop feeling like guesswork.

This guide explains how to find the main idea of a paragraph or passage, how to tell it apart from the topic, the thesis, the title and the summary, where topic sentences hide, how to sort supporting details into major and minor, and how to write an accurate title and summary. It also covers the traps that catch strong readers in multiple-choice questions. It includes two worked examples, a checklist, common myths and ten practice questions. Spelling follows British English.

It is the foundation for Making Inferences, Text Structure & Organization and Author's Purpose, Tone & Point of View. If you write, see How to Write a Perfect Paragraph and Thesis Statements.

1.What Is Reading Comprehension?

Reading comprehension is the ability to understand what you read: not just to say the words correctly, but to grasp what the passage says, how its parts fit together and why it matters. It works on three levels:

  • Literal: what the text says directly (who, what, when).
  • Inferential: what the text suggests (see Making Inferences).
  • Evaluative: what you think of it (is it fair, convincing, useful?).

Finding the main idea sits between the first two. You need the literal facts, and you need to see what they add up to.

2.What Is a Main Idea?

The main idea is the central point of a paragraph or passage: the one thing the writer most wants you to take away. Every other sentence exists to explain it, prove it or illustrate it. Think of an umbrella. The main idea is the canopy, and the supporting details are the ribs that hold it up.

Main idea and supporting details
An example about exercise.

Diagram 1. The main idea is wide enough to cover every detail beneath it.

Example

"Regular exercise offers benefits far beyond physical fitness. It improves mood by releasing endorphins, sharpens focus and memory, and can reduce symptoms of anxiety."

Main idea: exercise helps the mind as well as the body. Everything else is a supporting detail.

3.Topic, Main Idea, Thesis, Title and Summary

These five are often confused. Each does a different job.

TermWhat it isLength
TopicThe general subjectA word or short phrase
Main ideaThe point made about the topicA full sentence
ThesisThe main idea of a whole essayOne or two sentences
TitleA short label for the topic and angleA few words
SummaryThe main idea plus key details, in your own wordsMuch shorter than the original
Same passage, five labels

Topic: exercise.
Main idea: exercise helps the mind as well as the body.
Title: "More Than Muscle".
Summary: "Exercise improves mood, focus and memory, and eases anxiety, so it benefits the mind as well as the body."
Thesis (if it opened an essay): "Regular exercise is one of the most effective and affordable ways to improve overall wellbeing."

See Thesis Statements for the difference between a main idea you find and a thesis you write.

4.Stated and Implied Main Ideas

A main idea can be explicit (stated in a sentence) or implicit (left for you to work out).

Explicit

"Reading fiction can improve empathy by letting readers experience perspectives different from their own." The main idea is spelled out in one sentence.

Implicit

"She checked her phone for the fifth time in ten minutes. The flight was already forty minutes late, and the gate agent had stopped meeting the passengers' eyes."

No sentence says it, but the main idea is clear: the passenger is growing anxious about the delay.

For an implicit main idea, ask: What do all the details add up to? This uses the same skill as inference.

5.Topic Sentences and Where They Hide

When a main idea is stated, it usually sits in a topic sentence. Its position varies with the writer's style.

6.Supporting Details: Major and Minor

Supporting details are the facts, examples, reasons, statistics, quotations and short stories that develop the main idea. They are not all equal.

  • Major details prove or explain the main idea directly. Each is a separate reason. In the exercise example: "it improves mood", "it sharpens focus and memory", "it eases anxiety".
  • Minor details add more information to a major detail. "Endorphins are chemicals released during activity" explains how exercise improves mood, so it supports a major detail, not the main idea directly.
  • Irrelevant details do not support the point. They may be interesting, but a good paragraph leaves them out. If you spot one in your own writing, cut it.

Types of detail: a statistic, an example, a reason, a comparison, a definition, a quotation, an anecdote. Signal words such as for example, for instance, in addition, also, because often introduce them. See Text Structure & Organization for more signals.

7.A Five-Step Strategy

  1. Find the topic. Ask: "What is this generally about?" Answer in one or two words.
  2. Ask what the writer says about it. Not just "exercise", but "exercise helps the mind".
  3. Check the first and last sentences for a topic sentence.
  4. Test your answer. It must cover every detail (not too narrow) without being just the topic (not too broad).
  5. If nothing is stated, sum up what all the details have in common, in one sentence.

8.Main Ideas in Longer Passages

A passage of several paragraphs has a main idea for each paragraph and one for the whole. To find the overall idea:

  • Write the main idea of each paragraph in a few words.
  • Read your list. What do they add up to? That is the main idea of the passage.
  • Check the introduction and the conclusion, which often state it. Headings and subheadings also give clues.
  • Ignore a striking but minor example, even if it is memorable.

9.How to Choose a Title

A good title is the main idea squeezed into a few words: a signpost, not a summary. A title need not be a full sentence.

  1. Find the topic.
  2. Find the main idea.
  3. Notice the tone: informative, warning, celebratory?
  4. Compress it to a few strong words.
  5. Check that it fits the whole passage, not only the first sentence.
Worked example

Passage: "Bees and other pollinators help produce food. According to a United Nations-backed assessment, more than three quarters of the leading types of food crop benefit at least in part from animal pollination, and pollinator-dependent crops make up about 35% of global crop volume. Yet in Europe, populations of 37% of bee species are in decline. Scientists warn that losing pollinators could narrow the variety of food we eat."

Topic: bees. Main idea: declining pollinators threaten our food supply. Possible titles: "Why We Need Bees", "Bees and the Food on Our Plates", "The Cost of Losing Pollinators".

Title mistakes: too broad ("Bees"), too narrow (a detail only), misleading (promises something the passage does not deliver) and too long (a title that is a whole sentence).

10.Main Ideas in Different Kinds of Writing

The same skill adapts to different kinds of text.

  • News reports usually put the main point in the headline and first paragraph, and give the details in order of importance. Later paragraphs add background.
  • Textbooks use headings and bold terms as signposts. A heading often names the topic; the first sentence under it often states the main idea.
  • Opinion pieces state a claim and defend it. The main idea is the writer's position, so ask "what does the writer want me to believe?"
  • Stories have a main idea too, usually called the theme: the message about life that the events suggest. It is rarely stated, so infer it from what happens to the characters.
  • Poems compress ideas into images. Ask what feeling or idea all the images share.

11.Spotting Irrelevant Details

A well-built paragraph has unity: every sentence supports the main idea. A sentence that does not is irrelevant, however interesting. To test a sentence, ask: "Does this help prove or explain the main idea?" If the answer is no, it does not belong. For example, in a paragraph on the benefits of cycling to school, a sentence about the colour of the bicycle shed adds nothing. Cutting such sentences, in your own writing and when you summarise, makes the point clearer.

Try it yourself. Which sentence does not belong? "Walking to school keeps pupils fit. It also saves money on transport. The school canteen sells sandwiches. Fresh air helps pupils concentrate."

Sample answer: "The school canteen sells sandwiches." It does not support the idea that walking to school has benefits.

12.Writing a Summary

A summary keeps the main idea and the key details, in your own words, much shorter than the original. Follow these rules:

  • Start with the main idea.
  • Add only the major details; leave out examples and minor details.
  • Use your own words and sentences, and keep the order of ideas.
  • Do not add your opinion or new information.
  • Check that someone who had not read the passage would understand it.

Summarising shares skills with paraphrasing and citing. See Evidence, Citations & Avoiding Plagiarism.

13.Traps in Multiple-Choice Questions

TrapHow to avoid it
A detail that is true but not the main ideaAsk: does it cover the whole passage, or only one part?
Too broad (just the topic)The answer must say something specific about the topic.
Too narrow (one paragraph)Check it against every paragraph.
Distorted wordingWatch for words like always, never, only that change the meaning.
True in real life, not in the passageChoose what the passage supports.
First-sentence trapThe opening is not always the main idea; read to the end.

Process of elimination: cross out options that cover only a part, those that go beyond the text, and those that distort it. The remaining option should fit every paragraph.

14.Worked Examples

Example A: A stated main idea

"Bees do far more than make honey. Pollinators such as bees help produce about 35% of global crop volume, and more than three quarters of the leading food crops benefit at least in part from animal pollination. Without them, the variety of fruit, nuts and vegetables in our shops would shrink."

1Topic: bees (pollinators).
2What is said about it? They matter far more than for honey.
3Topic sentence: the first one, "Bees do far more than make honey." It is stated, but still check it.
4Test: the other sentences give the figures and the consequence. They all show how much we depend on bees, which fits the idea (a little more precise: pollinators are essential to our food).
5Major details: the 35% figure and the three-quarters figure. Consequence: a smaller variety of food.

Try it yourself. Give a title: "Cycling to school keeps pupils fit, cuts traffic outside the gates and saves families money on bus fares."

Sample answer: "Why Cycling to School Pays Off". The sentence names three benefits, so a good title is neither "Cycling" (too broad) nor "Bus Fares" (too narrow).

Example B: An implied main idea, with title and summary

Passage (invented): "Many firms have downsized their headquarters since more staff began working from home. Some have given up their offices entirely. Others have turned theirs into meeting spaces used only a few days each week. What used to be a fixed cost is now treated as a flexible choice."

1Topic: remote work and office space.
2Is the main idea stated? No single sentence says it all, so it is implied.
3Sum up the details: downsizing, giving up offices, turning them into meeting spaces. All show firms rethinking offices.
4Main idea: remote work has changed how companies use and value office space.
5Title: "The Office, Reimagined". Summary: "Since more employees work from home, many firms have shrunk or abandoned their offices or turned them into occasional meeting places, treating space as a choice, not a fixed cost."

A weak answer would be "some firms gave up offices entirely": true, but only one detail.

Try it yourself. Write the main idea of: "The shop opened at dawn for the bakers. Mid-morning brought the students, and by noon the lunchtime crowd filled every seat. At night, only a few tired cleaners remained."

Sample answer: the shop's customers change through the day, from bakers at dawn to cleaners at night. The details are in time order and show the pattern of one day.

16.Common Myths

MythThe main idea is always the first sentence.
FactIt is often first, but it can be last, in the middle or not stated at all.
MythThe main idea and the topic are the same thing.
FactThe topic is the subject. The main idea is the point made about it.
MythThe most interesting detail is the main idea.
FactA striking detail is still a detail. The main idea must cover them all.
MythA summary can include my opinion.
FactA summary reports the writer's ideas. Your opinion belongs in a response or review.

17.Key Terms

Main idea: the central point of a passage.
Topic: the general subject.
Topic sentence: a sentence that states the main idea of a paragraph.
Supporting details: facts, reasons and examples that back up the main idea.
Major detail: a detail that directly supports the main idea.
Minor detail: a detail that supports a major detail.
Explicit: stated directly.
Implicit: implied; the reader works it out.
Thesis: the main idea of a whole essay.
Summary: the main idea and key details in your own, shorter words.

18.Why This Matters

Finding the main idea is the base of most reading skills. It lets you study from textbooks, take useful notes, write summaries and choose titles. It also helps you decide what is important in a long report or news story. Continue with Making Inferences to learn to read between the lines, and Text Structure & Organization to see how writers arrange their ideas.

Key points to remember
  • The main idea is the point the writer makes about the topic, in a full sentence.
  • Details support it; major details prove it, minor details explain the major ones.
  • It can be stated at the beginning, middle or end, or implied.
  • Test it: it must cover all the details, but not just repeat the topic.
  • A title is the main idea compressed into a few words.
  • A summary is the main idea plus key details in your own words.
  • In multiple choice, avoid answers that are true but cover only one detail.
For teachers and parents

Activity: give pupils a short paragraph with the sentences cut apart and ask them to sort them into main idea, major details and minor details. Then ask them to write a title, a one-sentence main idea and a two-sentence summary, and to explain why a "too broad" and a "too narrow" alternative do not work.

Please note: some syllabuses use "central idea" or "gist" for what is called the main idea here; follow the terms your course uses. The passages in this guide are original or invented for practice. The bee figures in the examples follow FAO and IPBES summaries and are rounded; the exact figures depend on how crops are counted.
